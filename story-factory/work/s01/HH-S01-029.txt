Chapter One — Too Hot

Summer was supposed to be ending.

That was what everybody in Hickory Hollow said, at the beginning of September. The blackberries were finished. The apples were coming in. The swallows had begun to gather on the Mill roof, lining up along the ridge in rows, chattering about the long journey south. By rights, the mornings should have been turning misty, and the evenings cool, and folk should have been thinking about getting the woodpile in.

Instead, it got hot.

It started on the Monday, and it did not stop. Every morning, the sun came up over Sunrise Ridge already blazing, and climbed into a sky so pale and hazy that it was hardly blue at all, and stayed there, all day, pressing down on the Hollow like a great warm hand. There was no wind. Not a breath. The leaves on the hickory trees hung straight down, perfectly still. The grass in Fern Meadow went pale and crackly. And Silver Creek, which had been running lower all summer, dropped and dropped, until there were stones showing in the middle that nobody had seen since Buck was a boy.

"Hot," said Barney, on Monday afternoon, lying flat on his back in the shade of the Mill.

"Very hot," agreed Benny, lying beside him.

"Hottest," said Barney.

"You can't just say hottest," said Benny. "Hottest what?"

Barney thought about it. It was too hot to think about it for very long.

"Hottest everything," he said, and closed his eyes.

All over the Hollow, folk were doing the same thing. Lying down in the shade, wherever they could find it, and not moving very much.

Brody was lying under a big beech by the creek, with his tongue out. Rusty was lying in the long grass on the north side of the Great Barn, where the sun never reached. Reba had discovered that the cellar under the Mill, where the miller's wife kept the cheeses, was the coolest place in the whole Hollow, and had offered to help the miller's wife tidy it, very thoroughly, for as long as the heat lasted.

Maggie Mae was not lying down, because Maggie Mae had roses, and the roses were drooping. She went round Rose Arch Cottage with her watering can at dawn and again at dusk, when the sun was low, because in the heat of the day the sun drinks the water up before it ever reaches the roots. Charlie went with her, carrying the little can. And every evening the roses lifted their heads a little, and every afternoon they drooped again, and Maggie Mae said that was simply how it was going to be until the weather changed, and that the roses were being very brave about it.

And Beau was lying in the shadow of a fence post.

It was not a very big shadow. It was about the size of Beau, if Beau curled up very tight. And the trouble with the shadow of a fence post is that the sun moves, and the shadow moves with it. So every quarter of an hour or so, Beau would find that his tail was in the sun, and then his back, and then most of him, and he would have to get up, with a great sigh, and shuffle round the post, and lie down again in the new bit of shadow.

By the end of Monday afternoon, he had been all the way round the post, and was very nearly back where he had started.

"This," said Beau, to nobody in particular, "is the worst summer of my life."

Nobody answered. It was too hot.


Chapter Two — Keeping Cool

On Tuesday, everybody tried to think of clever ways to keep cool.

Charlie made fans. She made them out of folded paper, painted with daisies, one for everybody, and handed them out on Grandpappy's Bridge in the morning. Everybody fanned. It helped, a little. Then it made their arms tired, which made them hotter, which made them fan harder, which made their arms more tired.

"I think," said Benny, after a while, fanning slowly, "that the fanning is making more heat than it's taking away."

"Then stop fanning," said Barney.

Benny stopped fanning. After a moment he started again, because it was worse when he stopped.

Brody had a better idea. He found an enormous sycamore leaf, the biggest leaf in the whole Hollow, nearly as big as Charlie, and stood in the middle of the bridge and fanned everybody at once, in great slow sweeps, so that a warm, slightly leafy breeze went over all of them together. It was very kind of him, and it worked beautifully, until Brody got so hot from fanning that somebody else had to fan Brody, and then somebody else had to fan them, and in the end everybody was fanning somebody, and nobody was any cooler at all.

Maverick sat on the bridge rail with his beak open.

"Are you all right?" said Charlie.

"Perfectly," said Maverick, with his beak still open. "It's what eagles do, when it's hot. We keep our beaks open. It lets the heat out." He shut it, to show them, and then opened it again quickly. "It is not," he added, "a very magnificent look. But it works."

Benny tried to work out exactly how hot it was, so that they would know. He did it by putting his paw on the stones of the bridge and counting how long he could leave it there before it was too hot, which was four, and then on the wooden rail, which was nine, and then on Barney, who said that was not scientific and also not polite.

Rusty had the best idea of all. Rusty knew a spring.

"Up in the woods," he said. "Behind the Sugar Grove. It comes right up out of the ground, out of the rocks, and it's cold. Freezing cold. Even in summer. Trust me." He stood up. "I know a shortcut."

So they all went, with Rusty's father coming along, because it was in the woods. And the shortcut was a good deal longer than the ordinary way, and a good deal hotter, and went up a steep bank that everybody had to scramble up on their paws and knees. But at the top, under the shade of the great old maples, there was the spring, just as Rusty had said, bubbling up clear and bright out of a crack in the grey rock over a lip of rock into a stone basin, and spilling away downhill in a trickle through the ferns.

They put their paws in it.

It was so cold it made them gasp. It was so cold that Barney yelped and jumped straight out again, and then jumped straight back in. They sat round the spring in a ring, with their paws in the cold water, and their faces in the shade, and for the first time in two days, everybody was cool.

"Rusty," said Beau, with his eyes closed. "This is the best shortcut you've ever found."

"I know," said Rusty, very contentedly.

They stayed until the shadows were long. And they would have gone back the next day, and the day after, and every day until the heat broke, and been perfectly happy.

If it had not been for Sadie Belle.


Chapter Three — Who Wasn't There

Sadie Belle had not come to the spring.

Nobody had noticed at first, because it was hot, and when it is hot it is hard to notice very much except how hot it is. But on Wednesday morning, when everybody gathered on Grandpappy's Bridge to go back up to the spring, Sadie Belle was there, and she did not look as if she wanted to go.

"Where were you yesterday?" said Charlie.

"Granny Opal's," said Sadie Belle.

Everybody looked at her.

"I went past her hollow on Monday," said Sadie Belle. "At the end of the creek path. And she was sitting outside it, on her little chair, in her shawl, fanning herself with a bit of bark. Because inside her hollow it's close and airless, with no breeze at all, and it was too stuffy to sit in. And the elderberries only shade it in the morning. In the afternoon the sun comes right round onto the front of it. So she was just sitting there. In the sun. Fanning."

Sadie Belle looked at her hooves. "She said she was perfectly all right. She said she'd seen hotter. But she looked very wilted. So I stayed and fanned her, and fetched her some water from the edge of the creek. And yesterday I went back."

There was a silence on the bridge.

"Oh," said Charlie, in a small voice.

And then, the way it sometimes happens when one person notices something, everybody started noticing.

"The miller's wife hasn't been out of the Mill all week," said Reba slowly. "I've been down in the cellar with the cheeses. She's been sitting in the kitchen with the door shut against the heat. She can't walk down to the creek, because of her back. And the pump's all the way across the yard, in the sun, and she can't carry the water when it's heavy."

"The rabbits," said Brody. "The littlest one's family. There's nine of them in that burrow, and it's not a very big burrow, and the littlest one's mother looked so tired yesterday when I went past. All nine of them in there together, in this heat, and the little ones all cross and hot and not sleeping."

"And Grandpappy Buck," said Charlie. "He's been sitting out on his porch every afternoon. And his porch faces west. It gets the sun all afternoon. And when I said he should go inside where it's cooler, he said he's sat on that porch every afternoon since before anybody's grandmother was born, and he wasn't about to let a little bit of weather move him now."

Everybody looked at everybody else.

They thought about the spring, up in the woods, cold and clear and bubbling, where they had sat all yesterday afternoon with their paws in the water.

"We were up at the spring all afternoon," said Rusty slowly. "With our paws in the water."

For a while, the only sound on the bridge was the creek, trickling thinly over its stones.

Then Maggie Mae stood up, and straightened her bow.

"Well," she said. "We're not up at the spring now."


Chapter Four — The Plan

They held the meeting in the cool, dim shade under Grandpappy's Bridge, with their paws in the shallow water at the edge of the creek, where it was ankle-deep and slow and very nearly cool. Rusty's father sat on the bank above, keeping an eye on things.

"We can't make it less hot," said Benny. "That's weather. Nobody can fix weather."

"But we can't just do nothing," said Charlie.

"Granny Opal isn't weather," said Sadie Belle quietly.

So they made a list. Benny wrote it down.

There was Granny Opal, sitting out in the sun with no shade. There was the miller's wife, shut up in the hot Mill kitchen with no way to fetch water. There were the rabbits, nine of them, crammed into a stuffy burrow, with the little ones too hot to sleep. And there was Great-Grandpappy Buck, on his west-facing porch, being stubborn.

"Four," said Benny. "Four places."

"And there's ten of us," said Charlie. "So everybody takes one. And some get two or three of us."

It was Sadie Belle who said she would go back to Granny Opal, of course. Brody said he would go with her, because Granny Opal needed shade, and shade meant building something, and building something meant somebody big to carry the poles.

Benny said he would help with the shade too, because he knew exactly how to make one, and Barney said he would come and help Benny, which Benny said was a kind thought, though he looked a little worried.

Reba said she would look after the miller's wife, because she already knew where everything was in the Mill kitchen, especially the larder. Maggie Mae said she would come too, because the miller's wife needed something light to eat, and Maggie Mae knew how to make cucumber sandwiches so cool they were practically cold.

Charlie and Beau said they would take the rabbits.

"What will you do?" said Benny, pencil ready.

Charlie thought.

"I don't know yet," she said. "But there's nine of them, and some of them are very little, and I'm good with little ones. And Beau's good at being silly. That has to help."

"I am very good at being silly," agreed Beau.

That left Buck.

Everybody looked at Rusty.

Rusty looked at the water.

"Why me?" he said.

"Because you know the coolest place in the whole Hollow," said Charlie. "And you're going to have to get Grandpappy Buck to go there."

Rusty swallowed.

"That," he said, "is going to need the shortcut of my life."

And Maverick, sitting on the bridge rail above them with his beak open, said that he would fly over the whole Hollow, every morning and every afternoon, and keep an eye out for anybody else who needed help, and report back.

"It's what I'm for," he said. "Seeing things from above."

Benny wrote it all down, and underlined it, and looked at the list, and nodded.


Chapter Five — Shade

That same morning, Granny Opal was sitting outside her hollow at the end of the creek path, on her little low chair, in her shawl, fanning herself with a piece of bark, exactly as Sadie Belle had said.

"Good morning, Granny Opal," said Sadie Belle. "We've come to make you some shade."

Granny Opal looked at the four of them, Sadie Belle, and Brody with an armful of long straight poles, and Benny with his notebook, and Barney with a great roll of old sacking over his shoulder that the miller had lent them, and her bright black eyes went wide.

"Shade?" she said. "Oh, my goodness, you mustn't trouble yourselves. I'm quite all right. I've seen hotter. Where I come from, over the hills..."

But Sadie Belle only smiled at her, and Brody was already setting down the poles.

They built it right in front of her hollow, where she liked to sit. Brody's father came along to do the heavy part, digging the holes and setting the four tall poles firmly in the ground, because Benny said they must be firm enough not to fall on anybody. Then Brody, who was tall enough to reach, tied the corners of the sacking to the tops of the poles, under Benny's directions, so that it stretched out flat overhead like a roof. And Barney, who Benny had been worried about, turned out to be very good at one thing in particular, which was splashing.

"If you wet the sacking," said Barney, "the water dries off it. And when water dries off, it takes the heat with it. That's why you feel cold when you get out of the creek." He looked at Benny. "Isn't it?"

Benny looked at him in surprise.

"That's exactly right," he said. "That's evaporation."

"I just call it splashing," said Barney, and he took a bucket, a small one he could carry easily, and filled it at the edge of the creek where it was shallow, and very carefully tossed the water up over the sacking, a pawful at a time, until it was dark and damp all over.

And the air underneath the sacking, almost at once, went cool. Not cold. But cool, and shady, and soft, like the air inside a cave.

Granny Opal sat in her little chair under the damp sacking roof, with her paws in her lap, and closed her eyes.

"Oh, my," she said softly. "Oh, my, that's lovely."

Sadie Belle sat down on the ground beside her chair.

"We'll come back every afternoon," she said, "and wet it again. Barney's in charge of splashing."

"I am the Chief Splasher," said Barney, with great dignity.

Granny Opal opened one eye and looked at the four of them, standing round her in a row, hot and sticky and very pleased with themselves.

"Oh, my dears," she said, and closed her eyes again. "It's like sitting under a little cloud."


Chapter Six — Cold Things

At the Mill, Reba and Maggie Mae found the miller's wife exactly where Reba had said, sitting in her big chair in the Mill kitchen with the door shut, fanning herself with a pie plate.

"It's too hot to open the door," said the miller's wife. "It's like an oven out there. And it's like an oven in here. And I can't get down to the creek, because of my back, and I can't carry the water from the pump, not in this heat, and the miller's been out at the fields all week, from dawn till dusk, getting the hay in before it spoils."

So that afternoon, Reba and Maggie Mae set to work.

They opened the back door, the one on the north side of the Mill, which looked out over the mill-race, where the water came rushing down from the creek to turn the wheel. Even with Silver Creek running so low, there was still water in the race, and the air that came in off it was damp and cool. Then they opened the front door too. And even with no wind at all, a thread of cooler air began to drift in off the water of the race, slow and faint, and wander through the kitchen and out of the other side.

"A through-draught," said Maggie Mae. "That's what Grandpappy Buck calls it. You open the door on the cool side and the door on the hot side, and the cool air pushes the hot air out."

The miller's wife sat in her big chair in the middle of the through-draught and sighed with relief.

Then Reba fetched water from the pump. Not in the big bucket, which was too heavy for anybody, but in a little jug, one jugful at a time, walking back and forth across the yard along the strip of shade by the wall, again and again, until the big stone crock in the shady corner of the kitchen was full. And Maggie Mae made cucumber sandwiches, very thin, with the cucumbers she had brought from her own garden, and a jug of mint water, with mint from the Mill's own herb bed, chilled in the cellar.

"Eat," said Maggie Mae, setting a plate in the miller's wife's lap. "Cool things. Cucumbers are the coolest food there is."

The miller's wife ate a cucumber sandwich. Then another. Then she looked at Reba, who was very hot and very pink from walking back and forth across the yard with the jug, and who had not once, in the whole of the afternoon, gone down to the cellar with the cheeses.

"You've been back and forth across that yard all afternoon," said the miller's wife. "And you haven't even been down to the cellar once."

"No," said Reba, a little surprised at herself. "I haven't, have I."

The miller's wife patted the stool beside her, right in the middle of the through-draught, and poured Reba a cup of mint water, and held out the plate.

"Then you'd better sit here and have a sandwich," she said. "Before you melt."

So Reba had a sandwich. And then, since she was there, another one. And it was, she said afterwards, the best sandwich of the whole hot week, though it was only cucumber.


Chapter Seven — The Rabbits

Charlie and Beau did not know what to do about the rabbits.

They stood outside the burrow, a round hole in a grassy bank at the edge of Fern Meadow, and listened. From inside came the sound of eight hot, cross, sleepless rabbits. Somebody was crying. Somebody else was saying "It's my turn by the door." Somebody else was saying "Stop touching me," and somebody else was saying "I'm not touching you," and the littlest rabbit's mother, whose husband was out at the fields with the hay, was saying, very wearily, "Please, everybody, just try to lie still."

"We can't make the burrow bigger," said Charlie. "Or cooler."

"No," said Beau.

They thought.

"But we could make it emptier," said Charlie slowly. "For a bit. If the little ones came out somewhere cool, the burrow would be quieter. And then their mother could have a rest."

Beau's ears went up.

"Where's cool?" he said.

They both thought about it. Then they both said, at the same moment:

"The Great Barn."

The Great Barn, at the edge of Fern Meadow, was big and high and dim inside, with high old timber walls that kept out the sun, and great doors at each end that, if you opened them both, let a breeze go right through, when there was a breeze. And even when there was not, it was the coolest place for a long way round, because the roof was so high that all the heat went up into the rafters and stayed there.

So Charlie went in and asked the littlest rabbit's mother, very politely, whether the little ones might come to the Great Barn for the afternoon. And the littlest rabbit's mother looked at Charlie, and at Beau, and at the seven little rabbits all squabbling in the heat, and said yes, so fast that it was almost before Charlie had finished asking.

"I'll come along too," she said. "And sit in the cool. And close my eyes."

So they all went to the Great Barn, the seven little rabbits and their mother and Charlie and Beau, in a long, hot, straggling line across the meadow. And once they were inside, in the dim shadow, with both great doors open, Beau did what Beau was good at.

He was silly.

He did his fence post walk, shuffling round and round an imaginary post, chasing an imaginary shadow, sighing tremendously every time his tail got in the imaginary sun. The little rabbits laughed until they hiccupped. Then he taught them a game called Statues, where you have to keep perfectly still, which was a very good game for a hot afternoon, because keeping still is cool. And Charlie told them a story, very quietly, about a snowflake who got lost in summer and went looking for winter, and the littlest rabbit listened with her ears up the whole way through, and asked for it again.

And at the far end of the barn, in the coolest corner, on a heap of last year's hay, the littlest rabbit's mother lay down, and closed her eyes, and had the first proper rest she had had all week.


Chapter Eight — The Shortcut of Rusty's Life

That afternoon, Rusty had the hardest job of all.

Great-Grandpappy Buck was sitting on his porch in the full afternoon sun, in his dungarees and his old cap, rocking very slowly in his chair, with his eyes half closed, looking very hot indeed under his cap.

"Afternoon, Rusty," he said.

"Afternoon, Grandpappy Buck," said Rusty. He had been thinking all the way up the path about how to say it. He had thought of a great many clever ways. In the end, he forgot all of them and just said it.

"It's too hot on your porch," he said. "And everybody's worried about you. And I know a place in the woods, with a spring, where it's cool, and there's shade, and you can put your paws in the water. And I'd like to take you there."

Buck looked at him over his spectacles.

"I've sat on this porch every afternoon since before your grandmother was born," he said. "Through hot summers and cold ones. I don't see why a bit of weather should move me now."

Rusty had been ready for that.

"I know," he said. "That's why I'm not asking you to come because of the weather." He took a deep breath. "I'm asking you to come because I found it. All by myself. And it's the best place I've ever found. And I want you to be the one I show."

Buck rocked, and thought about that for a while.

Then Buck began to chuckle, very softly.

"Well," he said, "if that's not the cleverest shortcut I've ever been taken on, I'll eat my cap." He reached for his walking stick, and stood up, very slowly. "Lead the way. But take the long way round, mind. My shortcut days are behind me."

So Rusty took the long way round, in the shade of the late afternoon. The ordinary way, the way that was not a shortcut at all, along the shady side of the creek path and up through the trees past the Sugar Grove, with Rusty's father walking alongside. It took a very long time.

Halfway up, there was a place where the old maples leaned together over the path and made a green tunnel, deep and shady, and Buck stopped in the middle of it and stood there with his eyes closed and his cap in his paw, just breathing. Rusty, who usually went through that tunnel at a run, stopped too, and stood beside him, and breathed, and found that he did not mind waiting at all.

And when they got to the top, Great-Grandpappy Buck sat down on a mossy rock beside the spring, under the great old maples, and took off his cap, and put his old paws into the cold water.

He did not say anything for quite a while.

Then he said, "Rusty. You were right."

And Rusty, who had found a great many things in his life, decided that being told he was right by Great-Grandpappy Buck was the very best of them.


Chapter Nine — Paws in the Creek

By Friday evening, the heat had not broken.

But Granny Opal sat under her damp sacking roof every afternoon, with Barney splashing it whenever it started to dry. And the little rabbits spent every afternoon in the Great Barn, playing Statues with Beau, while their mother rested in the corner. And Great-Grandpappy Buck went up to the spring with Rusty every afternoon, the long way round, and came back looking ten years younger, he said, which still left him a good deal older than anybody else.

And every morning and every afternoon, Maverick flew over the Hollow, high and slow, and came back to report. He spotted the grown-ups out at the fields getting the hay in, and every day after that the youngsters carried jugs of the miller's wife's mint water out to them in the middle of the morning, along the shade of the hedges, with Brody carrying a jug in each paw. And on Friday afternoon, before he flew home to his roost, he landed on the rail of Grandpappy's Bridge, and said that he had flown over the whole Hollow from end to end, and that everybody who needed somebody had somebody.

That evening, as the sun went down behind Weather Ridge in a haze of gold, the whole Hollow came down to the creek.

They came to the wide, shallow stretch just below Grandpappy's Bridge, where the water ran over the stones only ankle-deep, and where the grown-ups could see everybody at once. Granny Opal came, on Sadie Belle's arm. The miller's wife came in the cart, and the miller lifted her down, gently, onto a flat stone at the water's edge. The rabbits came, all nine of them, with the littlest one on her mother's back. And Great-Grandpappy Buck came slowly along the bank on his stick, with Rusty beside him.

They all sat down together along the bank, young and old, side by side, and took off their shoes, those who wore them, and put their paws in the creek.

The water was not cold, like the spring. Silver Creek was much too low and warm for that. But it was cool. And it was moving. And after the long hot day, it felt like the best thing in the world.

There was a great deal of contented sighing, and not much talk.

The little rabbits splashed in the shallows, with their mother sitting right beside them. And Great-Grandpappy Buck sat on the bank with his old paws in the water and his cap pushed back, and looked at just one thing: Granny Opal's small pink paws in the creek, right next to Sadie Belle's hooves.

Then he said, "When you can't fix the whole trouble, fix the corner of it you can reach."


Chapter Ten — Low Water

They sat by the creek until the light was nearly gone.

The heat had not gone. It was still there, warm and heavy over the valley, even now, with the sun down. But down by the water, it did not seem to matter so much. The creek ran on over the stones, low and slow and gentle, murmuring to itself, cooling everybody's paws. The bats came out over the water, flickering. The crickets started up in the dry grass along the bank, and somewhere among the reeds one late frog croaked, once, and thought better of it. And one by one, the grown-ups stood up, and lit their lanterns, and began to gather everybody up for home. Sadie Belle's mother took Granny Opal's arm, with her lantern in her other paw, to walk her home along the creek path.

Benny and Barney sat on a little longer, with their paws still in the water.

They sat side by side on the flat stone at the edge, looking at the creek. Benny had his notebook on his knee, though it was much too dark to write. He had written a great deal in it that week. Lists, mostly. Who needed what, and who was doing it, and how many jugs of water Reba had carried across the Mill yard, which was a hundred and twelve, and how many times Barney had splashed Granny Opal's roof, which Barney said was a thousand and Benny said was thirty-one.

"It's low," said Benny quietly. "Silver Creek. Lower than I've ever seen it. Look. You can see the stones in the middle, all the way across."

Barney looked. It was true. In the very last of the light, the creek ran thin and shining between the stones, where usually it ran deep and dark and full.

"Grandpappy Buck says it hasn't been this low since he was a boy," said Benny.

"It'll come back up," said Barney. "When it rains."

"It will," said Benny. He was quiet for a moment, watching the water slide over the stones. "It makes you think, though. About where it's all going. All that water. Down past the Mill, and the Beaver Ponds, and out of the Hollow, and away. Further than we've ever been." He frowned. "I've never thought about that before. Where the creek goes, after it leaves."

Barney looked at his brother. Then he looked at the creek, running away down the valley in the dusk, thin and silver and shining, towards somewhere neither of them had ever seen.

"Somewhere good, I expect," said Barney.

Rusty's father called them from the path, holding up his lantern, ready to walk them home.

The brothers stood up, and dried their paws on the grass, and went up the bank to the path.

The night was still warm. Not a breath of wind. All over the Hollow, folk had left their doors and windows wide open for whatever cool the dark might bring. And along the rail of Grandpappy's Bridge, where everybody had left them on Tuesday morning and forgotten them, Charlie's paper fans lay in a long row, daisy side up, every one of them perfectly still.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
