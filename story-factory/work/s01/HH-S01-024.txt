Chapter One — Just Water

On a hot, still afternoon at the end of July, Charlie lay on her back in the long grass of Fern Meadow and looked at the sky.

She was supposed to be helping Maggie Mae pick beans. She had picked eleven. Then she had lain down for a moment, because it was hot, and the grass was soft, and the sky was very, very blue, with big white clouds sailing slowly across it like ships.

And she had seen a castle.

It was right there, above Sunrise Ridge. A great white castle, with towers, and a wall, and a round tall tower at one end with a pointed top, and a sort of drawbridge hanging down from the middle.

"Maverick!" she called. "Maverick, come and look!"

Maverick was sitting on his favourite fence post at the edge of the meadow, sunning his wings. He came gliding down and landed in the grass beside her, with a soft whump.

"Look at what?" he said.

"The castle," said Charlie, pointing. "Up there. Over the ridge. Can you see it? With the towers?"

Maverick looked up at the sky, where she was pointing. He looked hard, with one eye, and then with the other.

"That," he said at last, "is a cumulus cloud."

"A what?"

"A cumulus," said Maverick, settling his feathers importantly. "A big puffy one. You get them on hot afternoons, over the ridges. The sun warms the ground, and the warm air goes up, in a big invisible column, and when it gets high enough, it cools down, and the water in it turns into a cloud. It's just water, Charlie. Tiny drops of water, floating." He shook his feathers, as if the very thought made them damp. "I go round them. Grandpappy Buck's orders, no flying into cloud, and he's quite right. You can't see a thing in there."

Charlie looked at him. Then she looked back up at the sky.

"But it's a castle," she said.

"It's water," said Maverick.

"It's water shaped like a castle," said Charlie.

Maverick opened his beak to argue. Then he looked up again, and squinted, and tilted his head on one side.

The cloud over Sunrise Ridge did have a sort of tower at one end. And a sort of wall. And, if you looked at it a certain way, a sort of drawbridge hanging down from the middle.

"It's a bit like a castle," he admitted. "A very damp one."

"Who lives in it?" said Charlie.

Maverick stared at her.

"Nobody lives in it," he said. "It's a cloud."

"But if somebody did," said Charlie. "Who?"

And Maverick, who had flown round more clouds than anybody in the Hollow and never once thought about who might live in them, found that he did not know what to say.


Chapter Two — The Cloud Princess

Charlie, it turned out, knew exactly who lived in the castle.

"A cloud princess," she said, lying back in the grass with her paws behind her head. "And she's very small. Smaller than me, even. And she lives in the tallest tower, the round one with the pointy top. And every morning she looks out of her window, and down at all the creatures in the world, and wishes she could come down and visit." She frowned at the sky. "But she can't. Because she's made of cloud. And if she came down, she'd turn into rain."

Maverick lay down in the grass beside her, which eagles do not usually do, and looked at the castle.

"That's quite sad," he said.

"It is quite sad," agreed Charlie.

They watched the castle drift slowly across the sky. As it went, it changed. The tall tower got taller, and then began to lean. The drawbridge went thin, and then broke off, and floated away on its own.

"Oh no," said Charlie. "The drawbridge."

"It's evaporating," said Maverick. "The edges dry up in the sun."

"It's being stolen," said Charlie. "By a..." She looked round the sky. Far off, over Weather Ridge, there was a long, thin, wispy cloud, stretched out like a pulled thread of wool. "By that. The Wisp."

Maverick looked at the long thin cloud.

"That's a cirrus," he said. "It's very high up. Much higher than the castle. It's made of ice, not water. I've never flown that high."

"Ice," said Charlie, delighted. "Of course. The Wisp is made of ice. He's cold and thin and sneaky, and he steals drawbridges."

Maverick thought about this.

"Why does he steal drawbridges?" he said.

Charlie looked at him, surprised.

"I don't know," she said. "You decide."

Maverick looked up at the sky. He had never decided anything about a cloud in his life, except whether it was wet. He looked at the long, thin, icy cirrus, stretched out high over Weather Ridge, all on its own, far away from all the big friendly puffy clouds.

"Because he's lonely," said Maverick slowly. "He's so high up. Higher than anybody. And he's made of ice, so he's cold, and none of the other clouds go near him. So he steals drawbridges. So that somebody will come after him."

Charlie sat up and stared at him.

"Maverick," she said. "That's brilliant."

Maverick looked very surprised. Then he fluffed up his chest feathers, ever so slightly.

"It is quite brilliant," he said. "Yes."


Chapter Three — Tomorrow's Sky

They might have left it there. But the next morning, when Charlie came out of her burrow under the big beech, Maverick was already sitting on the lowest branch, waiting for her.

"I've been up," he said, before she could even say good morning. "Since the sun was up enough to warm the ridges. Up and up, round and round, as high as I could go. Looking at the sky. Properly. Not just at whether it was wet." He sounded slightly surprised at himself. He shuffled his feet on the branch. "There's no castle today. But there's something else."

"What?" said Charlie.

"Come and see," said Maverick.

He flew ahead, and she ran after him, up to the top of Fern Meadow, where you could see the whole sky. And there, over the valley, spread out across the morning like a great rumpled blanket, were hundreds and hundreds of little round puffs of cloud, all in rows, close together, ripple after ripple.

"Mackerel sky," said Maverick. "That's what the old folk call it. Because it looks like the scales on a mackerel. A fish," he explained, because Charlie looked blank. "Little high clouds, all in rows."

Charlie looked at the rows of little round clouds.

"It's an army," she said slowly. "No. It's not an army. It's sheep. Hundreds of cloud sheep. And they belong to the Princess. And when the Wisp stole her drawbridge, the sheep all went out over the sky to look for it." She clapped her paws. "That's what happens next!"

"That's tomorrow's chapter," said Maverick, and he sounded almost excited. "The sky writes it. Every day, there's different clouds. So every day, there's a different bit of the story."

Charlie stared at him.

"A story," she said, "that the sky writes. A bit at a time. And we just have to look up and read it."

"Exactly," said Maverick. "I'll go up every morning and tell you what's coming. And you tell me what it means."

They looked at each other. Then they both looked up at the mackerel sky, with its hundreds and hundreds of cloud sheep, and laughed.

That afternoon, Charlie told the story so far to Maggie Mae, while they picked beans. And Maggie Mae told Reba. And Reba told Benny and Barney. And by teatime, there were six youngsters lying on their backs in Fern Meadow, looking up at the sky, arguing about which of the little round clouds was the bravest sheep.


Chapter Four — Everybody's Chapter

By the third day, everybody in the Hollow wanted to be in the story.

It happened very simply. Every morning, Maverick went up over the ridges, early, and looked at the sky, and came back down and reported what kind of clouds were coming. And every afternoon, the youngsters lay on their backs in Fern Meadow and watched the clouds come, and decided what was happening in the story.

And everybody saw something different.

On the third day, the sky was full of big, flat-bottomed, fluffy cumulus clouds, all sailing along in a line.

"Boats," said Benny at once. "A fleet of cloud boats, sailing to rescue the Princess. I've counted them. Eleven."

"Twelve," said Barney.

"Eleven."

"There's a little one at the back."

"That's not a boat. That's a lump."

"It's a boat with a bear in it," said Barney. "Rowing. He's a very bad rower. Look, he's going round in circles."

And the little cloud at the back really was turning slowly round on itself, so that even Benny had to admit, crossly, that it might be a boat after all, and that the bear was hopeless.

Brody said he could see a cloud tree, very small, just starting to grow, on the back of one of the boats. Sadie Belle said that the last boat was the slowest, but it was going to get there first, because it wasn't in a hurry.

On the fourth day, there was a cloud that looked like a very long, thin fox, running. Rusty claimed it at once.

"That's me," he said. "That's a cloud fox. He knows a shortcut through the sky. Straight to the Wisp."

"Does it work?" said Charlie.

Rusty looked at the cloud fox, which was already stretching out thinner and thinner in the wind, until its legs came off.

"No," he said. "But he gets a lovely view."

"Pie," said Reba, pointing at a big round cloud with a criss-cross pattern across its top. "Lattice top. That's the Princess's birthday pie."

"Clouds aren't pies," said Maverick.

Everybody looked at the cloud. Maverick looked at the cloud.

"That is," he said slowly, "the most pie-shaped cumulus I have ever seen."

"The bear only came to the castle for the pie," said Reba. "Anybody would."

Beau found a cloud that looked exactly like Beau, in the middle of a magnificent leap.

"Look at that form," he said. "Look at that tail."

The cloud fell apart into three pieces.

"Wind," said Beau, after a moment. "Very bad wind."

And the littlest rabbit, who had been allowed to come and lie in the meadow too, pointed at a tiny round cloud all on its own and said it was a cloud baby rabbit, and it was lost, and it was crying, and everybody had to agree that the next part of the story was finding the cloud baby rabbit and taking it home, because otherwise the littlest rabbit would not stop worrying about it.

Maggie Mae, who had come to see what everybody was doing lying in the grass instead of picking beans, lay down beside Charlie for just a minute, to see. She saw a cloud that looked like a rose. Then a cloud that looked like a teacup. Then a cloud that looked exactly like a garden, with paths in it, going round in a wheel. And she stayed lying down for the whole afternoon, and the beans were never picked at all, and she said she did not mind in the least.

Maverick, meanwhile, had changed.

He still went up every morning, and came down, and said things like "Altocumulus, coming in from the west, medium height," very importantly. But now, after he had said it, he would add something else. Quietly. Almost shyly.

"And I think," he said, on the fourth morning, "that the Wisp is watching. From up high. He's watching the cloud boats come. And he's a little bit sorry about the drawbridge."

Everybody looked at him.

"That's good," said Charlie. "That's really good, Maverick."

Maverick looked at the grass.

"I made it up," he said. "Out of my own head." He sounded astonished.


Chapter Five — The Stuck Part

On the fifth day, the story got stuck.

It was not the sky's fault. The sky that day was plain, flat, and grey, from one side to the other. A thin, even sheet of cloud, with no shapes in it at all, like a great grey blanket pulled over the whole valley.

"Stratus," said Maverick, coming down from his morning flight. "Low and flat. Nothing in it. It'll be like this all day."

Everybody lay on their backs in Fern Meadow and looked up at the flat grey sky.

There was nothing to see. No castles. No sheep. No boats, no foxes, no pies.

"What happens today?" said the littlest rabbit.

Nobody knew.

They lay and looked at the grey sky, and the story just sat there, stuck, like a cart in mud. Barney said maybe the Princess was having a day off. Benny said that was not how stories worked. Rusty said maybe the whole castle had fallen in the sea. Charlie said there wasn't a sea, and Rusty said there might be, they hadn't seen the whole sky. They argued about it until Maggie Mae said that if they could not agree, perhaps they should all go home for lunch.

Charlie went home very cross.

She sat outside her burrow under the big beech, with her chin on her paws, and glared at the grey sky.

"Silly old stratus," she said. "Ruining everything."

There was a soft whump on the branch above her. It was Maverick.

"It isn't ruining it," he said. "It's just a grey day. There's always grey days." He thought for a moment. "Once, last autumn, the stratus came right down on Sunrise Ridge while I was sitting on a rock up there, and I was inside it without flying anywhere at all. It's very strange. You can't see anything. Not the ground, not the sky, not even the end of my own beak. Just grey, all round you, soft and wet. It's like being inside a blanket. And it's very, very quiet."

Charlie looked up at him.

"Inside a blanket," she said slowly. "Very quiet."

"Yes," said Maverick.

"Then that's it," said Charlie, and sat up. "That's today's chapter. The cloud boats sail into the grey, and nobody can see anything, and it's all soft and quiet, and they have to go very slowly, and listen. And they listen, and listen, and then..." She stopped. "And then they hear the cloud baby rabbit. Crying. Because it's lost in the grey too. And they follow the sound, and they find it."

Maverick looked at her.

"That," he said, "is the best chapter yet."

"It's your chapter," said Charlie. "You told me what being inside a cloud is like. I didn't know."

Maverick fluffed up his chest feathers. Then, slowly, he let them down again.

"It's our chapter," he said.


Chapter Six — The Giant

The sixth day was the hottest yet.

It was hot from the moment the sun came up. Hot and heavy and still, with not a breath of wind, and the air so thick and close that it felt like breathing through a warm wet cloth. The creek ran low and slow. The birds stopped singing by mid-morning. Even the bees seemed tired.

Maverick came down from his morning flight early, and landed on the fence post at the top of Fern Meadow, where the youngsters were already gathering. His feathers were ruffled, and he had a strange look on his face.

"There's a castle building," he said. "Over Weather Ridge. A big one. Bigger than any of them."

Everybody looked west. And there it was, far away over Weather Ridge: a great white cloud, puffy and bright, piling itself up into the sky, higher and higher, tower on tower.

"It's the Princess's castle!" said Charlie. "It's come back! It's bigger than ever!"

All day, they watched it grow. It grew and grew. By late afternoon, it was taller than any cloud anybody in the Hollow had ever seen, a vast white mountain of a cloud, boiling and bulging upwards, so tall you had to tip your head right back to see the top of it. And the bottom of it, all along its base, had gone dark. Grey. Then darker grey.

"It's a giant," said Barney, with great satisfaction. "That's a giant cloud. Coming to fight the Princess."

Everybody looked at Maverick, waiting for him to say what kind of cloud it was.

But Maverick was not looking at the cloud like a storyteller. He was looking at it the way an eagle looks at a sky he has to fly through. Very carefully. With his head on one side, and his eyes narrowed.

"Look at the top," he said quietly.

They looked. Right at the very top of the enormous cloud, high above everything, the top had spread out flat and wide, like the top of a giant mushroom, or a blacksmith's anvil, stretching away in a great white shelf across the sky.

"Cumulonimbus," said Maverick. "That's what it's called when the top goes flat like that. It means it's got so tall it's hit the ceiling of the sky, and it can't go up any more, so it spreads out." He looked at them all, lying in the grass. "It means a thunderstorm."

There was a pause in the meadow.

"A real one?" said Charlie.

"A big one," said Maverick. "And it's coming this way."

Far away, over Weather Ridge, so faint you could hardly hear it, something rumbled.

The littlest rabbit's ears went straight up, and she moved a little closer to Brody.

"Is the giant cross?" she whispered.

Nobody quite knew what to say. Then Brody picked her up, very gently, and set her on his knee.

"He's just clearing his throat," said Brody, in his big slow voice. "Like Grandpappy Buck does, before a story."

The littlest rabbit thought about this, and decided it was probably true, and stopped trembling.


Chapter Seven — Getting Home

Nobody had to be told twice.

But there were a great many youngsters in Fern Meadow, scattered all over it, lying in the long grass, and some of them were very small, and some of them were a long way from home. And the grown-ups were all busy at the Mill and in the fields, on the hottest day yet, and might not have looked up at the sky at all.

Maverick took charge.

"Benny," he said. "How long have we got? From the rumble?"

Benny looked at Maverick. Then he looked at the sky, and listened, and waited. A flicker of lightning went down, far away, over Weather Ridge, so thin and quick you would have missed it if you had blinked. Benny started counting, under his breath, slowly. He got to thirty before the rumble came.

"Thirty," he said. "That's far. Six miles, about. But it's coming."

"Then there's time," said Maverick. "Not a lot. Enough." He looked round at them all. "Everybody go home. Now. Not running. Walking fast. And everybody take somebody small." He looked at the littlest rabbit. "Brody, take her. Charlie, go with Maggie Mae. Rusty, you know every shortcut. Tell the folk at the Mill and the fields, and then stay at the Mill with the grown-ups till it's over. Quick feet, not silly feet. Beau, tell the families along the creek. Sadie Belle, the ones by the woods. And both of you, stop at the first house that takes you in."

Rusty set off at once, quick but careful, keeping to the lane, towards the Mill and the fields, where the grown-ups were working with their backs to the west and had not seen the sky at all. Beau went along the creek, knocking on doors. And everybody else went too. Walking fast, in twos and threes, each with somebody smaller, across the meadow towards home, while behind them the great dark cloud came on over Weather Ridge, and the light went strange and yellow, and the first cool breath of wind came rushing ahead of it through the long grass.

"What about you?" said Charlie, at the edge of the meadow, looking back.

"I'm going in too," said Maverick. "Not to my hickory. You never shelter under a tree in a thunderstorm, not even a magnificent eagle. I'm going up in the rafters of the Great Barn, where it's dry. It's only just across the meadow." He spread his wings. "And Charlie."

"What?"

"The giant," said Maverick. "In the story. He's not coming to fight the Princess." He looked up at the great black-bottomed cloud, towering over the ridge. "He's bringing the rain. Because everything's thirsty. The creek's low. The flowers are drooping. The whole Hollow's been waiting for rain all week." He looked at her. "He's coming to help."

Charlie stared at him.

Then Maverick lifted off, low and fast, across the meadow and in through the big open doors of the Great Barn, and Charlie hurried after Maggie Mae towards Rose Arch Cottage.

Rusty reached the Mill with the wind at his back, and the miller had the big doors shut behind him almost before he had finished his sentence, and then sent the grown-ups from the fields to fetch everybody else in. Beau's last knock was at a cottage near the bridge, and the grown-up who opened the door pulled him straight in and sat him down by the window. Sadie Belle's first door by the woods opened wide, and she stayed behind it while the grown-ups inside went round the rest of the houses themselves. And after that the grown-ups took charge of everything: the fields were cleared, the doors were shut, and the little ones were counted, twice.

By the time the first fat raindrops came smacking down onto the dusty lane, every youngster in the Hollow was indoors, and every grown-up too.


Chapter Eight — The Storm

It was a big storm. A very big storm indeed.

The rain came down as if the whole sky had been tipped over. It drummed on the roof of Rose Arch Cottage so loudly that Charlie and Maggie Mae had to shout to hear each other. The lightning flashed white at the windows, and the thunder came crashing down the valley a moment after, so close and so loud that it rattled the cups on the dresser.

Charlie sat on the window seat in Maggie Mae's kitchen, wrapped in a blanket, with her nose almost touching the glass, and watched it.

She thought about everybody else, all over the Hollow, in their own houses, watching the same storm from their own windows. Benny, counting between the flashes and the thunder, to see if it was coming closer or going away. Barney with his nose pressed to the glass like hers. The littlest rabbit, snug in her burrow with all her brothers and sisters, probably asleep. And Maverick, high in the rafters of the Great Barn, with his feathers fluffed up, dry and warm, listening to the rain drumming on the big roof, and not minding a bit, because he had seen it coming first.

She was not frightened. Not much. Maggie Mae was beside her, and the cottage was snug, and the rain was running down the window in long silver streams, and outside, the garden was drinking it all up.

"Maggie Mae," she said, between thunders. "Do you think the giant's sorry? For being so loud?"

Maggie Mae looked at her.

"What giant?"

So Charlie told her. About the giant cloud, and the flat top, and Maverick saying it was coming to help, because everything was thirsty.

Maggie Mae listened. Then she looked out of the window at her garden, at the roses lifting their heads in the rain, and the bean rows shining, and the rain barrel overflowing, and the dry brown patch on the lawn going dark and green again.

"I think," she said, "he's doing a very good job."

The storm lasted for an hour. Then, as suddenly as it had come, it began to go. The thunder rolled away down the valley, further and further, grumbling, until it was only a mutter far away over Sunrise Ridge. The rain thinned, and slowed, and stopped. And the clouds broke open, and the evening sun came pouring through the gaps, gold and slanting, and everything in Hickory Hollow was wet, and shining, and dripping, and fresh, and smelled of earth and leaves and rain.

Charlie and Maggie Mae came out onto the step of Rose Arch Cottage.

The whole valley was steaming gently in the sun. The creek, which had been low and slow all week, was running loud and brown and full. And over Sunrise Ridge, where the storm had gone, there was a rainbow. A whole one, from one side of the valley to the other, so bright it hardly looked real.

And just below the end of the rainbow, high over the ridge, the very last of the great giant cloud was sailing slowly away, golden and pink in the evening sun, with its top still spread out flat.

Like a castle.


Chapter Nine — The Last Chapter

That evening, they all came to Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch.

Everybody was there. Everybody was a little bit wet round the edges, from puddles, and everybody was very happy, because the air was cool and fresh for the first time in a week, and the creek was full, and nobody had been out in the storm.

Maverick flew in from the Great Barn. He landed on the porch rail, and shook the last drops of rain off his feathers, and everybody cheered.

"Maverick got us all home," said Brody, to Buck. "He saw the storm coming. Before anybody. From the shape of the cloud."

"And he told us all what to do," said Benny. "And who to take. And where to go."

"And he said the giant was coming to help," said Charlie.

Great-Grandpappy Buck looked at Maverick, over his spectacles.

"Did he, now," he said.

Maverick shuffled his feet on the rail.

"It's a story," he said, a little embarrassed. "Charlie and me. And everybody. The sky's been writing it. All week. A bit every day."

"Tell it to me," said Buck, and settled back in his rocking chair. "From the beginning. I've been hearing bits of it all week, all over the Hollow, and I can't make head nor tail of it. Somebody told me there was a bear in a boat."

"There is," said Barney. "He can't row."

So they told him.

They told him all of it, from the beginning, all talking at once and then taking turns: the castle over Sunrise Ridge, and the little Cloud Princess in the tallest tower who would turn into rain if she came down; the lonely Wisp, high and thin and made of ice; the eleven cloud boats ("Twelve," said Barney) and the bear who couldn't row; the cloud fox and his useless shortcut; and the grey day, when the boats sailed into the soft quiet blanket, and listened, and found the cloud baby rabbit. The littlest rabbit was very firm about that part.

And then, between them, they came to today. The giant.

"And the giant came over the mountains," said Charlie, "huge and dark and roaring, and everybody was frightened, and they all ran home. But the giant wasn't coming to fight anybody. He was coming to help. Because the whole world was thirsty. And he rained, and rained, and rained, until everything was drinking." She stopped, and thought. "And then..."

She looked at Maverick. Maverick looked at her.

"And then the Princess came down," said Maverick slowly. "In the rain. Because she was made of cloud, and the rain was made of cloud, and she'd always wanted to come down and visit. And now she could. And she came down to every garden in the world, all at once, and watered every flower." He looked out at the wet, shining evening, at the full creek and the dripping trees and the steaming meadow. "That's why everything's so green tonight."

Brody wiped his nose on the back of his paw, very quickly, so that nobody would see.

"And the Wisp?" said Charlie softly.

"The Wisp came too," said Maverick. "On the edge of the storm. And he gave back the drawbridge. And then, because he was made of ice, he turned into the cold part of the rain, the bit that makes your nose tingle. And all the other clouds came down with him, together, and he wasn't lonely any more."

He stopped.

Great-Grandpappy Buck sat very still in his rocking chair. Then he looked round the porch, at all the youngsters sitting on the boards and the steps, damp and happy, and at Charlie on the top step, and at Maverick on the rail beside her, with the low sun still bright on his white head.

Then he said, "A good story, a warm fire, and a friend beside you — that's the whole of what anybody needs."


Chapter Ten — Clear Sky

The sun was low over Weather Ridge by then, and the sky over the Hollow was clear.

Not a cloud anywhere. Not a castle, or a sheep, or a boat, or a fox. The storm had washed the whole sky clean, and it stretched from ridge to ridge, empty and deep and blue.

Charlie sat on the porch step and looked up at it.

"There's no story tomorrow," she said. "There's no clouds."

Maverick, on the rail beside her, looked up too.

"There will be," he said. "There's always clouds, round here, sooner or later." He looked at the low gold sun, and shuffled his feet. "And that's my bedtime. Eagles are home before dark. I'll go up first thing and see what's coming."

"Will you tell me?"

"I'll tell you first," said Maverick. "Goodnight, Charlie. Thank you for the castle."

"Goodnight, Maverick," said Charlie. "Thank you for the giant."

He spread his wings and lifted off the rail, and flew low across the meadow to his branch in the big hickory, with the sun still warm on his back. He was settled there, feathers smooth and folded, before the sun had even touched the ridge.

Charlie's mother had come up to Buck's porch while the story was being told, and had stood at the back to listen. Now she sat down on the top step, and Charlie leaned against her, and the sun slid down behind Weather Ridge, and the valley filled up slowly with blue dusk.

"Mama," said Charlie. "Did you know clouds are made of water? Tiny drops. Floating."

"I did know that," said her mother.

"And did you know there's a princess in one of them? A very small one. Smaller than me. And when it rains, that's her, coming down to visit."

"I did not know that," said her mother. "Tell me about her."

So Charlie told her. She had got as far as the Wisp when she stopped, and sat up, and pointed.

She had been wrong about the sky. There was one cloud after all. High, high up over Weather Ridge, higher than any eagle had ever flown, there was a long, thin thread of cirrus, all on its own. It was so high that it was still catching the last of the sunlight, long after the sun had gone from the valley below, and it hung there at the top of the dark-blue sky, soft and pink.

"That's him," said Charlie. "The Wisp. He gets the sun last, because he's highest of all."

Her mother looked up at it, and smiled.

"Then he's the one who says goodnight to everybody," she said.

They watched the thin pink thread together. Then, quite slowly, the colour faded off it, until it was only a pale grey wisp in the dusk.

"Night, Wisp," said Charlie, and yawned so wide that her whiskers trembled. Her mother laughed, and stood up, and held out a paw, and Charlie took it.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
