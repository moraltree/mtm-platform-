Chapter One — The Star Guide

Early in December, in the week before the moon goes dark, Hickory Hollow begins to look forward to Star Night.

It is an old custom, older than anybody can remember, and it comes before the Longest Night, in the very darkest part of the moon. On the first clear evening with no moon at all, everybody old enough to stay up a little late gathers on Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, wrapped in blankets, with mugs of hot cordial. The lanterns are put out. The fire is banked low. And everybody looks up.

And the Star Guide shows them the stars.

Not all of them. There are far too many for that. Just the old ones, the ones with names, the ones the first settlers of the Hollow looked up at on cold nights long ago and named, so that they would have friends in the dark. The Porch Star, which sits in the north, steady as a doorstep, and never moves, so that if you are ever lost you can find your way home by it. The Rocking Chair, five bright stars in a crooked zigzag, high up near the Porch Star, like a chair somebody has left out on a porch in the sky. The Little Lanterns, a tiny, misty cluster, like a pawful of sparks thrown up from a fire. And the Three Stepping Stones, three bright stars in a perfect row, rising over Sunrise Ridge in the early evening, as if somebody had laid them across the sky to walk on.

For two winters now, the Star Guide had been Maverick.

"Because I have the sharpest eyes in the Hollow," he would say, puffing out his chest. "Eagle eyes. I can see a beetle on a leaf from the other side of the valley."

He was a young bald eagle, bold and handsome and very sure of himself, with a gleaming white head, bright blue eyes, a tan explorer's vest covered in mountain patches and a red neckerchief. He was brave and loyal and kind, and he loved being Star Guide more than almost anything. Every winter he stood on Buck's porch rail with his wings spread wide and pointed out the old stars one by one in his grandest voice, and everybody went "Ooh", and it was the proudest night of his year.

This year was going to be the best Star Night of all. Because this year, for the first time, Charlie was allowed to come. And so were the three littlest rabbits.

"Is it soon?" said Charlie, bouncing on the bridge. "Is it soon? Will you show me the Little Lanterns? Will you show me the Rocking Chair?"

"The moon goes dark in four nights," said Maverick grandly. "And on the first clear night after that, I will show you every star that has a name."

Charlie squeaked so loudly that a heron took off from the creek in fright.

That night, the last thin sliver of the old moon rose late over Sunrise Ridge, long after everybody was in bed.

The next night, the clouds came.


Chapter Two — Staring at the Sky

They came in from the west, low and grey and heavy, and settled over the valley like a lid on a pot. And they stayed.

The moon went dark behind them. The night of the dark moon came and went. Not a single star showed. Not even a gap where a star might have been. Every evening the youngsters came up onto Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch in their blankets, and looked up at a sky as grey and blank as an old slate, and went home again.

On the fourth evening, Charlie did not say anything at all as she left. She just went quietly down the porch steps beside her mother, which for Charlie was very unusual.

Maverick hopped down the steps after them, and walked beside them along the snowy path to the round green door under the beech.

"Charlie," he said, at the door. "They will come out. The stars. They always do in the end."

Charlie looked up at him, very small in her blanket.

"When?" she said.

Maverick opened his beak to say tomorrow, the way he had said it three times already. Then he closed it again, because he did not know, and Charlie had heard tomorrow three times and it had never once been true.

"I don't know," he said at last. It was the hardest thing he had said all winter.

Charlie thought about that.

"That's all right," she said. "At least you didn't say tomorrow."

And she went inside, and her mother smiled at Maverick over her head, and closed the door.

Maverick did not like it. He did not like it at all. He was the Star Guide. And there was nothing he could do.

So he tried everything.

On the second cloudy day, he stood on the tallest fence post in Fern Meadow and stared at the clouds, very hard, without blinking, to make them go away.

"What's he doing?" asked Barney, who had come to watch.

"Staring at the clouds," said Benny.

"Is it working?"

Maverick's eyes began to water. Then his left eye twitched. Then he blinked, enormously, three times, and fell off the fence post backwards into a snowdrift.

"No," said Benny.

On the third cloudy day, Maverick decided to blow them away. He flew up and down Fern Meadow, flapping his great brown wings as hard as he possibly could, to make a wind. He flapped until his feathers stood on end. And he did make a wind. It went across the meadow and straight through Reba's back garden, where her mother's washing was hanging on the line, and it lifted three pairs of long woolly socks and a tablecloth and a nightcap clean off the line and carried them away down the hill.

Reba came running out, with her pink bow over one eye.

"MY MOTHER'S NIGHTCAP!" she shouted, and set off after it across the snow.

Everybody chased the washing. Rusty caught a sock in a bramble bush. Brody caught the tablecloth on his head. The nightcap blew all the way to the creek bank, where Beau made a tremendous leap and caught it in his teeth just before it landed in the water, and came back very proud, with the nightcap hanging from his mouth like a prize.

The clouds did not move an inch.

"Well," said Barney, lying on his back in the snow and looking up at them. "If you can't move them, you could at least name them. That one looks like a sheep. That one looks like a sheep that's fallen over. That one looks like Benny."

"It does not," said Benny.

"It's got a pencil behind its ear."

That evening, Maverick went up to Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, where Buck was sitting in his rocking chair looking at Weather Ridge, and asked him straight out when the clouds would go.

Buck looked at the ridge for a long while. Then he looked at Maverick, over his spectacles.

"What do you do," he said quietly, "about a thing you can't do anything about?"

Maverick opened his beak. He did not know. He did not want to know.

He flew off to the top of the tallest hickory by the creek, and sat there with his feathers fluffed out crossly, glaring at the grey.


Chapter Three — Into the Cloud

By the sixth cloudy day, Maverick had decided what he was going to do.

"I'm going to fly up above them," he told the others on the bridge. "Through the cloud and out of the top. The stars are still up there. I'll see if the clouds are clearing, and come and tell everybody."

"In the dark?" said Benny, alarmed.

"At dusk," said Maverick. "I can see perfectly well at dusk. Better than most."

"But inside a cloud you can't see anything," said Benny. "It's like being inside a bowl of porridge. How will you know which way is up?"

"I'm an eagle," said Maverick. "I always know which way is up."

Sadie Belle looked worried. "Have you asked Grandpappy Buck?"

"I don't need to ask anybody," said Maverick. "I'm the Star Guide. Finding the stars is my job."

And as the light began to fade that evening, he took off from the top of the tallest hickory and flew straight up into the clouds.

For a moment it was wonderful. He rose up and up, past the bare branches, over the roofs, into the bottom of the grey.

And then the grey was everywhere.

It was cold, and wet, and thick and white and dim all at once, so that he could barely see his own wingtips. There was no up. There was no down. There was no Hollow below and no sky above. There was only cloud, pressing damp against his feathers, and the light going dimmer and dimmer around him.

He flew on. He thought he was climbing. Then he was not sure. He turned, to try another way, and then he did not know which way he had turned.

And for the first time in his whole life, high in the air, Maverick did not know where he was. He did not know which way was up. That was the strangest, coldest feeling of all.

So he did the only sensible thing he could think of. He stopped flapping. He held his wings out wide and still. And he let himself glide, slowly, slowly, the way he had glided down a thousand times in daylight, trusting that down would come to meet him.

He came out of the bottom of the cloud far lower than he had expected, and much further away, over the dark snowy fields beyond the Old Mill. He saw a roof below him, and landed on it carefully, and gripped the ridge with his talons, and sat very still, getting his breath.

It was the roof of the miller's woodshed. He had never been so glad to see a woodshed in his life.

"Hello?" he called, rather shakily. "Hello down there?"

The woodshed door opened below him, and a lantern came out, and the miller looked up.

"Well, bless me," said the miller. "It's young Maverick. On my woodshed. In the dark." He held the lantern higher. "Come down, lad. Come down carefully. I'll walk you home."


Chapter Four — Grounded

By the time the miller had walked Maverick back along the creek path with his lantern, half of Hickory Hollow was out looking for him.

Benny had told. He had watched Maverick fly up into the cloud, and waited, and when Maverick did not come down again, he had run all the way to Buck's porch. Now there were grown-ups out along the creek with lanterns, calling, and the youngsters were huddled on Buck's porch with their families, looking very frightened.

When Maverick came up the path beside the miller, wet and bedraggled, with his neckerchief hanging limp and drips running off his beak, Charlie burst into tears of relief, and Brody hugged him so hard his feathers squeaked.

Great-Grandpappy Buck came slowly down the porch steps.

"Are you hurt?" he asked.

"No," said Maverick. "Just wet."

"Thank goodness," said Buck. Then his voice went firm, which it hardly ever did. "Young Maverick. You flew up into a cloud, as the light was going, on your own, without telling a grown-up. You gave everybody a terrible fright. Benny ran the whole way here."

"I know," said Maverick in a small voice. "I couldn't see anything up there. I didn't know which way was up. I've never not known that before." He looked at his talons. "I didn't think a cloud could do that."

"Now you know," said Buck. "So there'll be no more flying into cloud. And no flying after dark, not this winter, and not till you're a good deal older. Eagles are made for the daytime, Maverick."

"Yes, Grandpappy."

Buck looked at him a moment longer. Then he did not say anything else. He just put his old paw on Maverick's wet shoulder and kept it there, and walked him up the steps and into the warm kitchen, and sat him by the fire, and put a bowl of soup in front of him, and sat down beside him while he ate it.

When the soup was gone, and the families had taken the others home, Sadie Belle and Brody stayed on the hearth rug a little longer, with Brody's father waiting by the door.

"Can I tell you a secret?" said Maverick quietly.

They nodded.

"I can't see the little stars," said Maverick. "Not properly. In the daytime and at dusk, I can see better than anybody. But in the real dark, at night, the faint ones go all blurry. The Little Lanterns are just a sort of smudge. I've always just remembered where my mother told me they were, and pointed, and said the names." He hung his head. "I've never told anybody. I'm the Star Guide."

Brody thought about it carefully, the way he thought about most things.

"Remembering where they are is still knowing them," he said.

"But Charlie wants to see the Little Lanterns," said Maverick. "Properly. And when the clouds go, I'll be pointing at a smudge."

Sadie Belle tilted her head.

"Who sees best in the real dark?" she said.


Chapter Five — Asking

Maverick thought about it.

Not beavers. Not bears. Not deer, really, though Sadie Belle saw better than most at dusk. And then he thought of somebody who walked home with his family along the dark trails every winter evening without a lantern, and never once tripped, and always knew exactly where he was.

"Rusty," he said slowly.

"Foxes have eyes for the night," said Sadie Belle. "My mother says so. They shine in the lamplight, like little green moons. There's a sort of mirror at the back that catches every bit of light there is."

Maverick sat very still.

He did not like the idea. If he asked Rusty, then Rusty would know. And then everybody would know.

"I'd have to tell him I can't see properly," he said. "Out loud."

"Yes," said Sadie Belle.

Maverick looked into the fire.

"That's harder than flying into a cloud," he said.

The next morning, he found Rusty by the beaver ponds, nosing about in the snow. He landed on the frozen bank beside him, and stood there, and opened his beak, and shut it again.

"You're making faces," said Rusty.

"I need to ask you something," said Maverick. "It's quite difficult."

Rusty sat down with his white-tipped tail curled round his paws, and waited.

"Can you see the faint stars?" said Maverick. "Properly? At night? Like the Little Lanterns?"

"Course," said Rusty. "Clear as anything. Why?"

Maverick took a very big breath.

"Because I can't," he said. "Not in the real dark. And I want Charlie to see them properly on her first Star Night. And I can't show her, on my own. So I need your help." He looked at the snow. "Please."

Rusty did not laugh. He did not say fancy that. He just looked at Maverick for a moment as if he were seeing him a little differently, and then grinned.

"Course I'll help," he said. "But you'll have to teach me the names. I can see them. I just don't know what they're called."

Maverick stared at him.

"You don't know the names?"

"Never needed them. I just look. I call that steady one in the north Old Steady. And the little bunch of sparks, I call them the Fidgets, because they twinkle about so."

"The Fidgets," said Maverick faintly. "Those are the Little Lanterns. The first settlers named them."

"Little Lanterns," said Rusty, trying it out. "That's nicer, actually."

And for the first time in six cloudy days, Maverick laughed out loud.


Chapter Six — Waiting

The clouds stayed.

That evening, on Buck's porch, under the same flat grey sky, Charlie sat with her chin on the rail and looked up for a very long time.

"What if it's cloudy all winter?" she said. "What if I never get a Star Night?"

Maverick felt the old cross, helpless feeling begin to rise up in him, the one that made him want to stare at the clouds and blow at the clouds and fly up into the clouds.

He did not do any of those things.

Instead, he hopped down from the rail and sat beside her.

"I don't know when they'll go," he said. "Nobody does. Not even Grandpappy Buck. But while we wait, do you want to know where the stars are? Even if you can't see them yet?"

Charlie looked at him.

So Maverick pointed up at the blank grey sky, and showed her where everything was, behind the clouds. "The Porch Star is right there, in the north, steady as anything. The Rocking Chair is just above it, five stars in a zigzag, like this." He drew it in the air with his wingtip. "And the Stepping Stones will be rising over Sunrise Ridge, just about now, right there, three in a row."

Charlie stared at the grey patch where he pointed, very hard.

"I think I can feel them," she said.

They sat there together for a long while, in the cold, under the clouds, while Maverick told her the old stories about every star, the ones his mother had told him. It was not Star Night. But it was not nothing, either.

Then Benny, who had been listening from the steps, sat up suddenly.

"We can't move the clouds," he said slowly. "But what if we could bring the stars inside?"

"How?" said Barney.

Benny did not know yet. He was looking at the lamp in Buck's window, and frowning.

It was Reba who solved the next part. She had been poking about in Buck's cellar earlier that day, looking for a jar of honey, which she had found, and she had found something else as well.

"There's a big old tin drum down there," she said. "Like a bucket with no handle. It's all covered in little holes. Somebody's punched a pattern in it."

Great-Grandpappy Buck, in his rocking chair, went quite still.

"That," he said, "was my grandmother's star-tin. I'd clean forgotten it."


Chapter Seven — The Star-Tin

The star-tin was big and round and dented, and black with age. When Beau carried it up from the cellar, with great care and only one small wobble on the steps, and they set it on Buck's kitchen table and polished it, they found that it was punched all over with holes. Big holes and small ones, and very small ones, in patterns.

But most of the holes had rusted shut. And nobody, not even Buck, could remember which star was meant to go where.

"Then we'll make it again," said Benny. "New holes. In the right places."

Maverick drew the stars. He did it on a sheet of paper wrapped round the outside of the tin, with a stub of charcoal held in his beak, with his eyes half closed, remembering. The Porch Star, there, steady. The five stars of the Rocking Chair. The Three Stepping Stones in their row, and the two bright stars above and below them, like the edges of a doorway.

But he could not draw the faint ones. He had only ever seen them as a smudge.

So Rusty did those. He sat beside Maverick and shut his own eyes, and remembered walking home under a clear sky.

"There," he said, pointing with his paw. "Six little ones, all close together. That's the Little Lanterns. You've drawn a smudge. It's six. Some nights seven." He pointed again. "And three really faint ones, hanging down from the Stepping Stones, in a little line. Like a sword. You've missed those."

"I've never seen those," said Maverick, astonished.

"They're there," said Rusty. "You have to look a bit to the side of them. Then they come out."

Then they disagreed about the Rocking Chair.

"It's five stars," said Maverick. "Five. A zigzag. Like a W."

"It's more like an M," said Rusty, tilting his head.

"It's a W."

"From my side of the creek it's an M."

"It's the same sky on both sides of the creek!"

They both looked at Benny, who looked at the drawing, and then turned the paper upside down, and then the right way up again.

"It depends what time of night you look," said Benny slowly. "It goes round the Porch Star. Doesn't it? Like everything up there. So sometimes it's a W and sometimes it's an M."

Maverick and Rusty looked at each other.

"I knew that," said Maverick.

"So did I," said Rusty.

Neither of them had.

Then Buck held the tin steady on the table, and Brody, very carefully, with his big gentle paws, punched a hole through the tin at every charcoal star with a nail and a little wooden mallet. Big holes for the bright stars. Little holes for the faint ones. The tiniest pinpricks of all for the faintest, the ones only Rusty could see.

Charlie held the paper flat for him, and counted every hole as it went in, so that none were missed.

Maggie Mae brought her darkest curtains from home, to hang over Buck's windows. Sadie Belle stitched them so they would meet in the middle with no gaps. Barney practised saying "Ooh" in different voices, to be ready. Reba made a plate of honey biscuits shaped like stars, and only ate four.

When it was done, Great-Grandpappy Buck looked at the star-tin on his table for a long while.

"My grandmother used to light it on cloudy nights," he said, "when I was smaller than Charlie. I'd forgotten all about it, till Reba went poking in my cellar."

"I wasn't poking," said Reba. "I was looking for honey."

"Well," said Buck. "I'm very glad you were."


Chapter Eight — Stars on the Ceiling

That evening, the clouds were as thick as ever. But nobody minded.

Everybody came to Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge at dusk: all the friends, and Charlie, and the three littlest rabbits with their mother, and the miller and his wife. They crowded into Buck's big front room and sat on the floor in their blankets. Maggie Mae's dark curtains were drawn across every window.

Beau set the star-tin in the middle of the floor, on a flat stone hearth-tile, where nobody would knock it.

Then Great-Grandpappy Buck lit his little oil lamp with its glass chimney, and lowered it carefully into the open top of the tin, so that it sat inside on the bottom, steady and safe, with the tin all round it and the top left open to the air. Nobody else went near it.

The grown-ups blew out every other light in the room.

For a moment it was dark.

And then the stars came out.

They spread across the low wooden ceiling and the walls and the faces of everybody sitting there, soft little points of light, big and small and very small, scattered across the dark room just as they were scattered across the winter sky above the clouds.

Nobody breathed.

Then Maverick hopped up onto the back of Buck's old armchair, and spread his wings, and began, very quietly this time.

"That one. There, on its own. That's the Porch Star. It never moves. If you're ever lost, find that one, and it'll take you home."

"And those five, in a zigzag above it," said Rusty, beside him. "The Rocking Chair."

"And those three in a row," said Maverick. "The Three Stepping Stones."

"And the faint little line hanging down from them," said Rusty. "Like a sword. Look a bit to the side of it."

Everybody looked a bit to the side. And there it was.

"And that," said Maverick, pointing to a tiny cluster of pinpricks near the edge, "is the Little Lanterns. I always thought it was a smudge. But Rusty says it's six. Sometimes seven."

Charlie counted the six tiny points of light on Buck's ceiling, twice, on her paws.

"Six," she whispered. "I can see six."

"Then you can see better than me," said Maverick.

They took it in turns, one remembering the names and stories, one finding the faint ones, until every star in the tin had been shown.

The three littlest rabbits had stopped listening some time ago. They were lying on their backs on the rug, side by side, holding up their paws in the dark, and catching stars on them. Wherever they put a paw, a little point of light landed on it, and they giggled, and moved it, and the star moved too.

"I've got the Porch Star," whispered one.

"I've got three," whispered another. "In a row."

The third one did not say anything. She was holding very still, with a tiny cluster of the faintest pinpricks resting right on the end of her nose, and her eyes were crossed trying to look at it.

In his armchair in the corner, in the soft starry dark, Great-Grandpappy Buck watched them, and did not say anything at all. But anybody close enough would have seen him wipe his spectacles, very quietly, on the corner of Bramble's quilt.


Chapter Nine — The Real Sky

It was Brody who noticed.

He had got up to fetch more cordial from the kitchen, and on the way he lifted the corner of one of Maggie Mae's curtains, just to peep, and he stopped.

"Everybody," he said in a hushed voice. "Look."

They looked.

Outside, over Silver Creek, the clouds were thinning. Not blowing away. Just going, quietly, from grey to pale to almost nothing, the way mist goes off a morning creek. And through the thin places, one at a time, came stars.

Real ones.

Then everybody moved at once.

Buck lifted the lamp out of the star-tin and turned it down to the smallest glow. Blankets were gathered up. And the whole lot of them went out onto Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, into the sharp, clean, freezing air, with the miller and the rabbit mother and Buck all standing among them, and looked up.

It was still early in the evening. The clouds had gone from Sunrise Ridge to Weather Ridge. And low in the west, just above Weather Ridge, hung a moon so new and thin that it was barely a silver thread, sinking slowly down behind the ridge as they watched. In a little while it was gone, and the sky was dark, and so full of stars that there hardly seemed any dark left between them.

"Now," whispered Charlie. "Show me. The real ones."

Maverick stood on the porch rail. He could see the bright ones clearly. The Porch Star, steady in the north. The Rocking Chair, zigzagging above it. And just rising over Sunrise Ridge, low and bright, the Three Stepping Stones in their perfect row. He pointed to each one in turn, and said its name.

Then he turned to Rusty.

"The Little Lanterns," he said. "Can you see them?"

Rusty looked up, a little to the side, the way he always did, and his eyes caught the faint glow from Buck's turned-down lamp and shone back like two small green moons.

"There," he said. "Up and to the right of the Stepping Stones, a good way up. Six. No. Seven. Seven tonight."

Charlie looked where his paw pointed.

And there they were. Not pinpricks on a ceiling this time. A tiny, misty, sparkling pawful of real stars, very far away in the real winter sky, like sparks thrown up from a fire a very long time ago.

"Oh," breathed Charlie. "The Little Lanterns."

Maverick could see a soft, shining smudge. He looked at Charlie's face, tipped up to the sky, and that was enough.


Chapter Ten — The Old Owl

Later, when the three littlest rabbits had fallen asleep in their mother's arms and been carried home, and the miller and his wife had gone off along the creek path with their lantern, the friends stayed on Buck's porch a little longer, wrapped in blankets, looking up. Their families sat with them, along the back of the porch, waiting with lanterns unlit.

Great-Grandpappy Buck sat in his rocking chair with his old banjo across his knees, not playing, just resting his paws on the strings.

"Grandpappy," said Maverick quietly. "I'm sorry about the cloud. Flying into it without asking."

"I know you are," said Buck.

"I didn't want to wait," said Maverick. "And I didn't want to ask anybody. Because the Star Guide's supposed to know." He looked at Rusty, curled on the boards beside him with his eyes half shut. "But I didn't know. Not all of it."

Buck rocked slowly, looking up.

"My grandmother had a saying," he said. "She used to say it to me when I was a young beaver who wanted to go everywhere and ask nobody." He smiled, up at the stars. "Ask the old owl before you fly the new trail."

There was a pause.

"Even if the old owl's a fox?" said Maverick.

Buck chuckled softly.

"Especially then," he said.

He began, very gently, to play. It was a quiet tune, slow and silvery. And as he played, the friends lay on the porch in their blankets, looking up.

Sadie Belle found the Porch Star all by herself, and said so, quietly. Benny counted the stars of the Rocking Chair, twice, to be sure. Barney said the Three Stepping Stones were obviously for giants, and there must be a giant river somewhere for them to cross. Reba wondered whether the Little Lanterns might be somebody's honey biscuits, very far away. Brody found the faint sword hanging from the Stepping Stones by looking a bit to the side of it, and was so pleased that he hummed along with the banjo without knowing he was doing it.

Charlie lay with her head on Maggie Mae's arm and counted the Little Lanterns. Six. Seven. Six. Seven.

And Maverick sat on the porch rail with his feathers fluffed against the cold, listening to Rusty beside him, who was very quietly naming every faint star that Maverick could not see, while Maverick told him its name.

When the tune was finished, the families lit their lanterns, one by one, and gathered up their sleepy youngsters, and set off home together along the creek path in little bobbing groups of light. Rusty went with his family, and turned at the bottom of the steps, and pointed up once more, at the Little Lanterns, so that Maverick would know where to look.

High above the sleeping Hollow, in the deep winter dark, the Porch Star sat where it always sits, in the north, not moving, the way it has sat for every creature who ever looked up from that valley on a cold, clear night and wanted to find the way home.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
