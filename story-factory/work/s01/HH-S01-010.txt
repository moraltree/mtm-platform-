Chapter One — The Star Guide

In the deep of winter, when the nights are long and the air is cold and clear as creek water, Hickory Hollow has a custom called Star Night.

On the first clear evening after the new moon in December, when the sky is at its very darkest, everybody who is old enough to stay up a little late gathers on Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, wrapped in blankets, with mugs of hot cordial in their paws. The lanterns are put out. The fire is banked low. And everybody looks up.

And the Star Guide shows them the stars.

Not every star. There are far too many for that. Just the old ones, the ones with names, the ones the first settlers of the Hollow looked up at on cold nights long ago and gave names to, so that they would have friends in the dark. The Porch Star, which sits in the north and never moves, so that if you are ever lost you can find your way home by it. The Little Lanterns, a tiny, misty cluster of stars, like a handful of sparks thrown up from a fire. The Three Stepping Stones, three bright stars in a perfect row, rising in the east, as if somebody had laid them across the sky to walk on.

For two winters now, the Star Guide had been Maverick.

"Because I have the sharpest eyes in the Hollow," he would say, puffing out his chest. "Obviously. Eagle eyes. I can see a beetle on a leaf from the other side of the valley."

He was a young bald eagle, bold and handsome and very sure of himself, with a gleaming white head, bright blue eyes, a tan explorer's vest covered in mountain patches, and a red neckerchief. He was loyal and kind and brave, and he loved being Star Guide more than almost anything. Every winter he stood on the porch rail with his wings spread wide and pointed out the old stars one by one in his best and grandest voice, and everybody went "Ooh," and it was the proudest night of his year.

This year, it was going to be the best Star Night ever. Because this year, for the very first time, Charlie was old enough to stay up for it. And so were the three littlest rabbits.

"Is it tonight?" asked Charlie, on the first morning of the new moon, bouncing on the bridge. "Is it tonight? Can we see the Little Lanterns? Can we see the Porch Star? Will you show me the Stepping Stones?"

"Tonight," said Maverick grandly, "if the sky is clear, I will show you every star that has a name. And maybe a few that don't."

Charlie squeaked so loudly that a heron took off from the creek in fright.

Maverick looked up at the sky. It was pale and grey and soft, the colour of an old blanket, from Sunrise Ridge to Weather Ridge.

"It'll clear by tonight," he said confidently. "It always does."


Chapter Two — Clouds

It did not clear by that night.

When the youngsters gathered on Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch at dusk, wrapped in blankets, with their mugs of cordial, the sky above Hickory Hollow was one solid, heavy, starless grey. There was not a single star. There was not even a gap where a star might have been.

"Where are they?" asked Charlie, staring up.

"Behind the clouds," said Maverick. "Resting. They'll be out tomorrow."

They were not out tomorrow. The next evening, the clouds were thicker than ever, low and damp, with little flurries of snow coming out of them. And the evening after that, the clouds came right down onto the tops of the ridges, so that the whole valley sat inside a soft grey bowl, with no sky at all.

On the fourth evening, Charlie stood at the porch rail in her blanket and looked up for a very long time. Then she went home without saying anything, which for Charlie was very unusual.

Maverick flew after her, low over the snowy path, and landed on the gatepost outside her family's round green door just as she reached it.

"Charlie," he said. "They will come out. The stars. They always do in the end."

Charlie looked up at him from the doorstep, very small in her blanket.

"When?" she said.

Maverick opened his beak to say tomorrow, the way he had said it four times already. Then he closed it again, because he did not know, and Charlie had heard tomorrow four times and it had never once been true.

"I don't know," he said at last. It was the hardest thing he had said all winter.

Charlie thought about that.

"That's all right," she said. "At least you didn't say tomorrow." And she went inside.

Maverick did not like it. He did not like it at all.

He was the Star Guide. Star Night was his night. And here were all the youngsters waiting, and Charlie waiting, and the littlest rabbits waiting, and there was nothing he could do about it. He could not make the clouds move. He could not fly up and push them out of the way. He could not even say when they would go.

So he tried everything else.

On the fifth day, he stood on the tallest fence post in Fern Meadow and stared at the clouds so hard that his eyes watered, in case they could be stared away. They could not.

On the sixth day, he flew up and down the valley, flapping his great wings as hard as he could, to make a wind that would blow the clouds off over the ridges. It made a small breeze that blew Reba's washing off the line, and Reba was not at all pleased, and the clouds did not move an inch.

On the seventh day, he asked Great-Grandpappy Buck when the clouds would clear.

Buck stood at his porch rail in his cap and his spectacles and looked at Weather Ridge for a long time.

"I can't say, young Maverick," he said at last. "Weather Ridge isn't telling. Some winters the clouds sit for a week. Some winters for a fortnight. Nobody knows for certain sure."

"But somebody must know," said Maverick. "There must be a way to tell."

"There's a way to wait," said Buck. "That's about all there is, with clouds."

Maverick did not want a way to wait. He wanted a way to know. He stamped back down the porch steps, and up into the grey sky, and sat on the top of the tallest hickory by the creek, glaring at the clouds, with his feathers fluffed out crossly, until it got dark.

And as it got dark, sitting up there alone in the grey, he had an idea.

It was not a good idea. But it was a very big one.


Chapter Three — Above the Clouds

"I'm going to fly up above them," Maverick announced the next afternoon, on the bridge.

Everybody looked at him.

"Above what?" asked Brody.

"The clouds," said Maverick. "The stars are still there, aren't they? They're just on the other side. So if I fly up high enough, through the clouds and out of the top, I'll be able to see them. And I'll see if the clouds are going. And then I'll come down and tell everybody when Star Night is."

"At night?" said Benny.

"At dusk," said Maverick. "Just when the first stars come out."

Benny frowned. "You can't see anything inside a cloud," he said. "It's like being inside a bowl of porridge. And it'll be getting dark. How will you know which way is down?"

"I'm an eagle," said Maverick. "I always know which way is down."

"You landed in the thistles last week," said Barney helpfully.

"That was on purpose."

Sadie Belle looked worried. "Have you asked Grandpappy Buck?"

Maverick fluffed his feathers.

"I don't need to ask anybody," he said. "I'm the Star Guide. Finding the stars is my job."

And that evening, as the grey light began to fade and turn to blue, Maverick took off from the top of the tallest hickory and flew straight up into the clouds.

For a moment it was wonderful. He beat his great wings and rose, up and up, past the bare branches, over the roofs, and into the bottom of the grey.

And then the grey was everywhere.

It was cold. It was wet. It was thick and soft and white and grey all at once, so that he could not see his own wingtips. There was no up. There was no down. There was no Hollow below him and no sky above. There was only cloud, pressing damp against his feathers and dripping off the end of his beak, and the sound of his own wings, and the light going dimmer and dimmer around him.

Maverick flew on, and on. He thought he was going up. Then he was not sure. He turned, to try another way, and then he was not sure which way he had turned.

And for the first time in his whole life, high in the air, Maverick did not know where he was.

He did the only sensible thing he could think of. He stopped flapping, and held his wings out wide and still, and let himself glide down, slowly, slowly, through the grey, the way he had glided down a thousand times in daylight.

He came out of the bottom of the cloud much lower than he had expected, and much further away, over the dark, snowy fields beyond the Old Mill. And he landed, not very gracefully, in the soft top of the miller's haystack, and sank in up to his neck.

He sat there for a long time in the dark, with hay in his feathers, getting his breath back.

He had not found a single star.


Chapter Four — Grounded

By the time Maverick had climbed out of the haystack and walked back along the creek path in the dark, half of Hickory Hollow was out looking for him with lanterns.

Benny had told. He had watched Maverick fly up into the clouds, and waited, and when Maverick did not come down again, he had run all the way to Buck's porch. Now the grown-ups were out along the creek with lights, calling, and the youngsters were huddled on Buck's porch, wrapped in blankets, looking very frightened.

When Maverick came walking up the path, wet and bedraggled, with hay sticking out of his explorer's vest, Charlie burst into tears of relief, and Brody hugged him so hard that his feathers squeaked.

Great-Grandpappy Buck came down the porch steps slowly, with his lantern, and looked at Maverick for a long moment.

"Are you hurt?" he asked.

"No," said Maverick. "Just wet. And a bit lost. I wasn't lost for long."

"Well, thank goodness for that," said Buck. Then his voice went firm, which it hardly ever did. "Young Maverick. You flew up into a cloud, at dusk, on your own, without telling a grown-up. You frightened everybody half to death. Benny ran all the way here. The whole Hollow's out in the snow."

Maverick looked at his talons.

"There'll be no flying into clouds," said Buck. "And no flying after dark. Not this winter, and not until you're a good deal older. Eagles are made for the daytime, Maverick. You know that as well as I do."

"Yes, Grandpappy," said Maverick, in a very small voice.

Buck looked at him for a moment longer. Then his voice went gentle again.

"Now go inside and get dry by the fire," he said. "There's soup."

Maverick sat by the fire in Buck's kitchen for a long time, steaming gently, with a bowl of soup he did not eat. One by one, the grown-ups came back from searching, and their lanterns were put out, and the youngsters went home with their families. All except Sadie Belle and Brody, who stayed, and sat on either side of him on the hearth rug, and did not say anything.

At last Maverick spoke.

"Can I tell you something?" he said quietly. "A secret?"

They both nodded.

"I can't really see the stars," said Maverick. "Not properly. Not the little ones."

Sadie Belle looked at him in surprise. "But you're the Star Guide. You've got the sharpest eyes in the Hollow."

"In the daytime," said Maverick miserably. "In the daytime, I can see a beetle on a leaf from across the valley. But eagle eyes are made for the light. When it's dark, I see the big bright stars fine. But the little ones, the faint ones, like the Little Lanterns, they're just a sort of smudge to me. I've always just remembered where they are, from my mother showing me, and pointed at the right place, and said the names." He hung his head. "I've never told anybody. I'm the Star Guide. I'm supposed to be the best at it."


Chapter Five — Who Sees Best in the Dark

Brody did not say anything for a while. He was thinking, slowly, the way Brody thought about most things.

"That's not so bad," he said at last. "Remembering where they are is still knowing them."

"But Charlie wants to see the Little Lanterns," said Maverick. "Properly. It's her first Star Night. And if the clouds ever go, I'll be standing on that rail pointing at a smudge and telling her it's a cluster of stars, and I won't even be able to see it myself." He shook his head. "And anyway, the clouds are never going. I've tried staring at them, and blowing them, and flying through them, and nothing works."

"Maybe you can't make the clouds go," said Sadie Belle gently. "Nobody can. But maybe there's somebody who could help you with the seeing part. For when they do."

Maverick looked at her.

"Who sees best in the dark?" said Sadie Belle.

Maverick thought about it. Not owls. There were no owls among the friends any more. Not deer, really, though Sadie Belle saw better at dusk than most. Not beavers. Not bears.

And then he thought of somebody who walked home along the dark trails every evening without a lantern, and never once tripped, and always knew exactly where he was, and once said he could see a moth on a tree trunk by starlight.

"Rusty," he said slowly.

"Foxes have eyes for the night," said Sadie Belle. "My mother told me. They shine in the lamplight, like little green moons. That's because they've got a sort of mirror at the back, that catches every bit of light there is."

Maverick sat very still.

He did not like the idea. He did not like it at all. He was the Star Guide. He had always been the Star Guide. If he asked Rusty for help, then Rusty would know that he could not really see the little stars. And then everybody would know.

"I'd have to ask him," he said. "Out loud."

"Yes," said Sadie Belle.

"And tell him I can't see properly."

"Yes."

Maverick looked into the fire for a long time.

"That's harder than flying into a cloud," he said.

"I know," said Sadie Belle.

The next morning, Maverick found Rusty down by the beaver ponds, nosing about in the snow for something only a fox would find interesting. Maverick landed on the frozen bank beside him, rather more heavily than usual, and stood there, and opened his beak, and shut it, and opened it again.

"You're making faces," said Rusty.

"I need to ask you something," said Maverick. "It's quite difficult."

Rusty sat down in the snow with his white-tipped tail curled round his paws, and waited.

"Can you see the little stars?" said Maverick. "The faint ones? Like the Little Lanterns? Properly?"

Rusty looked at him, puzzled.

"Course," he said. "Clear as anything. Why?"

Maverick took a very big breath.

"Because I can't," he said. "Not really. And I want Charlie to see them properly on her first Star Night. And I can't show her, on my own." He looked at the snow. "So I need your help. Please."

Rusty did not laugh. He did not say I knew it, or fancy that, or the Star Guide can't see the stars. He just looked at Maverick for a moment, as if he were seeing him a little differently, and then grinned.

"Course I'll help," he said. "But you'll have to teach me the names. I can see them. I just don't know what they're called."

Maverick stared at him.

"You don't know the names?"

"Never needed them," said Rusty. "I just look. I call that bright one in the north Old Steady. And the little bunch of sparks, I call them the Fidgets, because they twinkle about so."

"The Fidgets," said Maverick faintly. "Those are the Little Lanterns. The first settlers named them."

"Little Lanterns," said Rusty, trying it out. "That's nicer, actually. Better than Fidgets."

And for the first time in nearly a fortnight, Maverick laughed.


Chapter Six — Still Cloudy

So they practised. Or they tried to.

The trouble was the clouds. Every evening Maverick and Rusty met on Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch at dusk, ready, and every evening the sky stayed grey and heavy and starless, and they went home again.

"What if it's cloudy all winter?" said Charlie, on the tenth evening, with her chin on the porch rail. "What if I never get a Star Night?"

Maverick looked at her small, disappointed face, and at the solid grey sky, and felt the old cross, helpless feeling rising up in him again. He wanted to fly up and tear the clouds apart with his talons. He wanted to shout at Weather Ridge.

Instead, he took a breath, and turned round, and looked at his friends.

"We can't move the clouds," he said slowly. "I've tried. Nobody can. But maybe we don't have to just sit and wait for them, either. Maybe there's a way to show Charlie the stars, even with the clouds there."

"How?" said Benny.

Maverick did not know. He looked at Benny, hopefully.

Benny frowned. He looked at the sky. He looked at the lamp in Buck's window. He looked at the old tin lantern hanging by the door, the one with tiny holes punched all round it in a pattern, so that when it was lit, little specks of light danced out over the porch.

"Specks of light," he said slowly. "Like stars."

"What?" said Barney.

"If we made a lantern," said Benny, and now his eyes were bright, "a big paper one, and pricked holes in it in the shapes of the stars, exactly where the stars are. And a grown-up put a lamp inside it, in a dark room. The light would come out of the holes and make little stars all over the ceiling. The Porch Star, and the Little Lanterns, and the Stepping Stones. In the right places."

"A star lantern," breathed Charlie.

"But I'd need to know exactly where every star goes," said Benny. "Every single one. Exactly."

Everybody looked at Maverick.

And Maverick, who could not see the little stars properly in the dark, but who had spent every clear winter night of his life looking at them and remembering exactly where his mother had pointed, smiled for the first time in ten days.

"I know where they all are," he said. "Every single one. I could draw them with my eyes shut."

Rusty grinned. "And I know where the little faint ones go," he said, "because I've seen them. All the ones you can't."

"So between you," said Benny, "you've got the whole sky."


Chapter Seven — The Star Lantern

They made it in Great-Grandpappy Buck's kitchen, at the big table, over three grey, snowy afternoons.

Maggie Mae found them a great sheet of thick, dark-blue paper, left over from something, and helped Benny bend a willow frame into a big round shape like a moon, and stitch the paper over it.

Then Maverick drew the stars.

He did it with a stub of white chalk held in his beak, with his eyes half closed, remembering. The Porch Star, here, alone, up at the top, where it always sits. The seven stars round it that the old folk called the Ladle. The Three Stepping Stones, in their perfect row. The two bright stars above and below them, like the edges of a doorway. And all the others, the ones with names and the ones without, every one in its place.

But he could not draw the faint ones. He had only ever seen them as a smudge.

So Rusty did those. He sat beside Maverick and closed his own eyes, and remembered walking home along the dark trails under a clear sky. "There," he said, pointing with his paw. "Six little ones, all close together, like sparks. That's the Little Lanterns. You've drawn a smudge. It's six. Some nights I can see seven." He pointed again. "And three really faint ones, under the Stepping Stones, in a little line. Like a sword hanging down. You've missed those."

"I've never seen those," said Maverick, astonished.

"They're there," said Rusty. "You have to look a bit to the side of them. Then they come out."

Then Brody took a thick darning needle and, very slowly and carefully, with his big gentle paws, pricked a hole through every chalk star. Big holes for the bright stars. Little holes for the faint ones. Tiny, tiny pinpricks for the very faintest, the ones only Rusty could see.

Charlie was not allowed to know what it was. She kept being sent out of the kitchen to fetch things that nobody needed.

Sadie Belle stitched a little cloth flap at the bottom where a lamp could go in.

Reba made star-shaped biscuits for afterwards, with honey, and ate only three of them.

And Beau, who could balance anything, practised carrying the finished star lantern round the kitchen on a pole, very smoothly, without a single wobble, until Great-Grandpappy Buck said that if he carried it round once more it would start to feel dizzy.

When it was finished, they looked at it in the grey daylight.

It did not look like much. It was a round, dark-blue paper ball with chalk marks and holes in it.

"Does it work?" asked Barney.

"Only one way to find out," said Benny.


Chapter Eight — Stars on the Ceiling

That evening, the clouds were as thick as ever. But nobody minded.

Everybody came to Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge at dusk, as if it were a real Star Night: all the friends, and Charlie, and the three littlest rabbits with their mother, and the miller and his wife. They crowded into Buck's big front room and sat on the floor in their blankets, and Charlie, who had been told only that there was a surprise, sat right at the front with her eyes as wide as saucers.

Beau carried in the star lantern on its pole and hung it from the hook in the middle of the ceiling.

Then Great-Grandpappy Buck lit his little oil lamp, the one with the glass chimney, and very carefully set it inside the cloth flap at the bottom of the lantern, so that it sat safe and steady in the middle.

And then the grown-ups blew out every other light in the room.

For a moment, it was dark.

And then the stars came out.

They came out all over the ceiling and the walls and the faces of everybody sitting there, hundreds and hundreds of little points of light, big and small and very small, scattered across the dark wooden ceiling of Buck's lodge exactly as they were scattered across the winter sky.

Nobody breathed.

Then Maverick climbed onto the back of Buck's rocking chair, and spread his wings, and began, very quietly this time, not in his grand voice at all.

"That one, there, all on its own. That's the Porch Star. It never moves. If you're ever lost, you find that one, and it'll bring you home."

"And those seven, round it, like a ladle," said Rusty, beside him. "That's the Ladle."

"And those three, in a row," said Maverick. "The Three Stepping Stones."

"And the little line of faint ones underneath," said Rusty. "Like a sword. You have to look a bit to the side of them."

Everybody looked a bit to the side. And there they were.

"And that," said Maverick, pointing to a tiny cluster of pinpricks near the edge, "is the Little Lanterns. I always used to think it was just a smudge. But Rusty says it's six stars. Sometimes seven."

Charlie stared at the six tiny points of light. Then she counted them on her paws, twice.

"It's six," she whispered. "I can see six."

"Then you can see better than me," said Maverick. And he said it without minding at all.

The lamp burned steadily inside the lantern, and the stars glowed on the ceiling, and Maverick and Rusty took turns, one remembering the names and one finding the faint ones, until every star in the paper sky had been shown.


Chapter Nine — The Real Thing

It was Brody who noticed.

He had got up to fetch more cordial from the kitchen, and on the way he passed the window, and he stopped.

"Everybody," he said in a strange, hushed voice. "Look."

They looked.

Outside the window, over Silver Creek, the clouds were moving. They were not tearing apart or blowing away. They were simply, slowly, quietly thinning, the way mist thins on a morning creek, going from grey to pale to almost nothing. And through the thin places, one at a time, came stars.

Real ones.

Nobody said a word. Then everybody moved at once.

The grown-ups put out the lamp in the star lantern and lit the porch lantern instead. Blankets were gathered up. Mugs were filled. And the whole lot of them went out onto Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, into the sharp, clean, freezing air, and looked up.

The clouds had gone. The whole sky over Hickory Hollow had opened up, from Sunrise Ridge to Weather Ridge, deep and black and clear, and it was so full of stars that it seemed hardly any dark was left between them.

Buck turned the porch lantern down to a tiny glow, so as not to spoil the dark.

"Now," whispered Charlie. "Show me. Show me the real ones."

Maverick stood on the porch rail. He looked up. He could see the big ones, bright and clear: the Porch Star, the Ladle, the Three Stepping Stones rising over Sunrise Ridge in their perfect row. He pointed to each one in turn, and said its name.

Then he turned to Rusty.

"The Little Lanterns," he said. "Can you see them?"

Rusty looked up, a little to the side, the way he had said, his eyes catching the faint glow of the porch lantern and shining back like two small green moons.

"There," he said, and pointed. "Up and to the left of the Stepping Stones. Six. No. Seven. Seven tonight."

Charlie looked where his paw pointed.

And there they were. Not on a ceiling this time. Not pinpricks in paper. A tiny, misty, sparkling handful of real stars, far away in the real winter sky, like sparks thrown up from a fire very long ago.

"Oh," breathed Charlie. "Oh. The Little Lanterns."

Maverick could not see all seven. He could see a soft, shining smudge. But he looked at Charlie's face, lit up in the starlight, staring at them, and found that he did not mind one bit.

It was, he thought, the best Star Night there had ever been.


Chapter Ten — The Old Owl

Later, when the littlest rabbits had fallen asleep in their mother's arms and been carried home, and the miller and his wife had gone off along the creek path with their lantern, the friends stayed on Buck's porch, wrapped in blankets, looking up.

Nobody wanted to go in. The sky was too beautiful.

Great-Grandpappy Buck sat in his rocking chair with his old banjo across his knees, not playing it, just resting his paws on the strings, looking up with everybody else.

"Grandpappy," said Maverick, after a long while. "I'm sorry about the cloud. Flying into it. Without asking."

"I know you are," said Buck.

"I just didn't want to wait," said Maverick. "And I didn't want to ask anybody. Because I'm the Star Guide, and the Star Guide's supposed to know." He looked at Rusty, curled up on the boards beside him with his eyes half shut. "But I didn't know. Not all of it. And when I asked, it turned out somebody else knew the bit I didn't."

Buck rocked slowly.

"There's an old saying," he said, "from long before your time, or mine. My grandmother used to say it to me when I was a young beaver who wanted to go everywhere and ask nobody." He smiled, up at the stars. "Ask the old owl before you fly the new trail."

There was a pause.

"Even if the old owl's a fox?" said Maverick.

Buck chuckled softly.

"Especially then," he said.

He began, very gently, to play. It was a quiet tune, slow and silvery, like starlight on snow. And as he played, the friends went on lying on the porch in their blankets, looking up.

Sadie Belle found the Porch Star, all by herself, and said so, quietly. Benny counted the stars in the Ladle, twice, to be sure. Barney said that the Three Stepping Stones were obviously for giants, and somewhere there must be a giant river they went across. Reba wondered whether the Little Lanterns might be somebody's biscuits, very far away. Brody found the faint sword under the Stepping Stones, by looking a bit to the side, and was so pleased that he hummed along with Buck's banjo without noticing he was doing it.

Charlie lay with her head on Maggie Mae's arm and counted the Little Lanterns, over and over. Six. Seven. Six. Seven.

And Maverick sat on the porch rail with his feathers fluffed out against the cold and looked up at the sky he could only half see, and listened to Rusty, beside him, very quietly naming every faint star that Maverick could not, one by one, while Maverick told him the names.

Up in the deep winter dark, over the sleeping Hollow, the Porch Star sat where it always sat, in the north, not moving, the way it had sat for every creature who had ever looked up from this valley on a cold, clear night and wanted to find their way home.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
