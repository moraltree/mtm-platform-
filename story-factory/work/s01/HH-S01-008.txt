Chapter One — The Week Before Harvest Home

The leaves were coming down.

All through the last week of autumn they came, red and gold and copper and brown, drifting down out of the hickories and the maples and spinning slowly onto Silver Creek, until the water was so thick with them that it looked like a river of little boats. The mornings were sharp and white with frost. The evenings came early, blue and cold, and smelled of woodsmoke. And the whole of Hickory Hollow knew, without anybody having to say it, that Harvest Home was coming.

Harvest Home was the greatest festival of the Hollow's year. When the harvest was all in and stored and the firewood was stacked, the whole valley gathered in Fern Meadow and the Great Barn for a feast, and music, and dancing. And when the feasting was done and the dark had come down, there was the going-round: the whole Hollow sitting together in a great circle, and every creature, from the oldest to the smallest, saying in turn what they were thankful for.

And every creature who spoke had a lantern.

That was the old custom. Each one made a lantern in the week before, out of thin paper and willow sticks, and on the paper they painted the thing they were most thankful for that year. At the going-round, the grown-ups lit a candle inside each one, very carefully, and as each creature said their thanks, their lantern was hung on a long line strung across the meadow, until by the end of the evening there was a whole glowing river of lanterns above everybody's heads, each one a different little picture of gladness.

"I know exactly what I'm going to paint," said Charlie.

She was sitting on the kitchen floor at Rose Arch Cottage, surrounded by sheets of thin cream paper, pots of paint, bundles of willow sticks, and a great deal of glue, most of which was on Charlie. Maggie Mae had invited everybody round to make their lanterns together, the way she did every year, and her cottage was full.

"What?" asked Brody.

"It's a secret," said Charlie. "Until the going-round. But it's got a bow on it."

Everybody looked at Maggie Mae, who had a pink polka-dot bow, and Maggie Mae pretended very hard that she had not noticed.

Benny had already built three lantern frames out of bent willow, and was measuring them to make sure they were all exactly the same. Barney had built one frame that was not the same as anything, and had a sort of tower on top. Reba was painting a basket of nuts, with every nut a different colour. Sadie Belle was stitching the edges of her paper with tiny careful stitches so that it would not tear. Rusty had found the best, thinnest birch bark in the whole wood to use instead of paper, and would not say where. Beau was painting something with a great many legs that might have been a deer and might have been a table. Maverick had painted a mountain, very large, with an eagle on top of it, quite small, so that nobody could say he was showing off.

"Is that you?" asked Barney, pointing at the eagle.

"It's an eagle," said Maverick. "Any eagle. Could be anybody."

"It's got a red neckerchief."

"Lots of eagles have red neckerchiefs," said Maverick airily.

Charlie dipped her brush in pink paint and looked round the warm, crowded, glue-smelling kitchen, at all her friends bent over their lanterns, and wriggled with happiness.

Then she noticed that somebody was missing.


Chapter Two — The Lantern Nobody Made

Great-Grandpappy Buck was sitting in the big chair by Maggie Mae's fire, with a cup of hot cordial in his paws and his spectacles on his nose, watching them all with a look of great contentment.

He did not have any paper. He did not have any willow sticks. He did not have any paint.

"Grandpappy," said Charlie. "Where's your lantern?"

Buck smiled.

"I don't think I'll make one this year, little one," he said.

The kitchen went quiet. Several brushes stopped in mid-air.

"But you always make one," said Brody. "You always say your thanks. You always say it last, and it's always the best one."

"Ah, well," said Buck. He held up one old grey paw and flexed it, slowly. The fingers did not quite straighten all the way. "These old paws are better at banjo than they are at paper these days. All that folding and fiddling and tying of tiny knots. I made a terrible mess of it last year, and it took me three days, and it fell to pieces on the line." He chuckled. "I'll say my thanks at the going-round, same as always. I just won't have a lantern to hang. That's all right. It's only paper."

He said it lightly, the way Buck said most things. And he went back to his cordial, and began asking Beau whether his painting was a deer or a table.

But Charlie did not go back to her painting.

She sat very still on the kitchen floor, with pink paint dripping off the end of her brush, and looked at Buck's old paw resting on the arm of the chair.

Every year, for as long as Charlie could remember, Buck's lantern had hung at the very end of the line, the last one, after everybody else's. It was always a picture of his porch. And every year, when he hung it, the going-round was finished, and everybody knew that Harvest Home had properly ended, and it was time for bed.

A line without Buck's lantern at the end of it would not feel finished at all.

Charlie looked round the kitchen. Benny was looking at Buck's paw too, with a small worried frown. So was Sadie Belle. So was Maggie Mae.

Charlie caught Benny's eye. Then Maggie Mae's. Then Brody's.

Nobody said anything out loud. But by the time Buck had finished his cordial and gone home to his lodge, slowly, with his walking-stick, every youngster in that kitchen was thinking exactly the same thing.

"We could make it," said Charlie, the moment the door closed behind him. "His lantern. For him. In secret."

"A surprise," said Barney, delighted.

"With a picture of his porch on it," said Brody. "Like always."

"But better," said Reba. "With all of us on it. On the porch."

"He'll be so surprised," breathed Sadie Belle.

Benny was already reaching for a fresh bundle of willow sticks.

"It'll have to be the best lantern anybody ever made," he said seriously. "Measured perfectly."

"And fun," said Barney.

"And measured perfectly," said Benny, "and fun."


Chapter Three — Everybody's Bit

They made it in secret, in Maggie Mae's cottage, every evening that week, after they had finished their own lanterns.

It turned out that a lantern for Great-Grandpappy Buck needed everybody.

Benny built the frame. He did not use ordinary willow sticks. He used the very straightest, springiest ones he could find, and he bent them into a shape that nobody had made before: not a plain box, but a little house, with a pointed roof and a porch on the front, just like Buck's lodge. He measured every stick twice. Then, because it was for Buck, he measured them a third time.

Barney decorated the roof. He made tiny shingles out of bark curls, and a chimney out of a hollow twig, with a wisp of grey wool coming out of the top for smoke. Nobody had asked him to. It was the best part.

Sadie Belle stitched the paper onto the frame, so carefully and with such tiny stitches that you could hardly see them, so that it would be strong enough not to tear in the wind.

Rusty brought birch bark for the porch, the thinnest and finest he had ever found, which glowed a warm, creamy gold when you held a lamp behind it. He still would not say where.

Reba painted the windows. She painted them yellow, as if there were lamps lit inside, and she painted the curtains, and a tiny pie cooling on the windowsill, because she said every proper home should have a pie on the windowsill.

Maverick painted the sky round it, deep blue, with stars. He had the steadiest wing for tiny dots.

Beau painted the creek running in front of the porch, with silver ripples. It was the best thing he had painted all week, and he knew it, and he kept stepping back to look at it.

Maggie Mae painted the flowers round the porch, little pink and yellow ones, all along the front.

And Brody and Charlie painted the most important part.

They painted the porch. Buck's rocking chair, with Buck in it, in his cap and his spectacles, with his banjo on his knee. And all round him, sitting on the steps and the boards and the rail, they painted everybody. Benny with his pencil. Barney with his grin. Reba with a nut. Rusty with his white-tipped tail. Sadie Belle with her headband. Beau with his blue eyes. Maverick on the rail. Maggie Mae with her bow. Brody with his banjo. And Charlie, very small, right in the middle, sitting on Buck's knee.

It took them all week.

When it was finished, they stood round Maggie Mae's kitchen table and looked at it in silence.

It was not perfect. One of Beau's ripples went uphill. Barney's chimney was a little crooked. Maverick's stars had got rather crowded in one corner. But it was the most beautiful lantern any of them had ever seen.

"He's going to love it," whispered Charlie.

"He'll cry," said Barney. "I bet he cries. I bet you a whole hickory-nut cake he cries."

"I don't bet hickory-nut cakes," said Reba. "They're too important."


Chapter Four — Nearly Found Out

Keeping a secret from Great-Grandpappy Buck turned out to be the hardest part of all.

On Tuesday, he came round to Rose Arch Cottage unexpectedly, with a basket of apples for Maggie Mae. Nine youngsters threw themselves in front of the kitchen table at once, in a tangle of tails and paws and wings, and stood there smiling very hard.

"Well now," said Buck, looking at them over his spectacles. "You all look very pleased with yourselves."

"We're just standing," said Barney.

"Very close together," said Buck.

"It's cold," said Barney. "We're keeping warm."

Buck looked at the roaring fire, and then at Barney, and his whiskers twitched. But he only put the apples down and went away again, and nine youngsters let out a breath that they had been holding for some time.

On Wednesday, Charlie nearly told him by accident. He asked her what she was painting on her own lantern, and she said, "It's a surprise, like yours," and then clapped both paws over her mouth.

"Like mine?" said Buck.

"Like... like everybody's," said Charlie desperately. "All the lanterns are surprises. Till the going-round. That's the rule."

"So it is," said Buck mildly. "So it is."

On Thursday, Benny noticed that Buck had walked past Rose Arch Cottage three times in one afternoon, very slowly, looking at nothing in particular.

"Do you think he knows?" said Benny anxiously.

"He can't know," said Maggie Mae. "We've been so careful."

"Grandpappy Buck knows everything," said Brody. "Mostly."

"Then we'll just have to be extra careful," said Charlie.

So on Friday, the night before Harvest Home, they wrapped the lantern in a soft cloth, and put it in a big hat box that Maggie Mae had, with a lid, and tied the box with a ribbon. And they decided that Barney would carry it to the meadow the next day, in the middle of all the bustle, because nobody would ever suspect a box that Barney was carrying of containing anything important.

"Why not?" asked Barney.

"Because you're usually carrying a frog," said Benny.

"Only sometimes," said Barney, wounded.

He promised to be very, very careful with it.

He meant it with all his heart.


Chapter Five — Harvest Home

Harvest Home came in golden.

It was a perfect day: cold and clear and bright, with a sky so blue it looked polished, and the last leaves blazing on the hickories round Fern Meadow. By the middle of the afternoon the whole Hollow had come.

There were long tables in front of the Great Barn, groaning with food: hickory-nut cake and cornbread and apple pies and pumpkin soup and baked potatoes and honey, and a great copper kettle of hot spiced berry-cordial steaming over a fire that the grown-ups tended. There was a fiddler from up the creek, and the miller with his spoons, and Great-Grandpappy Buck with his banjo. There was dancing on the grass, and games for the youngsters, and so much noise and laughing and good smell that the whole valley seemed to hum with it.

And across the meadow, from the corner of the Great Barn to the tall old hickory on the far side, the lantern line had been strung, high up, ready.

All the lanterns were waiting in a row on a long table under the barn roof, unlit. Every youngster had brought theirs. Charlie's had a pink bow on it. Brody's had a banjo. Rusty's birch-bark one had a fox curled up asleep on it, in front of a fire, which everybody said was the nicest one there. And under the very end of the table, tucked out of sight in its box with the ribbon, sat Buck's.

Barney had carried it all the way across the meadow without so much as a wobble. He was very proud.

"I told you I'd be careful," he said.

"You were," said Benny. "You really were."

As the afternoon went on, the light began to turn gold, and then orange, and the sun slid down towards Weather Ridge. And as it did, the wind began to rise.

It came up the valley slowly at first, just a breeze, setting the last leaves spinning down off the trees. Then it grew. It came in gusts, cold and sharp, flapping the tablecloths and setting the lantern line swinging. The fiddler's music sheets blew away and had to be chased. Maggie Mae's bow came loose and had to be retied.

"Bring the lanterns further in," called the miller's wife. "Under the barn roof, out of the wind, till it's time."

So everybody hurried to move the lanterns further back under the roof.

Barney ran to the end of the table to get Buck's box.

He picked it up. He turned, holding it tight.

And at that exact moment, the biggest gust of the whole day came roaring across Fern Meadow and straight in under the barn roof. It whipped the lid clean off the box. It snatched the cloth. And it took Buck's lantern, the little paper house with its porch and its painted friends, right out of the box and out of Barney's paws, and tumbled it over and over across the barn floor, and slammed it into the corner post with a terrible small crunch.


Chapter Six — Broken

For a moment, nobody moved.

Then Barney ran. He was there first. He knelt down beside the lantern in the corner and picked it up, very gently, with both paws.

It was broken.

Benny's beautiful frame had snapped in two places, so that one side of the little house had folded in. Barney's chimney had come off. The paper had torn right across the front, straight through the porch, so that half of the rocking chair was hanging loose. And a long jagged rip ran through the painted friends, right between Brody and Charlie.

Barney's face crumpled.

"I'm sorry," he whispered. "I'm so sorry. I was holding it so tight. I was being so careful. The wind just..."

Nobody said anything.

The others had come over now and were standing round him in a silent ring, looking at the broken lantern in Barney's paws. Charlie's eyes had filled with tears. Brody's ears had gone down. Benny was staring at his snapped frame.

"It's not your fault, Barney," said Sadie Belle softly. "It was the wind."

"It was me carrying it," said Barney. "I was the one carrying it. You all said nobody would suspect me. You should have let somebody else."

"Nobody could have held on to it in that gust," said Maggie Mae. "Not even Brody."

Barney did not answer. He just sat in the corner, holding the broken lantern, and looked at it.

Out in the meadow, the sun was nearly down. The grown-ups were beginning to light the little lamps on the long tables. The music had stopped. In a few minutes the going-round would begin.

"Can we fix it?" said Charlie, in a very small voice.

Benny knelt down and looked at the frame. He looked at it for a long time.

"There isn't time to build a new frame," he said. "Not properly. And the paper's torn right through. We'd need new paper. And new paint. And then it'd have to dry." He shook his head. "It took us a whole week."

"So he won't have a lantern," said Brody slowly. "After all that. The line won't have an end."

Charlie sat down on the barn floor and put her face in her paws.

It was very quiet in the corner of the barn, with the wind still gusting outside and the last of the light going out of the sky.

Then Sadie Belle, who had been looking not at the broken lantern but at the long table under the barn roof, at the row of their own lanterns, all finished and unbroken and waiting, said something very quietly.

"We don't need new paper," she said. "We've got paper."


Chapter Seven — Sadie Belle's Idea

Everybody looked at her.

"Our lanterns," said Sadie Belle. "We've all got one. We could each give a piece of ours. For the torn parts. And sticks for the frame."

There was a long pause.

It was not an easy thing to hear. Everybody had worked hard on their own lanterns. Everybody had painted the thing they were most thankful for. Charlie's pink bow. Brody's banjo. Rusty's sleeping fox, which everybody said was the nicest one there.

"But then ours would have holes in," said Reba. She did not say it unkindly. She said it slowly, as if she were trying the idea on to see whether it fitted. "In our thankful pictures."

"Yes," said Sadie Belle. "They would."

Reba looked at her own lantern, with its basket of rainbow nuts. She had spent three evenings on those nuts.

Then she looked at the broken little house in Barney's paws, with half of Buck's rocking chair hanging loose.

"I'll give the bottom bit of mine," she said. "Below the basket. There's lots of plain paper there. It'll be enough for the porch."

"I'll give my sky," said Maverick at once. "My mountain's got loads of sky round it. More than it needs. You can have a big piece of sky."

"I'll give the corner of mine," said Rusty. He paused. "Not the fox. But the corner."

"You can have all the edges of mine," said Sadie Belle. "I stitched them double. They're the strongest bits."

"I'll give two sticks," said Beau. "From the back, where nobody looks."

"And I'll give mine," said Charlie. She stood up and wiped her eyes. "The bow. You can have the bow. It can go on the front of the lodge. Like a wreath."

"Charlie, no," said Maggie Mae gently. "That's your thankful picture. That's what you're thankful for."

Charlie looked at her.

"I know," she said. "That's why it should go on his."

Maggie Mae did not say anything for a moment. Then she bent down and hugged Charlie very hard.

Barney had been listening to all of this with his eyes getting wider and wider. Now he looked down at the broken lantern in his paws, and then up at Benny.

"Can you fix the frame?" he asked. "If we give you sticks? Quickly? Not perfectly. Just enough?"

Benny looked at the snapped willow. For a moment his face went the way it always went when somebody asked him to do something not perfectly.

Then he took the pencil from behind his ear and put it away in his pocket.

"Not perfectly," he said. "Just enough. Pass me the sticks."


Chapter Eight — The Patchwork Lantern

They worked fast, in the corner of the Great Barn, with the wind roaring outside and the last of the light gone and the little table lamps glowing gold across the meadow.

Benny splinted the broken frame with Beau's two sticks, binding them tight with thread from Sadie Belle's sewing kit. He did not measure anything. He just looked, and bent, and tied, and looked again, and it was not perfect, but it stood up straight, and the little house had its shape back.

Sadie Belle cut her double-stitched edges off her own lantern and sewed them along the torn front of Buck's, so that the rip was held together by a row of tiny strong stitches, like a seam on a well-loved coat.

Maverick's piece of sky went over the gap in the roof. It was a slightly different blue from the rest. Nobody minded.

Reba's plain paper went behind the torn porch, so that Buck's rocking chair was whole again, though it now sat a little crooked. Rusty's corner patched the place where the creek had torn. Brody held everything steady with his big gentle paws while the others glued.

And Charlie's pink bow went on the front of the little house, right above the porch door, like a wreath.

Barney put the chimney back on last. It went on a little crooked. He straightened it. It went crooked again.

"Leave it," said Benny. "It looks like it's leaning to listen."

When it was done, they stepped back.

It did not look anything like the lantern they had made all week. It was patched all over, a piece of this lantern and a piece of that, odd bits of different papers and different paints, different blues and different stitches, a crooked chimney and a crooked chair and a bow where no bow had been before. You could see every single place where it had been broken, and every single place where somebody had given something to mend it.

They looked at it in silence. Then Charlie looked at her own lantern, sitting on the table with a hole in the middle where the bow had been.

"Mine's got a hole in," she said.

"So's mine," said Reba.

"So's mine," said Maverick, looking at the great gap in his sky.

Everybody's had a gap. A missing edge, a missing corner, a missing piece of sky. Nine lanterns with holes in, and one with every hole filled.

"It's all right," said Charlie slowly, as if she were only just finding it out. "The holes are where we put the pieces."

Outside, the miller's wife rang the little handbell, and the whole of Hickory Hollow began to gather in a great circle in the dark meadow, beneath the empty lantern line.

The going-round was beginning.


Chapter Nine — The Going-Round

The grown-ups lit the lanterns one by one.

They did it carefully, at the long table under the barn roof, out of the wind, each candle set in its little holder and lit with a long taper, so that no small paw had to go anywhere near a flame. Then each creature took their lit lantern on its long hooked pole and carried it out to the circle in the dark meadow, and said their thanks, and a grown-up hooked it onto the line.

The miller was thankful for a good dry harvest. The rabbit grandmother was thankful for her nine grandchildren, all of them, even the noisy ones. Maggie Mae was thankful for friends who came to her parties at all the wrong times. Reba was thankful for second chances, and everybody who knew about Nut Day smiled.

Benny was thankful for things that stood up straight, even when they had been splinted. Nobody else quite understood what he meant, but Barney did, and nudged him. Brody, holding up his banjo lantern with a corner missing, was thankful for tunes that came back to you when you had forgotten them. Rusty, whose sleeping fox now slept beside a gap, was thankful for the long way home, sometimes.

When Barney came up, he stood in the circle with his lantern for a long moment and did not say anything at all, which was so unlike Barney that the whole crowd leaned in.

"I'm thankful," he said at last, "that when you break something, your friends don't go away. They come and sit in the corner with you."

Then he went and stood next to Benny very quickly, before anybody could look at him too long.

When each youngster came up, the crowd could see that their lantern had a hole in it. A missing corner. A missing piece of sky. Nobody said anything. But people looked at each other, and wondered.

Charlie came up last of the youngsters. Her lantern had a big gap right in the middle, where the bow had been. She stood in the circle in the dark, with the light from it shining out through the hole and making a small round patch of gold on the grass.

"I'm thankful," she said, "for my friends. And for a lantern with a hole in it. Because the hole means a piece of it went somewhere better."

The crowd went very quiet.

Then the very last voice spoke, from the edge of the circle. It was Great-Grandpappy Buck, leaning on his walking-stick.

"Well now," he said. "I suppose it's my turn. And I've no lantern this year. But I'm thankful for..."

"Wait," said Charlie.

Barney came out of the dark, from behind the barn. He was carrying the patched lantern on its pole, very slowly and very carefully, lit from inside by its candle. The light shone out through Sadie Belle's stitches, and Maverick's blue sky, and Reba's yellow windows, and the little painted porch with all of them on it, and the pink bow above the door. You could see every patch. You could see every mend. It glowed like a little house with every lamp lit.

Barney held it out to Great-Grandpappy Buck.

"It's yours," he said. "We made it. All of us. And then it broke, because of me. And then we all fixed it." He swallowed. "It's not as good as it was."

Buck did not take it straight away.

He stood in the middle of the dark meadow, looking at the patched lantern glowing in front of him, at the little painted porch, at all their little painted faces, at Charlie's bow, at the crooked chimney leaning to listen. He looked at it for a long, long time.

Then he took off his spectacles and wiped them, very slowly, on a big spotted handkerchief.

"Not as good as it was?" he said, and his old voice was not quite steady. "Barney. It's better."

Barney looked up.

Then Buck took the lantern on its pole in his own paws, and turned to the circle, and said his thanks.

"I'm thankful," he said, "for this."

And a grown-up reached up and hooked it onto the very end of the line, where Buck's lantern had always hung, and the line was finished, and Harvest Home was done.

Charlie looked at Barney.

Barney looked at Charlie.

"He cried," whispered Barney. "I said he'd cry."

"You owe somebody a hickory-nut cake," whispered Charlie.

"I didn't bet one," said Barney. "Reba wouldn't let me."


Chapter Ten — Odd Scraps

Late that night, when the feast was eaten and the fiddler had gone home and the grown-ups had blown out every candle on the line and carried the lanterns safely back under the barn roof, the youngsters walked Great-Grandpappy Buck home along the creek path.

He carried the patched lantern himself, unlit now, cradled carefully in one arm, with his walking-stick in the other paw. Brody walked on one side of him in case he stumbled. Charlie rode on Brody's shoulders.

On his porch, Buck hung the patched lantern from the hook by the door, beside his own lamp. Then he lit the lamp, and its light shone through the patched paper from behind, and the little house glowed again, all its mends showing.

He settled into his old hickory rocking chair. It gave its first slow creak, and the porch went quiet.

For a while, he did not say anything at all. He just looked at the lantern, swaying very gently in the night breeze.

"Do you know what that reminds me of?" he said at last.

Nobody did.

He reached over the back of his chair and drew forward Bramble's old patchwork quilt, the one that always lay there, and spread it across his knees. In the lamplight, all its patches showed: a square of blue, a square of red check, a scrap of yellow flowers, a piece of faded green, a bit of somebody's old coat, a corner of somebody's old shirt, hundreds of odd little pieces from a hundred different places, all stitched together into one warm whole.

"Bramble made this from scraps," said Buck. "Every one of them came from something else. Something worn out, or torn, or outgrown, or given. Not one piece matches another. And it's the warmest thing I own."

He looked up at the lantern, and then round at all of them, sitting on his steps and his boards in the lamplight, every one of them carrying a lantern at home with a little hole in it.

"A quilt's made of odd scraps, young'uns," he said softly. "And there's nothing warmer in the world."

He reached for his banjo, and began to play.

It was a slow tune, soft and patched together, a little bit of one old song and a little bit of another. And on the hook by the door, the lantern swayed in the dark, glowing through all its mends: Sadie Belle's stitches and Maverick's sky, Rusty's corner and Reba's porch, Beau's sticks and Benny's splints, Barney's leaning chimney, and Charlie's pink bow above the little painted door.

One by one, the youngsters' families came along the creek path with their lanterns to take them home. One by one, the friends said goodnight, and went.

Charlie went last. She stopped at the bottom of the porch steps and looked back up at the patched lantern glowing by Buck's door.

"Goodnight, Grandpappy," she said.

"Goodnight, little one," said Buck. "Thank you for my lantern."

Then the porch was quiet, except for the slow creak of the rocking chair, and the soft notes of the banjo, and the wind in the bare hickory branches, carrying the last of the autumn leaves away into the dark.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
