Chapter One — The Rule

There is a rule on Blackberry Bank, and everybody in Hickory Hollow knows it.

You pick more than you eat.

That is all. It is not a difficult rule. It is written on nothing and enforced by nobody. Every summer, on the great day of the berry-picking, the whole Hollow climbs the long sunny slope with baskets on their arms and comes home in the evening with purple paws, and everybody agrees that it is an excellent rule, and everybody cheerfully breaks it.

But nobody broke it quite like Reba.

On the morning of the berry-picking, in the middle of July, Reba stood at the bottom of Blackberry Bank with her basket on her arm and her nose in the air, and breathed in.

The whole bank smelled of blackberries. Sun-warmed, dark and sweet, like the inside of a pie before the lid goes on. The brambles hung so heavy with fruit that their branches drooped to the ground, and every bush was shining black and purple in the morning sun, and bees were bumbling among the last of the white flowers, and there were already wasps nosing at the ripest fruit.

"Oh," said Reba, very softly. "Oh, my."

She was a young raccoon, grey-brown, with a black mask and a ringed tail and a pink bow, and she loved three things more than anything else in the world: finding things out, her friends, and food. Not necessarily in that order.

"Remember the rule," said Maggie Mae, beside her, with her own basket over her arm. "Pick more than you eat. These are for the pies."

"Of course," said Reba. "Absolutely. More than I eat."

Maggie Mae was in charge of the pies. She was in charge of the pies every year. On the evening of the day after the berry-picking, the whole of Hickory Hollow sat down together at long tables in Fern Meadow for the Berry Supper. And the very heart of the Berry Supper, the thing everybody looked forward to all year, was Maggie Mae's blackberry pies. Twelve of them. Golden and crisp on top, with a pattern of leaves cut into the pastry, and dark purple juice bubbling up round the edges, and a crumbly sugary crust that Barney said was worth waiting a whole year for.

And this year, for the first time, Maggie Mae had asked Reba to help.

"You've got the best nose in the Hollow," she had said. "You'll know which berries are just ripe. And you're good in a kitchen."

Reba had nearly burst with pride.

Now she stood at the bottom of Blackberry Bank, with her basket and her nose, and looked up at the thousands and thousands of shining berries.

"Twelve pies," she said. "How many berries is that?"

"A lot," said Maggie Mae. "Four big baskets full. At least."

"Easy," said Reba.

And she reached out, and picked her first blackberry of the year, and looked at it, plump and dark and shining in her paw.

And ate it.


Chapter Two — One for the Basket

It was a perfect blackberry.

It was warm from the sun, and so ripe that it almost burst on its own, and so sweet that it made her eyes close.

"That one was just to test," she said, with her mouth full. "To see if they're ready. They're ready."

She picked another one. She put it in her basket.

She picked another one. She looked at it. It was even bigger and darker and shinier than the first. It seemed a shame to put it in the basket, where it would only get squashed by the others. She ate it.

"Just checking that one too," she said.

All along the bank, the folk of Hickory Hollow were picking. The grown-ups picked steadily, in long rows, filling their baskets.

Benny was picking with great care and counting every berry. Barney was picking with great speed and dropping half of them. Brody was picking the berries off the very top of the tallest brambles that nobody else could reach. Charlie was picking the low ones, under the leaves, where nobody else could see. Sadie Belle was picking slowly and gracefully, one at a time, with her soft mouth, and putting every single one into her basket, because Sadie Belle never broke a rule in her life.

And Reba was picking too. One for the basket. One for Reba. One for Reba. One for the basket. One for Reba. One for Reba. One for Reba.

By the middle of the morning, her basket had a small heap of blackberries in the bottom of it. Her face had gone purple. Her paws had gone purple. Her pink bow had a purple spot on it. And there was a little ring of purple all round her mouth, so that her black mask looked as if it had been drawn on with blackberry juice.

Maggie Mae came along the row, with her basket nearly full, and looked into Reba's.

Then she looked at Reba's face.

"Reba," she said.

"I've been testing," said Reba. "For ripeness." She heard how that sounded, and her ears went a little pink under the purple. "Quite a lot of testing," she admitted. "Very thorough testing. You did say I had the best nose."

"I did," said Maggie Mae. "I didn't say you had the biggest tummy."

Reba looked down at her basket. Then she looked at Maggie Mae's basket, which was so full that the berries were piled up in a shining heap above the rim.

"I'll do better," she said. "After lunch. I promise. More than I eat."

And after lunch, she did do better. A little. She managed one for the basket, one for Reba, one for the basket, for nearly a quarter of an hour.

By the evening, when the whole Hollow came down off Blackberry Bank in a long, tired, happy, purple line, Maggie Mae's baskets were full: the one on her arm, and the three she had filled already and left in the shade at the bottom of the bank. All four of them, which was enough for twelve pies, just. And Reba's basket was about a third full, and Reba was so full herself that she had to walk home very slowly, holding her tummy, making small groaning noises.

"Never again," she said, at the bottom of the bank.

"You say that every year," said Rusty.

"This year I mean it," said Reba, and groaned.


Chapter Three — Guarding the Bowl

The next morning was the morning of the baking.

Reba arrived at Rose Arch Cottage bright and early, with her neckerchief tied back out of the way, and her purple paws scrubbed nearly clean, and her tummy feeling very much better.

Maggie Mae's kitchen was ready. The table was covered with the Berry Supper cloth, white, with a lace edge all the way round, which came out of the drawer once a year for the pie-making and never at any other time, because, Maggie Mae said, it made her bake better. On the tablecloth stood the great big blue mixing bowl, the biggest bowl in the Hollow, and in it, washed and drained, glistening and dark, were all the blackberries for the twelve pies. Every one of them. Heaped up to the brim.

Reba stood in the doorway and looked at the bowl.

The kitchen smelled of blackberries.

"Right," said Maggie Mae, rolling up her sleeves. "I've made the pastry for six. It's resting in the cool. Now I've got to run to the Mill for more butter, for the other six, and then we'll put them together, and the miller's wife's going to bake them in the big Mill oven this afternoon, because mine only holds two." She picked up her basket. "Reba, will you stay here and guard the berries?"

"Guard them?" said Reba.

"From flies," said Maggie Mae. "And wasps. Keep the cloth over them. Shoo anything off. I'll be half an hour."

"Half an hour," said Reba. "Guard the berries. Absolutely."

Maggie Mae went out of the door, and down the path, and away along the lane towards the Mill.

Reba stood alone in the kitchen with the big blue bowl.

She put the cloth over it, carefully, as she had been told. She sat down on the stool beside the table, and folded her paws in her lap, and looked at the cloth.

A wasp came in at the open door.

Reba sat up very straight. Here, at last, was something to guard against. The wasp flew once round the kitchen, and landed on the cloth, right over the berries, and walked about on it, sniffing, as if it knew perfectly well what was underneath.

Reba did not swat it, because you never swat a wasp, everybody knows that. And she did not flap at it either. Instead she kept very, very still. Then she slid off the stool, slowly, and opened the window as wide as it would go, and stood well back by the dresser, and waited. The wasp walked about a little more. Then it found nothing it could get at, and lifted off, and drifted out of the open window into the sunshine. Reba shut the window behind it, and did up the little latch on the inside, and sat back down on her stool, extremely pleased with herself.

"Guarded," she said.

The cloth was white. Underneath it, she knew, there were thousands of blackberries.

She could smell them through the cloth.

She looked at the clock on the wall. One minute had gone.

She looked at the cloth.

She lifted one corner of it, just to check that no flies had got under. No flies had got under. There were the blackberries, dark and glistening, heaped up to the brim.

She put the corner of the cloth down.

She looked at the clock. Two minutes.

She lifted the corner of the cloth again.

"Just one," she said. "Just to check they're still ripe."

They were still ripe.


Chapter Four — Just One More

It is very difficult to eat just one blackberry from a bowl of thousands. Reba discovered this almost at once.

After she had eaten one, the bowl looked exactly the same. So it did not seem to matter very much if she ate another. And after the second, the bowl still looked exactly the same. And after the third, and the fourth, and the fifth.

"These are for the pies," she told herself firmly, and ate a sixth.

By the time ten minutes had gone, there was a small dip in the top of the heap.

Reba looked at the dip. Then she took some berries from the edges and piled them up in the middle, so that the dip went away, and the heap looked like a heap again.

"There," she said. "Nobody would ever know."

She ate three more from the edge.

By the time twenty minutes had gone, the heap was not a heap any more. It was a sort of flat purple field, a good way below the rim of the bowl, and no amount of piling-up would make it look like a heap again.

Reba looked at it in horror.

Then she had an idea.

She went out into Maggie Mae's garden, and picked a great handful of big green leaves from the cabbages, and came back, and stuffed them into the bottom of the bowl, under the berries, so that the berries were lifted up again, nearly to the brim.

"There," she said, a little shakily. "Cabbage. Nobody would ever know."

But it was all lumpy. It looked like a bowl of blackberries with something hiding underneath it. Which it was.

Reba stood on her stool, and looked down into the bowl, and her tummy gave a guilty little turn.

And then she saw it. Right at the very far side of the bowl, on the very top, an enormous blackberry. It was as big as a robin's egg. It was so dark it was nearly black, and so ripe that it shone like the inside of a puddle. It was the most perfect blackberry in all of Hickory Hollow, and it was right at the far edge of the bowl, just out of reach.

"Just that one," whispered Reba. "That's the last one. That's the very last one. And then I'll stop. I promise."

She leaned over the bowl.

She stretched.

She stood on tiptoe on the stool.

She leaned further.

Her claws were just touching the giant blackberry when the stool went one way, and Reba went the other, and her paw caught the edge of the great big blue mixing bowl as she went.

And the bowl tipped.

Slowly, slowly, as if it were thinking about it, the biggest bowl in Hickory Hollow tipped over on its side. It rolled off the edge of the table, and landed on the floor with a tremendous clang. And every single blackberry in it went everywhere. Across the table. Across the white Berry Supper cloth. Across the floor, in a great purple tide, under the dresser, under the sink, into the corners. Onto the cushions of the chairs.

And onto Reba, who had landed on her bottom in the middle of the floor, and was sitting in a purple puddle of squashed blackberries, not hurt a bit, only very purple, with cabbage leaves on her head.


Chapter Five — The Squirrel

For a while, Reba did not move.

She sat in the purple puddle, with the cabbage leaves on her head, and looked round at Maggie Mae's kitchen.

It was purple. Everything was purple. The floor was purple. The cushions were purple. And the Berry Supper cloth, the one that made Maggie Mae bake better, was not white any more. It had a great dark purple stain right across the middle of it, spreading slowly outwards, through all the delicate little holes in the lace.

The giant blackberry, the one she had been reaching for, was lying by her foot. It had been squashed flat.

And at that exact moment, the front door opened, and Maggie Mae came in with the butter.

She stopped in the doorway.

She looked at the floor. She looked at the table. She looked at the tablecloth. She looked at the empty blue bowl, lying on its side by the dresser. And then she looked at Reba, sitting in the middle of it all, purple from ears to tail, with cabbage leaves on her head.

"Reba," she said, very quietly. "What happened?"

And Reba, who was so worried about what Maggie Mae would say that she could hardly think, opened her mouth and said the first thing that came into it.

"A squirrel," she said.

Maggie Mae looked at her.

"A squirrel," said Reba again, faster. "Came in. Through the window. Jumped on the table. Knocked the bowl over. I tried to stop it. It was a very big squirrel. And then it ran off."

Maggie Mae stood in the doorway, with the butter in her paw, and looked at Reba.

She looked at the purple ring round Reba's mouth. At the purple on her paws. At the purple on her whiskers, and her chin, and her bow. At the cabbage leaves on her head. At the closed window, with its little latch firmly done up on the inside.

Reba looked at the window too.

And then her face crumpled.

"There wasn't a squirrel," she said, in a very small voice. "I made it up. It was me. I ate the berries. I was supposed to guard them, and I ate them, lots of them, and then I tried to hide it with cabbage leaves, and then I reached for the biggest one, and I fell off the stool, and the bowl went over." Her eyes filled up. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I've ruined your Berry Supper cloth. And the pies. And the Berry Supper. And everything." She put her purple paws over her purple face. "And then I told a fib about it. Which is the worst bit."

Somewhere under the dresser, a blackberry rolled slowly to a stop.

Then Maggie Mae put the butter down on the dresser, very carefully. She walked across the purple floor, squelching, and knelt down in the purple puddle beside Reba, and took the cabbage leaves off her head.

"Thank you," she said quietly, "for telling me the truth."


Chapter Six — What's Left

They sat on the kitchen floor together for a while.

Then Maggie Mae stood up, and looked round her kitchen, and pushed up her sleeves.

"Right," she said. "First things first."

She lifted the purple cloth off the table, bundled it up, and pushed it straight down into the basin of cold washing-up water that was standing in the sink.

"Cold water," she said. "Straight away. Never hot. Hot water can set berry juice in, but cold water, if you catch it while it's fresh, starts drawing it out." She pressed the cloth down under the water, and a purple cloud came swirling up out of it. "That can soak while we work. Now. Let's see what's left."

They went round the kitchen together, on their paws and knees, and picked up every blackberry that was still whole. The ones on the table were all right, mostly. The ones that had rolled onto the clean flagstones by the door were all right. The ones in the purple puddle, and under the dresser, and on the cushions, were not.

When they had finished, there were enough good blackberries for four pies.

Not twelve. Four.

Reba looked at the four-pie heap in the bottom of the big blue bowl, and her lip wobbled.

"The Berry Supper's tonight," she said. "Everybody's coming. The whole Hollow. And there'll only be four pies. And it's my fault."

Maggie Mae looked at the bowl too.

"There's still berries on Blackberry Bank," she said slowly. "Lots. We only picked the bottom half yesterday."

Reba looked up.

"But the pastry," she said. "And the oven. The miller's wife's baking them this afternoon."

"The pastry will keep in the cool for a few hours," said Maggie Mae. "And the Mill oven's big. If we had the berries by the middle of the afternoon..." She looked at Reba. "But it's a lot of picking. Eight pies' worth. Three baskets, at least. Just us two, it would take till dark."

Reba thought about it. She thought about the long, hot, sunny slope of Blackberry Bank, and the thousands and thousands of shining, sun-warm, perfect berries.

And she thought about what she would have to do. Not eat any.

Not one.

"I'll go," she said. "I'll pick them. All of them." She stood up, purple and sticky and determined. "But I can't do three baskets on my own by the middle of the afternoon. Nobody could."

She looked out of the kitchen window, towards the lane, and the meadow, and the Hollow beyond.

"I'll have to ask for help," she said.


Chapter Seven — The Second Picking

Reba went round the Hollow, purple from ears to tail, and knocked on every door she could think of, and said the same thing at every one.

"I've done something silly," she said. "I ate the pie berries, and I knocked the bowl over, and there's only enough for four pies, and the Berry Supper's tonight, and it's my fault. Will you help me pick some more?"

It was the hardest thing she had ever said. She said it eight times.

And every single time, the answer was yes.

By eleven o'clock, there was a whole crowd at the bottom of Blackberry Bank, with baskets. Benny and Barney. Brody. Charlie. Sadie Belle. Rusty and Beau. Maverick, who said he could not pick, but could spot the best bushes from above. And Reba's mother, who had come to keep an eye on everybody near the brambles, and to bring a big jug of water, because it was going to be hot.

"Right," said Reba, standing at the front of them all, with her basket. "Thank you. All of you. And there's one rule today, and it's a different rule. It's for me. I'm not eating any. Not one. All of mine go in the basket."

Everybody looked at her.

"Not one?" said Barney, astonished. "On Blackberry Bank?"

"Not one," said Reba.

"You won't manage it," said Rusty, kindly.

"I know," said Reba. "So you have to help me."

So they did.

Maverick, sitting on a dead branch at the top of the bank, called out where the best bushes were. "Left a bit! No, your left! That one, by the stump, it's black all over!" He was right every time, and very pleased about it. Brody stretched up for the high ones, as he always did, and dropped them carefully into Reba's basket. Rusty found a patch round the back of the bank, in the shade, where the berries had stayed cool and nobody had picked at all the day before. And Beau kept going down to Reba's mother for the water jug, and bringing it back up, so that nobody got too hot.

It was Charlie who had the first idea. Every time Reba picked a berry, Charlie, who was picking the low ones beside her, sang out, "One for the basket!" And Reba put it in the basket. And picked another. "One for the basket!" And put it in.

It was Benny who had the second idea, which was to count, out loud, every berry that went into Reba's basket, so that she could hear the number going up, and it was much harder to eat a berry when everybody would hear the number stop.

And it was Barney who had the third idea, which was to stand right next to Reba and eat a blackberry very slowly and very loudly, with his eyes closed, saying "Mmmmm," so that she would know what she was missing. That was not a helpful idea, and Sadie Belle took him firmly by the ear and led him to a bush of his own at the far end of the row, and gave him a basket of his own to fill.

Reba picked. She picked and picked and picked. The sun was hot on her back, and the bank smelled so sweet she could hardly bear it, and the berries were so ripe that some of them fell into her paw by themselves, as if they wanted to be eaten.

"One for the basket," sang Charlie.

"Four hundred and twelve," said Benny.

Reba's paw went to her mouth, all by itself, with a berry in it. She stopped it, a whisker away from her nose. She looked at the berry. Her whole face scrunched up.

"One for the basket," she said, through her teeth, and put it in the basket.

Everybody cheered.


Chapter Eight — The Big Mill Oven

They had three baskets by half past two.

Three big baskets, heaped and shining. Reba's basket was the fullest of all. Benny said it had eight hundred and forty-three berries in it, and he had counted every one, and not one of them had gone into Reba's mouth on the way.

They carried the baskets down to Rose Arch Cottage in a long, tired, happy line, and Maggie Mae met them at the gate, and looked into the baskets, and then looked at Reba.

"All of them?" she said.

"All of them," said Reba. "Benny counted."

Maggie Mae only put her arms round Reba, purple and sticky as she was, and hugged her.

But before a single pie was made, there was the kitchen. It was still purple from one end to the other, and it was Reba's purple, so Reba did it. Her mother filled a bucket of soapy water at the pump and carried it in, and Reba got down on her knees with the scrubbing brush and scrubbed the flagstones, round and round, under the dresser and into every corner, until the floor was grey again instead of purple. Charlie and Sadie Belle sponged the chair cushions. Rusty wiped the dresser. And Reba scrubbed the last purple splash from under the sink herself, and sat back on her heels, and said, "There."

Then everybody helped make the pies.

Maggie Mae rolled the pastry, and cut out the leaves herself with her little knife, and then gave Charlie a fork, and showed her how to press the tines gently into each leaf to make the veins. Sadie Belle lined the pie dishes. Benny measured the sugar, very exactly. Brody carried the dishes. Barney washed the berries, and only ate four, which he said was a record. And Reba filled the pies, every one of them, heaping the berries up in the middle, dark and shining, just right.

She was heaping up the twelfth when Barney came and stood beside her. He had his own basket from the far end of the row, the one Sadie Belle had given him, and there was not much in it, because Barney had been Barney. But in the very bottom there was one blackberry. A big one. Dark and shining and perfect.

"I saved it," said Barney, a little gruffly. "For you. Because of the Mmmmm."

Reba looked at the blackberry for quite a while.

Then she took it, very carefully, and put it right on the top of the twelfth pie, in the very middle, where the pastry leaves would meet over it.

"That's the best place for it," she said.

Barney looked at the pie, and then at Reba, and for once in his life he could not think of a single thing to say.

Then they carried all twelve pies to the Old Mill, on trays, very carefully, in a long procession along the lane. And the miller, who was the only one allowed near the great hot oven, put them in, two rows of six, and shut the iron door, while the miller's wife sat in her big chair by the kitchen door and timed them on the big clock.

The smell, when they came out, was so wonderful that half the Hollow came to the Mill to see what it was.

Twelve pies. Golden and crisp, with leaves on top, and dark purple juice bubbling up round the edges. They stood in two rows on the long Mill table to cool, and Reba stood beside them with her paws held tightly behind her back, just in case.


Chapter Nine — The Tablecloth

There was one thing left.

While the pies cooled on the Mill table, Reba and Maggie Mae walked back to Rose Arch Cottage, to the basin in the sink where the Berry Supper cloth had been soaking since the morning.

Reba lifted one corner of it out of the water. The great dark stain had gone a dull, murky purple, but it was still there, right across the middle.

"It's ruined," she said. "Isn't it."

Maggie Mae looked at it, and held it up to the window, dripping.

"Not yet," she said. "Now we press it. Gently. From the back. No rubbing, or the juice spreads and the lace tears."

So they did it together, at the sink, in the late afternoon. Reba's mother pumped fresh cold water and carried it in for them, a jug at a time, and Maggie Mae and Reba very gently pressed the water through the stain from the back, again and again. The water went pink. They changed it. It went pink again. They changed it again. And slowly, very slowly, the stain faded to a lighter purple, and then, which surprised Reba very much, to pink.

"It goes pink when the lemon touches it," said Maggie Mae, squeezing half a lemon over what was left. "Now the sun."

They laid the cloth out flat on the grass in the garden, where the evening sun was still warm and bright, and Reba sat down beside it, and watched it, and did not leave it once, not even when she heard the others laughing out in the lane.

And by the time the shadows were long, the pink had faded to almost nothing at all. Reba bent down so close that her nose nearly touched the cloth, and squinted.

"There," she said. "There's a tiny bit left. Look. Just there."

Maggie Mae bent down too, and squinted at it.

"Where?"

Reba pointed.

"Oh, that," said Maggie Mae. She picked up the cloth and shook it out briskly. "That's where the middle pie goes. Nobody will ever see it." And she folded it over her arm and went in to lay the tables.

That evening, at the long tables in Fern Meadow, the whole of Hickory Hollow sat down to the Berry Supper.

There was cornbread, and honey, and new cheese, and salad from the old garden behind the hedge, and jugs of cold cordial. And in the middle of every table, there was a blackberry pie, golden and leafy and bubbling.

Great-Grandpappy Buck sat at the top of the longest table. He had heard, of course. Everybody had heard. He watched Reba, sitting at the end of the table with her purple paws in her lap, waiting while everybody else took their pie first. He watched her take the smallest slice. He watched Maggie Mae lean across and swap it for the corner piece, the one with the most crunchy burnt sugar round the edge, which everybody knew was Reba's favourite.

Then he said, in his slow, warm voice, to nobody in particular, "Sorry's a fine word, but 'let me fix it' is a finer one."


Chapter Ten — Purple

The sun went down behind Weather Ridge, and the long tables in Fern Meadow emptied slowly, family by family, as the light went soft and blue.

Somebody had to wash the pie dishes.

Reba had said she would. And so, when the last of the supper was cleared, she carried the stack of twelve purple, sticky, crumby pie dishes to the Old Mill, where the big stone sink was. The miller filled it for her with warm water from the kettle, and she stood on a box in front of it, and began.

She was not on her own for long.

Charlie came, and stood on another box, and dried. Benny came, and stacked the dried dishes in a perfectly straight tower, in order of size. Barney came, and made bubbles in the washing water, and blew them at Charlie. Charlie blew them back, and soon there were bubbles everywhere, floating up into the rafters of the Mill kitchen in the lamplight. Brody came and reached the high shelf for them. Sadie Belle came, and Rusty, and Beau, and then Maggie Mae, with her sleeves rolled up, and took the pie dish Reba was holding, and finished it for her, because Reba's paws had gone wrinkly.

The miller's wife watched them from her big chair by the door, with a cup of tea, and laughed so much at Barney's bubbles that she had to put the cup down.

When they had finished, the twelve pie dishes stood clean and dry in a tower on the Mill table, and the floor was wet, and everybody had bubbles in their fur. They stood looking at each other in the lamplight, all of them, and every single one of them had purple paws. Purple from the picking. Purple from the filling. Purple from the eating. Even Sadie Belle, who never broke a rule in her life, had a purple patch on the end of her nose that nobody had dared to mention.

Reba looked round at them all.

"You've all gone purple," she said, wonderingly.

"So have you," said Rusty.

"I was purple first," said Reba. And everybody laughed, and the laughing went round the warm Mill kitchen and up into the rafters with the last of the bubbles.

Outside, the light had gone from Fern Meadow, and the long tables stood empty under the first stars, and all over the Hollow, doors were closing and lamps were going out.

And up on Blackberry Bank, where nobody was picking now, the long slope lay still in the warm summer dark.

The bottom half of it had been picked clean, twice over. But the top half, where nobody had gone at all, was still heavy with berries. Thousands and thousands of them, dark and ripe on the drooping brambles, more than anybody in the Hollow could ever pick, or ever eat. They were not for the pies. They were left for the bees in the morning, and the wasps, and the mice, and the birds.

Somewhere deep in the middle of the brambles, a little grey catbird had settled down for the night, right next to its breakfast.

A soft breeze came down off the ridge, and the whole bank rustled once, gently, and smelled of blackberries, and was still.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
