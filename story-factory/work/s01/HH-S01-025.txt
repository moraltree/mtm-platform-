Chapter One — Forty-Two Trips

In the first week of August, the weather turned hot and dry, and the brothers' bean bed in the bee garden began to droop.

It was the bed they had planted in June, on the day the whole Hollow restored the old garden behind the cottage at the edge of the Hickory Woods. Two long rows of runner beans, climbing up tall poles, with bright red flowers and the first long green beans just starting to hang down. Benny had measured every pole. Barney had named every plant. And every evening since, the brothers had gone to the garden together to water them.

That was the trouble.

The pump was by the cottage door, which faced the gate. The bean bed was at the far side of the garden, along the back wall, on the other side of the wheel of paths. As the crow flies, it was only a little way past the back corner of the old cottage. But there was no water at the back of the cottage. The water was at the front. And the watering can held only so much.

Each trip went like this. Fill the can at the pump. Carry it along the path, past the herbs, round the curve of the wheel, past the lettuces and the leeks and the strawberries, all the way to the far wall, trying not to slop. Water one plant, or two, if you were careful. Walk all the way back with the empty can. Fill it again.

"Forty-two," said Benny, on the Monday evening, setting the can down at the end of the row and wiping his forehead. "That's forty-two trips. To the pump and back. I counted."

"It felt like a hundred," said Barney, lying flat on his back on the path.

"It was forty-two," said Benny. "Every evening. And now it's hot, it'll have to be every morning, too. That's eighty-four trips a day." He sat down beside his brother. "Eighty-four trips a day, until the end of the summer. I worked it out. It's two thousand, five hundred and twenty trips."

Barney opened one eye.

"That's too many trips," he said. "We could do them faster. If we ran."

"We'd spill it," said Benny.

"We could carry two cans each."

"We'd spill twice as much."

Barney thought about it. "We could ask Brody to carry them. He's very big."

"That's still the trips," said Benny. "Just Brody doing them."

Barney closed his eye again. "It's still too many trips," he said.

"It is," agreed Benny. "Much too many."

They lay on the path together and looked up at the bean poles, with the bees going in and out of the red flowers, and the long green beans hanging down.

"There must be a better way," said Benny.

Barney, who had closed his eye again, said nothing.

But Benny was already thinking. Benny was always thinking. He sat up, and took out his notebook, and the pencil from behind his ear, and began to draw.

"A machine," he said. "A watering machine. So we never have to carry the can again."

Barney opened both eyes.

"Can I help?" he said.

"You can fetch things," said Benny, drawing fast. "This is going to need a great deal of fetching."


Chapter Two — The Plan

By Tuesday morning, Benny had a plan. It covered four pages of his notebook, and the back of a paper bag that Reba had given him, and one of Maggie Mae's seed packets.

He laid them all out on the kitchen table in the brothers' lodge, and explained.

"The water," he said, "has to get from here, the pump, to here, the beans. That's the problem." He traced a line across the drawing of the garden with his pencil. "So we make a channel. Out of wooden troughs. Joined end to end. Going all the way round the path, from the pump to the bean bed. And the water runs along it, all by itself, because the troughs slope downhill, very slightly."

"Brilliant," said Barney.

"But the pump's lower than the beans," said Benny, pointing. "The garden slopes up a bit, towards the wall. I measured. So the water won't run uphill. So we need to lift it, first. Up to the top of a tower. Here." He drew a tall tower next to the pump. "With a bucket on a rope, and a pulley, to haul the water up. And then it pours into the top trough, and runs down, all the way to the beans."

"Brilliant," said Barney, a little less certainly.

"But somebody has to haul the bucket," said Benny. "Which is nearly as much work as carrying the can. So we need a counterweight. A big stone, on the other end of the rope. So the bucket goes up by itself." He drew a stone. "And a lever to let it go."

Barney looked at the drawing. It had a tower on it now, and a pulley, and a rope, and a stone, and a lever.

"And," said Benny, turning the page, "the water mustn't all come at once, or it'll wash the beans away. So we need a little gate in the last trough, that opens a bit at a time. And a bell, on a string, that rings when the water gets to the beans, so we know it's arrived. And a second bell, in case the first one doesn't work." He turned another page. "And a little wheel in the trough, so that the water turns it on the way past, and it turns a flag, so you can see from the cottage door if the water's running."

Barney looked at all four pages, and the paper bag, and the seed packet.

"Benny," he said slowly. "How long will it take to build?"

Benny did a sum.

"About a week," he said. "If we work hard."

"And how many trips is a week?"

Benny did another sum, and paused, and frowned at it.

"Eighty-four a day," he said. "So five hundred and eighty-eight." He looked at the drawing. "But after that, none at all. Ever again."

Barney looked at the drawing. Then he looked out of the window, towards the Old Mill, where the miller was rolling a barrel along the lane.

"All right," he said. "Let's build it."


Chapter Three — Troughs

They started on Wednesday.

Benny could not build the whole machine on his own, and neither could Barney, and some of it needed grown-ups, so they went round the Hollow asking for help, just as everybody always did.

The miller gave them six old wooden troughs that had stood in the corner of the Mill yard for as long as anybody could remember, long and narrow and a bit cracked. Brody's father, who was handy with tools, said he would cut them to length and join them, as long as nobody else touched the saw. And by Wednesday evening, there was a long line of troughs running all the way round the path in the bee garden, propped up on stones and bricks and old flowerpots, from the cottage door to the bean bed, tilting very slightly downhill, at exactly the slope Benny had worked out.

"Now," said Benny, "the test."

Barney fetched a bucket of water from the pump, and poured it into the top trough.

The water ran along it beautifully. It ran into the second trough, and the third. It ran round the curve of the path, and into the fourth trough, and the fifth.

And then it reached a crack in the fifth trough, and most of it poured out onto the path, and soaked Charlie, who had come to watch.

"I'm wet," said Charlie.

"Crack," said Benny, writing. "Fifth trough. Needs patching."

They patched it with clay from the dry edge of the creek bank, which Barney dug, and which got everywhere, including into Benny's notebook, which Benny said was not the clay's fault but was definitely Barney's.

Then Barney had his first idea of the week, which was that if the water would not stay in the troughs, they could follow it along the path with the watering can and catch it. Benny said that would mean carrying the watering can, which was the whole point of not carrying the watering can. Barney said he had not thought of that. Benny wrote it down anyway, under a heading that said IDEAS (BARNEY), so as to be fair. Barney fetched another bucket. The water ran along the troughs, and round the curve, and past the patched crack, and into the sixth trough.

And then it reached the end of the sixth trough, and stopped, and sat there, because the sixth trough was the one nearest the bean bed, and the bean bed was uphill, and the water would not go uphill.

"That's where we need the tower," said Benny. "At the start. To lift the water higher, so there's enough slope all the way."

Barney looked at the troughs. Then at his brother. Then at the bean bed, drooping in the heat at the far end of the garden.

"Benny," he said. "The beans are thirsty now."

"I know," said Benny. "Just a few more days. Then it'll be perfect."

So that evening, they carried the watering can. Forty-two trips.


Chapter Four — The Tower

On Thursday, they built the tower.

It was not a very tall tower. Brody's father would not let it be any taller than Brody, and it had to be on the flat ground by the pump, where nobody could fall off anything. But it was a tower. Four strong poles, lashed together at the top by Brody's father, with a little platform on top, and a pulley hanging from a crossbar, and a rope over the pulley, and a bucket on one end of the rope.

On the other end of the rope, Benny tied a big round stone. The counterweight.

"When the bucket's full," said Benny, "it's heavier than the stone. So it stays down. When you pour some water out of the bucket..." He stopped. He looked at the drawing. "No. When you take the stone off... No." He frowned. "When you put the stone on..."

It took them most of the morning to work out how a counterweight was supposed to work, and they were not entirely sure, even then. Benny drew it nine times. Barney suggested asking the miller, who knew about weights and pulleys from the Mill, and the miller came and looked, and scratched his head.

"The stone wants to be a little heavier than a full bucket," he said. "Then it'll lift the full bucket up by itself." He scratched his head again. "Mind you, then somebody has to pull the empty bucket back down every time, and that lifts the stone back up."

Benny wrote that down, and looked at it, and decided not to look at it again.

The miller found a stone. It was much heavier than Benny had expected. It took Brody's father and the miller together to lift it onto the rope, with Brody steadying the rope for them, and the miller tied the knot. Everybody else stood well back from both sides of the tower, as the miller told them to. Benny read out the drawing from the notebook, and Barney stood with Charlie, watching the knot very hard in case it came undone.

Then Barney filled the bucket at the pump.

Nothing happened.

"Now the lever," said Benny. And Brody's father, who had said that nobody but him was to touch the lever, pulled it.

The bucket went up. Slowly, creaking, swinging, up and up the side of the tower, while the stone came down on the other side. It reached the top. It bumped against the crossbar. It tipped.

And all the water poured out, in a great silver splash, not into the top trough, but straight down the outside of the tower, onto Barney, who had just that moment stepped forward to look up at the top.

Barney stood there, dripping, with his eyes shut.

"Needs a spout," said Benny, writing.

"Needs a spout," agreed Barney, through the water running off his nose.

By Thursday evening, the bucket had a spout, which was made of a bent piece of tin from Maggie Mae's shed, and the water went into the top trough, mostly. It ran along all six troughs, and round the curve, and past the patched crack. And it got to the end of the sixth trough, and this time it had enough slope to keep going, and it went, and spilled out over the end, and onto the bean bed.

About a cupful of it.

The rest had leaked out of the joins, or splashed off the spout, or evaporated in the heat, or soaked into the wood of the old troughs, which were very dry and very thirsty themselves.

Benny looked at the cupful of water, sinking into the dusty earth at the end of the bean row.

"One bucket," he said slowly, "makes one cupful."

He did a sum. He did not like the answer.

That evening, they carried the watering can. Forty-two trips.


Chapter Five — Bells

On Friday, the beans began to go yellow at the edges.

Barney noticed. He noticed first thing in the morning, when he went to say hello to them, which he did every day, because he had named them. Bean Seven had a yellow edge on its lowest leaf. So did Bean Eleven, and Bean Twelve. He stood looking at them for a while, and then he went to tell Benny.

But Benny did not hear him. He was too busy building the bell.

The bell was a little brass one that Maggie Mae had lent him, from the hook by her kitchen window. It hung on a string over the last trough, and the string was tied to a small float made of cork, so that when the water came down the troughs, the float would bob up, and pull the string, and ring the bell, and everybody would know the water had arrived.

And the second bell, in case the first one did not work, was the big iron handbell from the Great Barn, which the farmer used for calling everybody in from the fields, and which was very loud.

And the little water wheel, in the third trough, was made from a cotton reel and eight paddles, exactly like the model of the Old Mill wheel the brothers had made in the winter, and it was supposed to turn a stick with a flag on it, so that you could see from the cottage door whether the water was running.

By Friday afternoon, the machine was almost finished, and half the Hollow had come to look at it.

Maverick sat on the garden wall and said that he had seen a great many things from the air, but never anything quite like this. Reba asked whether the troughs could be used for lemonade afterwards. Rusty said he knew a much quicker way to get water to the beans, which was to wait for it to rain, and Benny said that was not an invention, it was weather. Sadie Belle walked slowly all the way round the machine, looking at every part of it, and said that it was beautiful, which made Benny go pink, and then said, very gently, that it was a long way round for some water, which made Benny go pink in a different way.

Charlie, who had been soaked on Wednesday, stood well back, holding Maggie Mae's umbrella.

It was magnificent. It was the most complicated thing anybody in Hickory Hollow had ever built. There was the tower, with its pulley and its rope and its counterweight stone and its lever. There were the six long troughs, propped on their stones and flowerpots, running all the way round the garden path, with their joins sealed with clay. There was the little water wheel, with its flag. There was the float, and the string, and Maggie Mae's brass bell. And there was the big iron handbell.

"Ready?" said Benny, standing beside Brody's father at the lever, with his notebook.

Everybody nodded.

Barney filled the bucket at the pump, and stood well back.

Brody's father pulled the lever.

The bucket went up the tower, creaking. It tipped. The water poured out of the spout, into the top trough, and began to run. Down the first trough. Into the second. The little water wheel in the third trough began to turn, and the flag went round, and round, and everybody cheered.

Into the fourth trough. Round the curve. Into the fifth, past the patched crack. Into the sixth.

The float bobbed up.

The string tightened.

Maggie Mae's brass bell went ting.

And the handbell, which was tied to the same string, because Benny had run out of string, swung over and went CLANG against the side of the trough. It went so hard that the whole line of troughs jolted on its stones, all the way round the path to the middle of the garden, where the bee gums stood. And out of the little door at the bottom of the fifth gum came dozens of bees, in a cross, buzzing cloud, and everybody in the garden walked very quickly and very quietly to the gate.


Chapter Six — What Barney Saw

Nobody was stung. The miller, who knew about bees, shooed everybody out of the garden quietly and calmly, and shut the gate, and they all waited in the lane while the bees settled down again, which took most of the afternoon, and was very boring. Barney lay in the lane and counted clouds. Charlie lay beside him and argued about which ones were castles. Maverick, from the top of the wall, reported on the bees every few minutes, very importantly, as if he were describing a battle. "Still cross," he said. "Still quite cross. Slightly less cross. Going in now. Most of them. One very cross one is still out, looking for the bell."

The handbell was given back to the farmer.

And Benny sat down on the bank of the lane, outside the garden wall, with his notebook in his lap and his head in his paws.

"It doesn't work," he said. "None of it works. It leaks. It splashes. It frightens the bees. It takes a whole bucket to get a cupful to the beans. And it's taken a whole week, and the beans are going yellow, and we've still been carrying the can every night anyway." He looked at the notebook, with all its pages of drawings. "It's the most complicated thing I've ever made. And it's the worst."

Barney sat down beside him.

For a while he just sat. Then he stood up again, and brushed himself down.

"Come for a walk," he said. "I want to show you something."

"What?"

"You'll see," said Barney, and would not say another word about it, all the way down the lane and along the creek to the Old Mill, though Benny asked him eleven times.

Outside the Old Mill, Barney stopped, and swept out one paw, like a showman at the fair.

"Behold," he said grandly. "The Barney Water Machine."

Benny looked.

There was a barrel. A big old wooden barrel, standing on two bricks, right under the place where the gutter came down from the Mill roof. It had a board on top for a lid, with a notch cut out for the pipe, and a little tap at the bottom.

"It's a barrel," said Benny.

"It's a barrel," agreed Barney. "It has no levers. It has no pulleys. It has no bells at all. Every time it rains, the rain runs off the Mill roof, into the gutter, down the pipe, and in. And every evening, the miller's wife fills her can from that little tap, and waters her pots by the Mill door, and never goes near the pump." He knocked on the side of the barrel, and it gave back a deep, full sound. "I've been watching it all week, every time I fetched something from the Mill. It works every single day."

Benny opened his mouth. Then he shut it again.

"The old cottage in the bee garden has got a roof," said Barney. "And a gutter. And there's the old water butt, the one the tadpoles came out of on the day we cleared the garden. It's still lying round the side of the cottage, in the nettles. Empty."

"Which corner does the cottage gutter come down?" said Benny slowly. "I never looked."

Neither of them knew. But Maverick, who had followed them down the lane to see what was going on, was already lifting off the Mill roof. He went up, and round, and away over the big hedge, and came gliding back a minute later, and landed on the barrel lid.

"Back corner," he reported. "The one nearest the far wall. The pipe comes down about four hops from your beans."

Benny looked at his brother.

"It hasn't rained for a week," he said. "The butt would be empty."

"We fill it from the pump," said Barney. "Just once. And after that, every time it rains, it fills itself." He shrugged. "And it'll rain. It always rains, round here, sooner or later. Maverick says."

"I do say that," said Maverick.

Benny stared at the barrel.

Then, for a moment, he felt cross. Not with Barney, exactly. With the barrel. With how plain and ordinary it was, standing there by the Mill door, doing its job, without a single lever or pulley or bell.

"But that's not an invention," he said. "It's just a barrel. Anybody could have thought of that."

"Anybody didn't," said Barney. "The miller's wife did. Years ago. And she hasn't carried a watering can from the pump since."

Benny opened his mouth to argue. Then he looked at the barrel again, and at the miller's wife's pots by the Mill door, green and flowering in the heat, every one of them. And he thought of the long line of troughs back in the bee garden, with the bucket hanging from the tower, and the clay drying and cracking in the joins.

He closed his mouth.

"Anybody didn't," he said slowly. "Except you."


Chapter Seven — The Straw

But Barney was not finished.

"And there's another thing," he said, rather shyly. "I went and looked at Granny Opal's garden this week. When you were drawing the bells. Her beans aren't drooping at all. And she only waters them once a day. Even in the heat."

"Why?" said Benny.

"Straw," said Barney. "She's put straw all round them. All over the ground, thick. She calls it a sun hat for the earth. It keeps the sun off, so the water stays down in the ground instead of drying up into the air. She says where she comes from, everybody does it." And he took a long piece of straw out from behind his ear, where he had been keeping it all afternoon, exactly where Benny kept his pencil, and twiddled it at his brother.

Benny laughed, for the first time all day.

Benny looked at his brother.

Then he looked down at his notebook. At the four pages of drawings, and the paper bag, and the seed packet. At the tower, and the pulley, and the rope, and the stone, and the lever, and the six troughs, and the patched crack, and the spout, and the gate, and the float, and the two bells, and the little wheel with the flag.

And then at Barney's idea. Which was a barrel. And some straw.

"That's it?" he said.

"That's it," said Barney. "A barrel and a hat."

Benny was quiet for a while. Then he closed his notebook.

"Barney," he said. "It's the cleverest thing anybody's thought of all week."

Barney's ears went pink.

"Really?"

"Really," said Benny. He stood up. "Come on."

On Saturday morning, they set to work.

Brody's father hauled the old water butt out of the nettles, and scrubbed it out, and set it up on two flat stones at the back corner of the cottage, right under the end of the pipe. Then he made it a board lid, with a notch cut for the pipe, so that nothing and nobody could ever fall in. After that it had to be filled from the pump, just once, and this time it was Brody who carried the cans, two at a time, because he was very big, exactly as Barney had once said. It was still the trips. But it was the very last time.

Then the brothers went to Granny Opal's hollow at the end of the creek path, to ask her exactly how she did the straw. She came out in her shawl and showed them. She knelt down slowly on the path outside her door, and laid the straw round her bean stalks with her small pink hands. It was thick, but it never quite touched the stems, so that the stalks could breathe.

"Water first," she said. "Give the ground a good long drink. Then put its hat on."

Barney watched every handful. Benny measured how thick it was: three fingers.

Meanwhile Reba and Charlie had gone to the farm, and they came back up the lane walking beside the farmer, who was pushing a barrowful of old straw.

So Barney turned the little tap at the bottom of the butt, and filled the watering can right there at the end of the bean rows, and they gave the dry ground a good long drink, one plant after another. It still took forty-two cans, because dry earth is very thirsty, and nothing can change that. But every can went from the butt to the beans in four hops, instead of all the way round the wheel and back. Then they spread the straw, three fingers thick, right up to the stalks but never touching them, until not a bit of bare earth showed anywhere.

"Now," said Barney, "we wait and see."


Chapter Eight — The Rain

On Saturday night, it rained.

Not a big storm, like the one at the end of July. Just a long, steady, soft summer rain, that started after dark and went on all night, pattering on the roofs and the leaves and the creek, and dripping off everything, and filling everything up.

Benny lay in bed in the brothers' lodge, and listened to it.

"Barney," he whispered, into the dark. "Are you awake?"

"Yes," whispered Barney.

"Listen," said Benny. "It's raining."

They listened to it together. Then Benny said, very quietly, "I bet it's running off the cottage roof right now. Into the gutter. And down. And into the barrel."

"Filling it up," said Barney sleepily.

"Filling it up," said Benny. "All by itself. While we're asleep."

He thought about his machine, still standing in the garden, with its tower and its troughs and its pulley and its little flag. He thought about the bucket going up and tipping over onto Barney's head. He thought about the handbell, and the bees.

And he started to laugh. Quietly, at first. Then more.

"What?" said Barney.

"The bees," said Benny. "When the handbell went. Everybody tiptoeing for the gate. And the miller. And Charlie, wet through, at the very first test." He laughed until he had to hold his tummy. "I made a machine to save us carrying water. And it took a whole week. And all it did was soak you, and Charlie, and frighten the bees."

Barney began to laugh too.

"And it rang a bell," he said.

"It rang a bell," said Benny. "Ting. CLANG."

They laughed together in the dark, in their two beds, while the rain pattered on the roof, until they were too tired to laugh any more.

And in the morning, when they went to the bee garden, before breakfast, through the wet sparkling grass, the barrel at the corner of the cottage was full to the brim. The straw round the beans was dark and soft with rain, and when Benny pushed his paw down through it, the earth underneath was cool and damp, and the beans were standing up straight again. The yellow leaves were no worse, the drooping was gone, and there were new green leaves and new red flowers opening at the top of every pole.


Chapter Nine — Taking It Down

On Sunday afternoon, they took the machine down.

It took them less than an hour, which Benny said was a very interesting number, considering it had taken most of a week to put up.

They gave the troughs back to the miller, who said they would make very good flower boxes for the Mill. They gave the tin spout back to Maggie Mae, who said she would plant a geranium in it. They gave the brass bell back to Maggie Mae too, who hung it on its hook by her kitchen window again, and said it had never sounded so pleased to be home. Brody's father took down the tower, pole by pole, and took the rope off the pulley.

And Benny kept one thing.

The little water wheel. The cotton reel with eight paddles, which had turned in the third trough, and turned the flag, and been the only bit of the whole machine that had worked exactly as he had drawn it.

He put it in his pocket.

That evening, they went to Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, because it was a fine evening after the rain, and everybody was there, and because Buck had heard all about the machine, as everybody had, and had sent word that he would very much like to hear about it from the brothers themselves.

So they told him. The forty-two trips. The plan, with four pages and a paper bag and a seed packet. The troughs, and the crack, and Charlie getting wet. The tower, and the counterweight that nobody understood, and the bucket tipping over Barney's head. The spout. The cupful. The bells, and the float, and the ting, and the CLANG, and the bees pouring out of the fifth gum.

Everybody on the porch was laughing by the end of it. Even the miller, who had been the one to shoo everybody out. Even the miller's wife, who had come to the porch in the cart, and who laughed loudest of all when she heard that it was her own rain barrel that had done it.

"That old barrel," she said, wiping her eyes. "I put it there the summer my back went, because I couldn't carry the can from the pump any more."

"And then," said Benny, "Barney showed me a barrel."

"And some straw," said Barney.

"And I said wait for it to rain," said Rusty, from the bottom step. "Nobody wrote mine down."

"I did," said Benny, and opened his notebook, and showed him. Under IDEAS (BARNEY) there was a new heading, IDEAS (RUSTY), and underneath it, one word: WEATHER.

"And some straw," said Barney.

"And some straw," said Benny. "Granny Opal's straw. Now the water's four hops from the beans. And with the hat on, she says six cans once a day will do it, even in the heat. And when it rains, it's nothing at all. And the beans are better than they've ever been."

Great-Grandpappy Buck looked at the two of them over his spectacles, with his eyes crinkling.

Then he said, in his slow, warm voice, "Build it right the first time and you'll have time to sit on the porch."


Chapter Ten — Seventy-Eight

The sun went down behind Weather Ridge, and the evening came on cool and fresh after the rain.

Benny and Barney sat on the top step of the porch, side by side. They had nowhere to be. The beans were watered. The butt was full. There were no troughs to patch, no towers to build, and no bells to ring.

"What will you do now?" said Charlie, who was sitting on the step below. "With all the time? You've got seventy-two trips a day left over."

Benny thought about it.

"Seventy-eight," he said. "You did it the old way. It was eighty-four a day, morning and evening. Now it's six, once a day. Eighty-four take away six is seventy-eight."

"Seventy-eight," said Charlie. "What will you do with them?"

Benny opened his notebook at a clean page, and took the pencil from behind his ear.

Barney reached over, very gently, and took the pencil out of his brother's paw, and put it back behind his ear. Then he closed the notebook, and put it on the boards.

"Nothing," said Barney. "We're going to do nothing. I'll show you how. I'm very good at it."

He lay back on the porch boards, with his paws behind his head, and looked up at the sky.

Benny lay down beside him, rather stiffly, like a plank.

"How long do we do it for?" he said.

"You don't measure it," said Barney. "That's the whole point."

They lay there. Out over the creek, the swallows were skimming low, back and forth, catching the last of the evening insects.

After a while, Benny said, "I'm counting the swallows. I can't help it."

"Count slower," said Barney.

So Benny counted slower. "Fourteen," he said. And then, after a while, "Fifteen." And after a longer while, "Sixteen."

The light went soft and blue over the meadow. The first bats came out. The porch boards were still warm from the day, and the air smelled of wet grass and rain.

"Seven...teen," said Benny, very slowly, with his eyes half closed.

And then he did not say eighteen at all.

Barney turned his head and looked at his brother, fast asleep on the porch boards, with his pencil behind his ear and his paws folded on his tummy. Then he smiled, and turned back to the sky, and did nothing at all, which he was very good at.

Over the creek, the swallows went on skimming, back and forth, back and forth, with nobody counting them, until the light was gone.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
