Chapter One — A Chuckle Before Breakfast

Autumn had come to Hickory Hollow in the night, without knocking.

When the sun climbed over Sunrise Ridge that morning, it found the hickory trees gone the colour of butter and the maples along Silver Creek blushing red to their very tips. A thin mist lay on the water. The air smelled of woodsmoke, wet leaves and somebody's cornbread, and every spider's web in the valley was strung with tiny beads of dew.

It had rained hard in the night, and the wind had come with it, roaring through the hickories and flinging leaves about. Now both had gone, and everything was washed and shining.

Great-Grandpappy Buck was already on his porch. He was always first. He sat in his old hickory rocking chair with Bramble's patchwork quilt over his knees and a mug of hot berry-cordial steaming at his elbow. He wore his denim dungarees and his sturdy boots, his cap pulled low against the chill, and his spectacles perched on the end of his nose so that he could look over them at the weather.

"Well now," he said to Weather Ridge. "That was a lively night."

He rocked. The chair creaked. The creek talked to its stones. A Carolina wren on the woodpile sang its loud, bright tea-kettle, tea-kettle, tea-kettle.

And then, from somewhere very close by, something chuckled.

It was not a big laugh. It was a small, round, bubbling sort of chuckle, like somebody trying not to give away a joke. Glub-a-hup. Hup. Hup.

Buck stopped rocking.

He sat very still. His whiskers lifted. And slowly, a look came over his old face that nobody in the Hollow had seen there for a long time: the look of somebody hearing a tune they had not heard since they were young.

Glub-a-hup, went the chuckle again. Hup-hup.

"Well, I'll be," whispered Buck. "Well, I'll be. After all these years."

He knew that sound. He had known it the moment it began. He had made it himself, long, long ago, and then forgotten it the way you forget a song you used to hum.

His shoulders began to shake. His spectacles slid down his nose. And Great-Grandpappy Buck laughed out loud, all by himself, on his own porch, at a joke that was older than anybody in the valley but him.

He wiped his eyes with a corner of the quilt. He thought about going round the side of the lodge to look. Then he thought about the youngsters of the Hollow, who would soon be up and about, and how dearly they loved a mystery.

"Let's see who finds it first," he said to himself. And he smiled his slow, crinkly smile and went back to his cordial.

Across the creek, on the path that ran along the far bank, a small striped somebody had stopped dead with an acorn halfway to her mouth.

She had heard it too.


Chapter Two — Charlie Hears It First

Charlotte the chipmunk, whom everybody in the Hollow called Charlie, was very young, very curious and very good at hearing things.

She was walking along the creek path with Maggie Mae, carrying a little basket of late roses to give to Great-Grandpappy Buck. Or rather, Maggie Mae was carrying the basket, and Charlie was carrying the acorn, and also asking questions, which was how she carried most things.

"Maggie Mae," she said in an enormous whisper. "Did you hear that?"

Maggie Mae stopped. She was a young skunk, black and white and elegant, with a pink polka-dot bow and a magnificent fluffy tail, and she smelled, as she always did, of roses and warm honey.

"Hear what, sugar?"

"Somebody laughing. Over there. By Buck's."

They both listened. The creek chattered. A blue jay complained about something. Then, faint across the water, came a small bubbling sound, glub-a-hup, hup, and under it the deep, wheezy chuckle of an old beaver on his porch.

Charlie's blue eyes went perfectly round. Her purple gingham bow quivered.

"Something's laughing," she breathed. "Something that isn't anybody."

"Well, I declare," said Maggie Mae. "It does sound like a chuckle."

"Maybe it's a tree," said Charlie at once. "Maybe the hickories laugh in autumn, when their leaves tickle. Maybe nobody's ever noticed because nobody listened hard enough. Maybe I'm the first."

Maggie Mae smiled. She had been Charlie's best friend for as long as Charlie could remember, and something like a big sister for even longer, and she had learned that Charlie's ideas burst out of her like dandelion seeds: all at once, in every direction.

"Trees don't laugh, honey."

"How do you know? Have you asked one?"

Maggie Mae opened her mouth, and then closed it again, because she had not.

Charlie was already halfway to Grandpappy's Bridge.

"I'm going to find it!" she called back over her shoulder. "I heard it first, so it's my mystery! Don't tell anybody!"

She ran so fast that she ran straight into a large, soft, warm, dark wall on the far side of the bridge.

"Oof," said the wall. "Mornin', Charlie."

It was Brody, the black bear cub, with his red neckerchief and his little green rucksack, sitting on the creek bank with his head on one side.

"Charlie," said Brody, in a hushed and wondering voice, "did you just hear something laugh?"

Charlie looked at him in dismay. Her own secret mystery, and somebody else had heard it.

"No," she said.

Then, because she was an honest chipmunk at heart, she said, "Yes. But I heard it first."

"I heard it from my fishing spot," said Brody happily. "Down at Grandpappy's Pool. I think it's a song. A little song, trying to get out of somewhere. Like when a banjo string hums after you've stopped playing."

Charlie frowned. That was a good idea. It was nearly as good as hers.

And then, from the path behind them, came the sound of running feet, and splashing feet, and flapping wings, and Charlie realised with a sinking heart that it was not going to be her secret for very long at all.


Chapter Three — Everybody Has a Theory

By the time the sun was halfway up the sky, everybody had gathered beside Grandpappy's Bridge, because that was where news was always shared in the Hollow.

It turned out that Buck's end of the creek had been a busy place that morning. Everybody had been passing, and everybody had heard something.

"It was laughing at my jam," said Reba.

The young raccoon held up a jar of blackberry jam that she had been carrying to Buck's as a present, though it was now noticeably less full than when she started. "I was sitting on Buck's woodpile, just checking it had set," she explained, licking a paw, "and something went glub-hup, right underneath me. I think it was jealous."

"Jam doesn't laugh," said Benny.

The elder beaver brother had a carpenter's pencil behind his ear and a frown on his face. He did not like things that could not be explained.

"I think it was a loose board," he said. "On Buck's porch. Squeaking. Wood squeaks when it's wet."

"It wasn't a squeak," said Rusty. "It was a laugh."

The red fox pup was sitting on the bridge with his white-tipped tail tucked round his paws, looking uncomfortable. "I know every sound in the Hickory Woods," he said. "Every bird. Every frog. I can whistle all of them. And I don't know this one." He did not seem to like that very much.

"Maybe it's a fish with the hiccups," said Barney happily.

Benny's younger brother, scruffier and rounder, had been in the creek since dawn and was still dripping. "A very small fish," he added, "that drank its breakfast too fast."

"I heard it from the sky," said Maverick.

Everybody turned to look at him, because the young bald eagle was very easy to look at and liked it that way. He had been sitting in the big sycamore above Buck's lodge, polishing his beak on a branch. "I think," he said, puffing out his chest above his tan explorer's vest, "it was somebody very impressed with my flying."

"I don't think it was laughing at your flying," said Sadie Belle gently.

The young deer had been picking watercress at the edge of the creek, near the bottom of Buck's garden. She said it so kindly that Maverick did not mind at all. "Maybe it was just happy," she said. "Maybe somebody's had good news."

"Maybe somebody's planning a surprise party," said Maggie Mae, "and can't keep it in. I never can keep a surprise in."

There was a rustle in the long grass, and a young bobcat bounded onto the boards of the bridge with all four paws together, skidded, and stopped just in time.

"I heard it TOO!" announced Beau, his blue eyes shining. "So I pounced on it."

"You pounced on it?" said Charlie, horrified. "My laugh?"

"Well," said Beau. "I pounced at it. At a heap of leaves at the bottom of Buck's garden. The laugh wasn't in the heap of leaves. But a great many leaves were."

He shook himself, and a great many leaves came out of his fur.

Sadie Belle giggled, then quickly stopped, and went a little pink under her floral headband.

"Right," said Benny, pulling the pencil from behind his ear. "Lots of friends. Lots of ideas. One of them is a jealous jar of jam. We need a plan."

"Oh, please don't let it be a long one," said Barney.

"It will be exactly as long as it needs to be," said Benny.

Charlie tugged at Maggie Mae's yellow sleeve.

"It's my mystery," she whispered. "I don't want everybody finding it."

Maggie Mae looked down at her, and said nothing, but she put a paw on Charlie's head, very lightly, for a moment.


Chapter Four — Benny's Map of Laughs

Benny's plan, when it came, was a very good one, which annoyed Barney a little.

"A sound is loudest near where it starts," Benny explained. He had found a flat piece of birch bark and was drawing on it: the creek, the bridge, the garden, Buck's lodge. "So if each of us stands somewhere different and listens, and we mark down how loud it is at each place, the loudest places should all point to where it's coming from."

"Like following the smell of pie," said Reba, who understood at once.

"Exactly like following the smell of pie."

So they spread out along the bank. Maverick took the sycamore again, so that he could see everybody and flap a wing when the chuckle came. Rusty took the bottom of Buck's garden. Brody went back to Grandpappy's Pool. Sadie Belle stood among the watercress, and Beau by his famous heap of leaves, and Reba on the woodpile with her jar, just in case. Barney and Benny stood on the bridge. And Charlie and Maggie Mae waited on the path at the end of the lodge, where the porch steps came down.

Then they waited.

Waiting is very hard when you are young, and hardest of all when you are a chipmunk, because a chipmunk's legs are made for running and her tail is made for twitching, and neither of them is interested in sitting still. Charlie counted pebbles. She counted Maggie Mae's polka dots, twice, and got a different number each time. Maggie Mae unfolded a cloth from her basket and handed her a honey biscuit, because Maggie Mae always brought something to share.

And then, just as Charlie had her mouth full, it came.

Glub-a-hup. Hup-hup-hup.

It was close. Very close. It seemed to come bubbling up out of the ground somewhere near the end of the porch. Up in the sycamore, Maverick flapped both wings so hard that he nearly fell off his branch. From the bridge came Barney's voice shouting "THERE!" and Benny's voice shouting "Write it DOWN!"

And then somebody laughed.

It was Brody, down at the pool. Brody could never hear a laugh without joining in, and his laugh was big and soft and round, and it rolled up the bank. And Sadie Belle, hearing Brody, laughed her little silvery laugh. And Beau, hearing Sadie Belle, began to laugh too. Reba laughed with a mouthful of jam. Up on his porch, Buck was laughing so hard that his chair rocked by itself.

The laughing went along the creek from one friend to the next, and then further. A rabbit in the lane heard it and laughed. The miller's wife at the Old Mill heard the rabbit and laughed. Somebody up on the meadow heard her and laughed, without the faintest idea why.

For a little while, a whole stretch of Hickory Hollow was laughing its head off, and nobody knew at what.

Charlie, with her cheeks full of biscuit, could not laugh at all. She could only say "mmf-mmf-mmf" and wave her paws, and Maggie Mae had to pat her on the back.

When it was over, everybody came running with their marks. Benny laid them out on his bark map, one by one, and drew a line from each, and the lines all crossed in exactly the same place.

Everybody leaned in to look.

The lines crossed right under the end of Buck's porch.


Chapter Five — The Wrong Shortcut

Ten friends arrived at Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch at once, which was a great many friends for one porch. Maverick arrived from above. Beau came over the garden wall. Rusty arrived by a shortcut through a bramble bush and was still picking out the burrs.

"It's here," said Charlie, before anybody else could speak. "Benny's map says so. Something under your porch is laughing."

Buck looked at them all over his spectacles, and his whiskers twitched.

"Is it, now?" he said. "Well. You'd best have a look."

So they looked.

They looked behind the woodpile and under the rocking chair and inside Buck's boots, which he took off for them very obligingly. Benny tapped every porch board in turn, listening for a squeak, and found none. Maverick flew round the lodge twice at roof height, peering down from the air. Brody looked under the steps with his nose. Sadie Belle looked at Buck, because she said that was the best place to look if you wanted to know a secret, and Buck only smiled and rocked.

But the porch did not laugh. It was perfectly, stubbornly silent.

They waited a long time. Nothing happened. The morning went soft and golden.

"Maybe it's gone," said Brody sadly.

Rusty had been very quiet. He did not like not knowing things. He had been listening to every sound in the Hollow his whole life, and now there was one he could not find, and it made him feel prickly inside, as if he had walked into his own den and found the furniture moved.

"Maybe," he said slowly, "it's a trick. Somebody making the noise. Somebody small, who can hide anywhere."

He did not say Charlie's name. But everybody, without meaning to, looked at Charlie.

Charlie went hot from her ears to the tip of her tail.

"It isn't me!" she cried. "I'd never do a trick with it! I heard it FIRST!"

And she spun round, jumped off the porch steps, and was gone round the end of the lodge before anybody could say a word.

"Rusty," said Great-Grandpappy Buck.

It was not loud. It was not cross. But the whole porch went still.

"Nobody on this porch is playing tricks," said Buck. "Not Charlie. Not anybody. I'll tell you that for certain."

Rusty's ears went flat. "I didn't mean..."

"I know you didn't," said Buck, more gently. "But it landed all the same." He looked towards the corner of the lodge, where a small striped tail had just disappeared. "She's gone round by the elderberry, at the end of the porch. She'll want a moment to herself first. Maggie Mae, my dear. In a little while, would you go to her?"

Maggie Mae nodded, and stood at the top of the steps, and waited, and watched the corner.


Chapter Six — What Charlie Found

Charlie had not run far. She had only run round the end of the porch, to where an elderberry bush grew against the lodge wall, and crawled underneath its low branches where nobody could see her.

There she sat with her paws over her eyes and felt as small and hot and cross as she had ever felt in her life.

It was not fair. She had heard it first. She had wanted it to be hers. And now everybody thought she had made it up.

She sat for a while. A ladybird walked across her toe. Slowly, the hot feeling began to cool.

And then, from very close by, something chuckled.

Glub-a-hup.

Charlie froze.

It was so close that she could feel it, a tiny bubbling tremble in the ground under her paws. It came from behind her. From under the porch.

The end of the porch was raised up on old stone footings, and between the footings, behind the elderberry, there was a gap: a low, dry, shadowy space, just tall enough for a chipmunk, with soft earth on the floor, a little stone channel running out towards the creek, and sunlight coming through between the boards above in thin gold stripes.

Charlie crept to the edge of it and peered in.

Just under the edge of the porch, tucked between two footings, half hidden by ferns, was a little stone basin, green with moss and full of water. And running down into it, from the corner of the porch roof just above, down the side of the end post and in under the boards, was a long wooden rain-pipe made of hickory, split and hollowed and pegged together. It was old, and grey, and mossy, and Charlie had never once noticed it before.

The bottom of the rain-pipe dipped right down under the water in the basin. A thin trickle was still running down it from the roof. And as the water came down, the pipe gulped air, and the air bubbled up through the water in the basin, and the whole thing went glub-a-hup, hup, hup.

It was laughing.

Charlie stared at it. Then, slowly, she began to smile.

A laughing rain-pipe. And she had found it, all by herself, before anybody else.

Now it really could be her secret. She could come every morning after rain and sit beside it under the porch. When the others asked, she could say she did not know. It would be hers and only hers.

She sat very still and thought about that.

The pipe chuckled again. Glub-a-hup.

And Charlie found that she did not feel like laughing at all.

It was strange. On the path, when everybody along the creek had laughed together, she had laughed so much that she could not breathe. Here, by herself, the pipe was still funny. But it was only a little bit funny. It was like a honey biscuit with no honey.

There was a soft rustle in the elderberry, and Maggie Mae's black-and-white face appeared between the leaves, with her pink bow slightly crooked.

"There you are, sugar," she said quietly. "May I come and sit?"

Charlie looked at her. Then she looked at the basin. Then she took a big breath.

"Maggie Mae," she said. "I found it. Come and see. And then can we get everybody else? I think it's better with everybody."


Chapter Seven — The Laughing Rain-Pipe

They all squeezed round the end of the porch, which was quite a squeeze. Maverick stood on the woodpile. Beau lay flat along the garden wall. Brody, the biggest, sat at the back and peered over everybody's heads. Charlie crouched at the very front, beside the gap under the porch, and showed them.

"Well, I'll be," said Benny slowly.

He knelt and looked in at the stone basin and up along the grey rain-pipe. Then he did something unusual for Benny. He put his ear down close to the basin and listened, with his eyes shut.

Glub-a-hup.

"Air," he said at last. "The bottom of the pipe's under the water. When the water comes down, it pushes the air in front of it, and the air has to bubble out through the basin. Glub. Hup. Like blowing through a straw into a cup."

"A fish with the hiccups," said Barney, delighted. "I was nearly right."

"I was right," said Reba. "It's a jar. It's just a very big jar."

"But why didn't it laugh when we were all on the porch?" asked Sadie Belle.

Benny looked up at the corner of the roof, where the rain-pipe began, and at the thick, damp mat of leaves the storm had piled in the gutter.

"Because it's only a trickle now," he said. "The rain stopped hours ago. The wet leaves up there are just letting it go a little at a time. It only laughs when enough comes down at once."

Everybody turned to Great-Grandpappy Buck, who had come slowly down the porch steps with his walking-stick, and was leaning on the wall with a look on his face like a beaver who has been caught.

"Ah," said Buck. "Well now. I'd better tell you."

He lowered himself onto the edge of the woodpile.

"When I was a young beaver, not much older than Benny and Barney, I built this porch roof," he said. "And I built it a little bit wrong. Every time it rained, all the water off the whole roof came pouring down in one spot. And that spot happened to be exactly where I liked to stand. Every single rain, I'd come out of the door, and splosh. Straight down the back of my neck."

Barney laughed so hard that he had to sit down.

"So I made a rain-pipe," said Buck, "to carry the water off. And I didn't measure the bottom of it quite right, and it ended up under the water in the basin. The first time it rained, it went glub-a-hup all night long. My mother said it sounded like the porch was laughing at me for building it wrong. And I got so fond of it that I never had the heart to fix it."

"Then why did it stop?" asked Charlie.

"Leaves," said Buck. "A great many autumns ago, the top of it filled up with old leaves and moss, and it went quiet. And I got old, and I forgot all about it. Last night's wind must have blown the old plug clean out." He chuckled. "I knew it the first moment I heard it this morning. It gave me quite a turn."

"You KNEW?" said Rusty. "All morning?"

Buck's face went serious.

"I did," he said. "I thought it would be a fine thing for you to find out for yourselves. And it was, mostly. But I ought to have spoken up sooner, when it started to go wrong. When Charlie was blamed for something she hadn't done." He looked at Charlie. "That was my mistake, little one. I'm sorry for it."

Charlie blinked. She had never heard a grown-up say sorry to her quite like that before.

"That's all right," she said. "I found it anyway."

"So you did," said Buck. "So you did."


Chapter Eight — Making the Rain-Pipe Laugh

"It's gone quiet again," said Brody, a little sadly.

He was right. The trickle had slowed to a drip, and the basin was still.

"It needs more rain," said Benny. "And it's not going to rain today."

Charlie looked at the rain-pipe. Then she looked at Buck's big watering can by the door, and at the creek, and at all her friends.

"We could give it some rain," she said.

So they did.

Great-Grandpappy Buck said that nobody was to climb on any roof, not his and not anybody's, and that he would do the pouring himself. The porch roof was low at that end, so low that Buck could reach the gutter from the porch itself, standing steady with one paw on the post and the long-spouted can in the other. The fetchers would bring the water up the steps a little at a time, so that his can never got too heavy. So that was the plan. Buck poured. Everybody else fetched.

Brody filled the little cans and pails from the shallow edge of the creek, where the water barely covered his paws. Sadie Belle and Beau and Benny made a line from the creek to the porch steps to pass them along, and Beau only dropped his twice, which for Beau was a record. Reba fetched her biggest kettle, in case one can was not enough. Maverick flew slow circles overhead and shouted "More!" and "Less!" and "That was a good one!", because somebody had to be in charge of the sky.

Buck poured.

The rain-pipe gulped and gurgled. Under the porch, the basin went glub-a-hup, hup, hup, HUP.

And everybody laughed.

Barney found that if Buck poured slowly, it went glub. Hup. Glub. Hup. And if he poured fast, it went glub-a-hup-hup-hup-a-glub-a-HUP. So Barney stood at the bottom of the steps and called out instructions, slow, fast, slow, slow, fast, until the rain-pipe was laughing in rhythm, like a song.

"It's singing!" shouted Brody. "I told you it was a song!"

"It's laughing in time," said Benny, but he was grinning.

The rabbits from the lane came to see what the noise was, and laughed. The miller came up from the Old Mill with flour on his whiskers, and laughed so hard that he sneezed a cloud of flour all over the woodpile, and everybody laughed at that. By the middle of the afternoon there was a crowd round the end of Buck's lodge. Some were laughing at the rain-pipe. Some were laughing at each other. And some were laughing simply because everybody else was.

Charlie lay on her back on the grass by the garden wall with her paws in the air, laughing until her sides hurt, and Maggie Mae lay beside her with leaves all down her tail and did not care a bit.


Chapter Nine — Rusty's Apology

When the crowd had gone home and the watering can was back by the door, the light began to turn.

The sun went orange and low over Weather Ridge, and the long shadows of the hickories reached across the creek. The air grew cool and smelled of woodsmoke and apples.

Rusty found Charlie sitting on the porch steps.

He stood beside her for a moment without saying anything, which was unusual for Rusty. He scuffed one paw on the step, then the other.

"Charlie," he said at last. "I'm sorry I said it might be somebody small playing a trick."

Charlie looked up.

"I didn't really think it was you," said Rusty. "I just didn't like not knowing. I thought I knew every sound in this valley, and then there was one I didn't, and I felt silly, and I said the first thing that came into my head." His ears went down. "It was a shortcut. A thinking shortcut. And it went the wrong way, and you got hurt by it."

Charlie thought about that for a while.

"I ran off," she said. "That wasn't very grown up."

"You came back, though," said Rusty. "And you brought everybody to see it."

"I nearly didn't," Charlie admitted. "For a little while I was going to keep it."

Rusty grinned for the first time all day. "I'd have kept it for a week," he said. "Just to be the only one who knew."

"Friends?" said Charlie.

"Friends," said Rusty.

And because Rusty could whistle any sound in the Hollow, once he had heard it, he pursed his lips and did a very small glub-a-hup, exactly like the rain-pipe, and Charlie laughed so suddenly that she fell off the step.

Behind them, the door of the lodge opened, and the warm smell of baking came out. Maggie Mae had been busy in Buck's kitchen, with Buck minding the oven for her, because nobody can spend a whole afternoon laughing without getting hungry, and now there was cornbread and honey and a great dish of baked apples with cinnamon. Reba was already standing beside the table, holding a fork and looking innocent.

One by one, the friends came up onto the porch. Benny and Barney, still arguing about whether the rain-pipe laughed better fast or slow. Brody with a smudge of flour on his nose from the miller's sneeze. Sadie Belle and Beau came side by side, Beau telling a long story about the time he pounced on a heap of leaves. Sadie Belle had seen it with her own eyes that very morning. But she listened all the way through as if it were the most exciting thing she had ever heard. Maverick glided down to the porch rail with a flourish, slipped a little, and pretended he had meant to.

And Great-Grandpappy Buck settled into his rocking chair.


Chapter Ten — Two Joys

The rocking chair gave its first slow creak, and the porch went quiet.

The sky over Sunrise Ridge had gone a deep, soft blue. One star had come out, low and silver. Down in the reeds by the beaver ponds, a frog said something sleepy. The lantern by the door had been lit, and it made a small gold circle on the old boards, and everybody was inside it.

Buck reached for his banjo, which leaned in its corner by the door. He tuned one string, very gently, with his head on one side.

"Brody," he said. "Would you play along?"

Brody beamed from ear to ear. He took his own little banjo out of his rucksack. Buck had been teaching him, string by string, all through the summer, and he was not very good yet, but he loved it with his whole heart.

Buck began a slow tune, soft as water going round stones. Brody followed a little behind, finding the notes one at a time. It had a wobble in it where Brody's paw slipped. Nobody minded.

Charlie climbed into Maggie Mae's lap, and Maggie Mae drew a corner of Bramble's quilt over both of them.

"Grandpappy," said Charlie sleepily, when the tune was done. "Will the rain-pipe laugh again tomorrow?"

"Only if it rains," said Buck.

"I hope it rains," said Charlie.

"So do I," said Barney.

"So do I," said everybody, all at once, and then they laughed, because they had all said it together.

Buck smiled, and the lamplight caught his long white whiskers.

"I heard that old rain-pipe this morning, all by myself," he said. "And it made me laugh. But it wasn't half as funny as this afternoon, with all of you, and half the Hollow, and the miller sneezing flour on my woodpile." He rocked, slowly, and the chair creaked. "A shared trouble is half a trouble, young'uns. And a shared joy is two joys."

He began to play again, very softly. The tune went down and down, like a path going down to the creek in the dark. Brody played along for a while, and then his paw slowed, and stopped, and his chin sank onto his chest with the little banjo still in his arms.

Charlie's eyes closed. Then Barney's. Then Benny's, though he had been trying very hard to keep them open in case anything needed measuring.

Out beyond the porch, the stars came out over the Hollow until the whole sky was full of them. The creek went on talking to its stones. And all along Silver Creek, in every cabin and lodge and burrow, the lamps went out, one after another, until the valley lay dark and warm and safe beneath the sky.

Down under the end of the porch, behind the elderberry, the old rain-pipe let one last drop fall into its mossy basin.

Hup, it went, very quietly, as if it were laughing in its sleep.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
