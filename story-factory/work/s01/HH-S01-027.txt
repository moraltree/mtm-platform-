Chapter One — All at Once

In the last week of June, on a warm, dark evening, Rusty saw something very strange at the edge of Fern Meadow.

He was walking home along the creek path with his father, long after the sun had gone down behind Weather Ridge, when the last of the light had gone out of the sky, when he stopped so suddenly that his father nearly walked into him.

"Look," whispered Rusty. "Over there. By the woods."

At the far edge of the meadow, where the long grass ran up to the dark trees of the Hickory Woods and the creek went chattering in among the roots, there were lights.

Little lights. Hundreds of them. Tiny pale yellow-green sparks, hanging in the dark among the trees.

That was not strange in itself. Everybody in Hickory Hollow knew fireflies. On summer evenings they drifted up out of the grass all over the Hollow, blinking on and off, here and there, each one in its own good time, like somebody idly striking very small matches.

But these were not blinking here and there.

These were blinking all at once.

Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash. Every single light at the edge of the woods, together, on the same beat. And then, all at once, every single one went dark.

Rusty stood quite still in the dark lane, with his white-tipped tail held out behind him and his amber eyes very wide, and waited.

Nothing. Just black trees and the sound of the creek.

And then, as suddenly as before, all at once, the whole edge of the woods lit up again. Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash. And went dark.

"Dad," whispered Rusty. "Did you see that?"

"I saw it," said his father, very quietly. He was staring at the trees too.

They stood together in the lane and watched it happen again, and again, and again. Lights, all together. Dark, all together. Lights. Dark. Like the whole wood was breathing.

Then his father put a paw on his shoulder, and they walked home. But Rusty kept looking back over his shoulder all the way to the bend in the path, until the trees hid the meadow from sight.

He could not stop thinking about it, all through supper. He had seen fireflies every summer of his life. He had chased them through the long grass when he was very small, and watched them drift up over the creek on warm nights, and once he had even had one land on the end of his nose, where it blinked twice, very politely, and flew away again. Fireflies were ordinary. Fireflies were just part of summer, like honeysuckle and thunder and staying up late.

But he had never, ever seen fireflies do that.


Chapter Two — Theories

By the next morning, everybody in the Hollow had heard about the lights at the edge of Fern Meadow. And everybody had a theory.

"It's a signal," said Beau, on Grandpappy's Bridge, with great certainty. "Somebody in the woods is sending messages. Flash, flash, flash. It's a code."

"Who would be sending messages in the woods at night?" said Charlie.

Beau thought about it.

"Somebody mysterious," he said.

"It's the creek," said Barney. "The water catches the moonlight, and sparkles up into the trees."

"There wasn't any moon," said Rusty. "I looked."

"Then it's sparkling without the moon," said Barney, undaunted. "Very clever water."

Benny had brought his notebook.

"Rusty," he said. "How many flashes, each time? Before they all went dark?"

Rusty frowned, and thought back.

"Five," he said. "Or six. Or maybe seven. And then dark for a bit. I didn't count properly. I was too busy staring."

"Then we need to go back and count properly," said Benny, writing down FIVE OR SIX OR SEVEN, and underlining it twice, which did not make it any more exact.

Reba thought it might be somebody's kitchen, with the lamp going on and off. Brody wondered, very gently, whether it might be stars that had come down too low and got caught in the branches. Maggie Mae said that whatever it was, she would be wearing her good shawl to look at it, because a thing like that deserved a shawl.

"It's ghosts," said Barney, and then, seeing Charlie's face, "friendly ones. Very small friendly ghosts, with lanterns. Having a dance."

"It's not ghosts," said Benny firmly. "There's no such thing."

"Then it's a dance without ghosts," said Barney. "Which is even stranger, if you think about it."

Nobody could quite argue with that, so nobody tried.

Sadie Belle had not said anything at all. She was sitting at the end of the bridge, with her legs folded underneath her, listening to everybody.

"What do you think it is, Sadie Belle?" said Charlie.

"I don't know yet," said Sadie Belle. "I'd like to see it first."

Maverick landed on the rail of the bridge with a great flap and a great deal of importance.

"I'll find out," he said. "Nothing gets past me. I'll fly over the woods tonight and have a look from above, and in the morning I'll tell you exactly what it is."

There was a short pause on the bridge.

"Maverick," said Charlie. "You don't fly after dark. Grandpappy Buck says so."

Maverick's chest feathers went down, ever so slightly.

"I don't," he admitted. "Eagles are home before dark." Then they went up again. "But I can fly over the woods today. In the daylight. And look for clues."

"What sort of clues?" said Rusty.

Maverick looked at the sky, and then at the woods, and then at the creek.

"Mysterious ones," he said, and took off.


Chapter Three — The Wrong Way to Look

That evening, a great many youngsters wanted to go and look at the lights.

Nobody was going to the edge of the woods in the dark on their own, of course. So Great-Grandpappy Buck said he would come along, and Rusty's father, and Sadie Belle's mother, and between them they made a plan. Everybody would meet at the Great Barn, at the edge of Fern Meadow, at sundown, and walk across the meadow together on the mown path, with the grown-ups carrying the lanterns.

Maverick came too, but only as far as the Great Barn, and only until sunset. He sat in the high loft doorway, right up under the roof, where he could see the whole meadow, until the sun touched the ridge.

"I'd watch from here," he said. "Strategically. But eagles are home before dark." And he flew off to his roost while the sky was still gold.

So the rest of them set off. Rusty went first, because he had seen the lights first. Then Beau, and Benny and Barney, and Charlie holding Maggie Mae's paw, and Brody, and Reba, and Sadie Belle, and the grown-ups with the three lanterns swinging, making three bright golden pools of light on the path.

And they were not quiet.

They were not quiet at all. Beau was explaining his signal theory, loudly. Barney was explaining his clever-water theory, louder. Benny was explaining how he was going to count the flashes, and Reba was asking whether anybody had brought anything to eat, and the lanterns were swinging, and everybody's feet were swishing through the grass at the edge of the path, and Rusty kept saying, "Shh! Shh!", which was the loudest noise of all.

They got to the edge of the woods.

They stopped, and looked.

There was nothing there. Not a single light. Just the dark trees, and the creek, and three swinging lanterns lighting up a great many eager faces.

"They were here," said Rusty. "They were right here. Hundreds of them."

"It's because they saw us coming," said Beau. "They're shy. Mysterious signallers are always shy."

"Shall I do a flash back?" said Barney, and before anybody could stop him he waved his paws in front of the nearest lantern, so that its light went flicker, flicker, flicker across the trees.

Nothing flashed back.

Rusty tried whistling. Rusty could whistle almost any bird in the Hollow, and he thought that perhaps, if the lights belonged to some kind of night bird, it might answer. So he whistled like a whip-poor-will. Then like an owl. Then like a whip-poor-will that had got muddled and was trying to be an owl. Nothing answered except a real owl, very far off, who sounded rather offended.

They waited a long while. Benny got out his notebook, and then realised it was too dark to write in, except right next to a lantern, and wrote NOTHING very large in the lantern light, and underlined it. Reba sat down. Barney lay down. Beau did his signal again, with his whole body, leaping up and down in front of the lanterns, and very nearly fell into a bramble.

Then Great-Grandpappy Buck said it was getting late for small folk, and they all walked home again across the meadow, swishing and swinging, and nobody saw a single flash all the way.


Chapter Four — Everything Goes Wrong in a Different Way

On the second evening, they tried again. And this time, everybody had a better idea.

Benny's better idea was to bring a slate and a piece of chalk, so that he could count in the dark by feel, making a mark for every flash. Beau's better idea was a bigger lantern, so that the signallers could see they were friendly. Rusty's father agreed to carry it, since lanterns are for grown-ups. Barney's better idea was to bring a drum, for reasons he could not quite explain. And Rusty's better idea was a shortcut.

"Trust me," said Rusty, at the edge of the mown path. "I know a shortcut. Straight across the long grass. We'll be there in half the time."

Rusty's father said no.

"We stay on the mown path," he said. "All of us. All the way."

Rusty did not see what difference it could possibly make, since it was just grass. But he stayed on the path.

They got to the edge of the woods.

The lights were there.

For one wonderful moment, everybody saw them, hanging among the dark trees beyond the creek. Flash. Flash. Flash. All together.

"There!" shouted Beau.

And at the same moment, Barney, who had been told quite firmly that the drum was not a good idea, got so excited that he forgot, and banged it.

BOOM.

Beau jumped clean into the air with fright, and knocked Rusty's father's arm, and the big lantern swung up and out, high over the creek, so that its bright golden glare went sweeping right across the trees.

The lights at the edge of the wood flickered, and faltered, and went raggedy, one here, one there, all out of time. And then, in the glare of the big lantern, they seemed to fade away into nothing.

There was a terrible silence.

"Was that me?" said Barney, in a small voice. "Was it the drum?"

"I think it was the lantern," said Benny slowly. "They went wrong the moment the light went over them," holding up his slate, which had three chalk marks on it, and a big smudge where he had dropped it.

Rusty's father lowered the lantern. Barney lowered his drum. Everybody looked at the dark trees, where the lights had been.

And it was then, in the dark and the quiet, that Sadie Belle said, very softly, "Look down."

Everybody looked down.

In the long grass at the edge of the path, close to their feet, there was a tiny light. Just one. It glowed for a moment, pale and soft, and went out. Then another one, a little further off, did the same. They did not flash together. They did not flash in fives or sixes or sevens. They just glowed, low down in the grass, very quietly, every now and then, as if they were waiting for something.

"What are those?" whispered Charlie.

"I don't know," said Sadie Belle. "But they're in the grass. Not in the trees."

They walked home very slowly that night, without saying much. And every one of them, even Beau, kept to the very middle of the mown path, and did not let so much as a toe touch the long grass at the edges.


Chapter Five — Clues in the Daylight

The next morning, Maverick came down from his morning flight with a great deal to report.

He landed on the rail of Grandpappy's Bridge, where everybody was waiting for him, and folded his wings very carefully, and cleared his throat.

"I have flown over the edge of the woods every morning since Rusty saw the lights," he said. "And yesterday, four times. Low. Looking for clues."

"And?" said everybody.

"And there's nothing there," said Maverick. "Nothing at all. No signal lamps. No secret kitchens. No stars caught in the branches." He looked at Brody kindly. "Sorry, Brody. No clever water, Barney. Just trees, and ferns, and the creek, and some moss."

He had, he explained, flown the first pass high, to get the lie of the land, and seen nothing. He had flown the second pass lower, and seen a squirrel, who had been very cross about it. He had flown the third pass so low that he had got a fern caught round one foot. He had had to land on a stone in the creek to get it off, which he would prefer not to discuss. And on the fourth pass, he had given up flying altogether, and sat on a branch at the edge of the woods for a whole hour, perfectly still, just looking.

"And that's when I saw them," he said. "Beetles."

"Beetles?" said Sadie Belle.

"Hundreds of them," said Maverick. "Little ones. Brown and black, with a bit of pink and yellow at the front, like a little collar. Just sitting about on the undersides of the leaves, and on the ferns, all along the edge of the woods by the creek. Not doing anything. I nearly didn't notice them at all." He fluffed up his feathers. "I notice everything, of course."

Sadie Belle was very still.

"Maverick," she said slowly. "Will you show me? Where the beetles are? Now, in the daylight?"

So Maverick flew ahead, low and slow, and Sadie Belle walked along the creek path underneath him, and Rusty went with her, and Sadie Belle's mother came too, to keep an eye on things by the water. And at the very edge of the woods, where the creek ran in among the roots, Maverick landed on a branch and pointed with his beak at a big fern.

Sadie Belle bent down, very gently, and looked underneath the fern without touching it.

There, clinging to the underside of the frond, was a small, plain beetle. Long and thin and dark, with soft wing cases, and a little rosy collar behind its head, and, at the very end of its body, a pale, creamy-yellow band.

"Rusty," whispered Sadie Belle. "Look at the end of it."

Rusty looked.

"It's pale," he said. "Like it's made of wax."

"That's the bit that lights up," said Sadie Belle. "I've seen it before, on the fireflies that come in the grass by our house. This is a firefly. Sleeping. They sleep all day, and come out at night."

Rusty stared at the little beetle under the fern. It did not look like anything very special at all.

Rusty reached out a paw towards the fern, to pick the beetle off and have a proper look at it. Then he stopped, with his paw in the air.

"It's asleep," he said. "Isn't it. If I pick it up, I'll wake it." And he put his paw back down by his side.

Sadie Belle smiled at him.

"Then the lights in the woods," said Rusty slowly, "are just fireflies?"

Sadie Belle shook her head.

"Not just," she said. "Ordinary fireflies blink any old how. Each one on its own. These ones blink together. All of them. At once." She looked along the edge of the woods, at the hundreds of plain brown beetles hiding under the leaves, waiting for the dark. "I don't know why. But I'd like to."


Chapter Six — How to Watch

So that afternoon, the youngsters held a meeting in the Great Barn, to work out how to watch properly.

It was Sadie Belle who started it, but everybody had something to say. Even Barney, who sat on his drum and did not bang it once.

"The first night," said Sadie Belle, "we had three bright lanterns swinging about, and they didn't come out at all. The second night they came out, but the moment the big lantern swung over them, they stopped flashing together and went all raggedy. So maybe they need it dark. Properly dark."

"Fireflies make their own light," said Benny, who had been thinking very hard. "So maybe they need the dark so they can see each other's. If you wanted to flash at the same time as somebody, you'd need to be able to see when they flashed."

Everybody thought about that.

"So if somebody holds up a great big lantern," said Beau slowly, "they can't see each other at all. It's like..." He frowned. "It's like trying to see a candle at midday."

"And the grass," said Rusty suddenly. "The little lights in the grass. The ones that glowed by themselves, low down. The fireflies in the trees are flashing at them. Aren't they? Like they're asking something. And the ones in the grass are answering." He stopped, and looked at his feet. "And I wanted to take a shortcut right through the middle of them."

Benny did not write anything down. Maggie Mae patted Rusty's paw. And that was all anybody needed to say about it.

"So," said Maggie Mae, who liked a list. "No big lanterns. No drums. No talking. No shortcuts. We stay on the path, we sit down, and we keep still."

"And we wait," said Sadie Belle.

"How long for?" said Reba.

Sadie Belle thought.

"As long as it takes," she said.

There was a pause in the barn.

"I've never kept still for as long as it takes," said Barney honestly. "I don't think I can."

"Then we'll practise," said Charlie. "Now. Everybody. Like the game Sadie Belle taught us in the snow, last winter. Sit down and keep still and don't say anything, and we'll see who can do it the longest."

So they practised, there and then, on the dusty floor of the Great Barn. Everybody sat down in a row, and folded their paws, and kept perfectly still.

Barney lasted eleven seconds, and then he had to tell everybody how well he was keeping still. Beau lasted a little longer, but he kept opening one eye to see whether he was winning, and Maggie Mae said that did not count. Reba's tummy rumbled so loudly that everybody laughed and had to start again. Benny kept still for a very long time, but he was counting under his breath the whole while, and Maggie Mae said that was not keeping still, that was arithmetic.

Brody was the best at it. Brody sat like a great soft rock, with his eyes half closed, and did not move a whisker, and after a while a small brown moth flew in through the barn door and landed on his ear, and he did not even notice.

"How do you do it?" said Charlie, impressed.

Brody opened one eye.

"You stop waiting for it to be over," he said. "And just sit."

They practised all afternoon. By teatime, even Barney could manage a whole minute.

Up in the high loft doorway of the Great Barn, in the afternoon sun, Maverick had been listening to all of this with his head on one side.

"I have a clue too," he said. "About the dark. The first night Rusty saw them, there wasn't any moon. Tonight there's a moon. A young one. Very thin. Just a sliver, low in the west, over Weather Ridge. But it goes down very soon after the sun. It'll grow a little bigger every night, and set a little later, so tonight's the best of it." He looked pleased with himself. "If you wait until it's gone down, it'll be properly dark."

"Maverick," said Sadie Belle. "That's a very good clue."

"I know," said Maverick modestly.


Chapter Seven — The Thin Moon

That evening, they went back for the third time.

They met at the Great Barn at sundown, as before. This time Maverick's mother had come too, and settled in the loft doorway beside him, so that he could stay and watch, and spend the night safe in the barn with her, instead of flying home in the dark.

Sadie Belle's mother had brought a big soft rug, folded over her shoulder, to spread on the path so that nobody would need to sit in the long grass. Rusty's father had brought nothing at all except a pocketful of patience, he said, which was all anybody really needed. And Great-Grandpappy Buck had come in his old cap, with his lantern, walking slowly, as he always did, and nobody hurried him, because tonight, for once, nobody was in a hurry. Maverick flew up to the loft doorway, early, and settled himself there, wide awake and very determined to stay that way. And then everybody set off across the meadow on the mown path, in a long, quiet line.

It was a very different line from the first night.

Nobody talked. Nobody swished. Barney had left his drum at home. There was no big lantern. Benny had his slate, but he carried it close to his chest so that it would not clatter. And the three grown-ups had turned their lantern wicks right down low, and closed the little tin shutters on three sides, so that only a soft, dim glow came out of one side, just enough to see the path by, pointing down at their feet and nowhere else.

Over Weather Ridge, low in the deep blue sky, there was the young moon, exactly as Maverick had said. A thin silver curve, like a fingernail, hanging just above the dark line of the ridge.

They got to the end of the path at the edge of the woods. Sadie Belle's mother spread the rug on the mown path, and everybody sat down on it, very carefully, in a row. The grown-ups set their shuttered lanterns down behind them on the ground. And then, one by one, Great-Grandpappy Buck turned each lantern down until it went out.

It was dark. Really dark. Charlie found Maggie Mae's paw, and held it. Brody sat close on her other side, very large and warm and comfortable.

And they watched the moon.

Slowly, slowly, the thin silver curve sank lower over Weather Ridge. It touched the top of the ridge. It slid down behind it, a little at a time, until there was only a bright silver tip left above the trees.

Then that was gone too.

And the whole of Fern Meadow went dark, from one edge to the other, under a sky full of stars.

Nobody moved. The creek chattered on among the roots. Somewhere far off, an owl called, once. The grass smelled warm and sweet. And everybody sat in a row on the mown path, perfectly still, perfectly quiet, waiting.

Barney did not say one word about how still he was keeping. Beau kept both eyes on the woods. Reba's tummy began to rumble, very softly, and she pressed both paws against it, hard, until it stopped. Benny held his slate on his knees and did not count anything at all, because there was nothing yet to count. And Brody sat like a great soft rock at the end of the row, as he had in the barn, and just sat.

For a long while, nothing happened at all.

Then, just in front of them, among the dark trees beyond the creek, one small light blinked.


Chapter Eight — The Breathing Wood

Then another.

Then a dozen, here and there, out of time, like the first few raindrops before a shower.

Then a hundred.

And then, all at once, every single light in the woods flashed together.

Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash.

And went dark.

Everybody on the path went perfectly still. Benny made a mark on his slate by feel. Then another. Then four more.

The dark went on. One second. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven.

And then, all at once, the whole edge of the wood lit up again. Flash, flash, flash, flash, flash, flash. Hundreds and hundreds of pale golden-green lights, all together, hanging in the dark among the trunks and the ferns, high and low and far back into the woods, further than anybody could see, all flashing at exactly the same moment.

And then darkness again. Every single one. All together.

It was like watching the wood breathe. In, with light. Out, with dark. In. Out. Slow and steady and enormous, all the way along the creek, as far as you could see.

Every one of them stayed exactly where they were. Charlie was holding Maggie Mae's paw so tightly that Maggie Mae's fingers had gone numb, and Maggie Mae did not mind in the least.

Then, in one of the dark moments, Sadie Belle touched Rusty's arm, very lightly, and pointed down.

In the long grass beside the path, close to their feet, there was a soft little glow. Then another one, a little way off. Low down, in the grass. Two quick little glows, and then quiet.

They came just after the woods went dark. Every time. As if the wood had asked something, and the grass was answering.

Rusty looked at the little glow in the grass, and then at the great breathing wood, and then back at the grass. He did not say anything. But very, very carefully, he tucked his white-tipped tail in close against his side, so that it would not brush against a single blade of the long grass, and kept it there.

Up in the high loft doorway of the Great Barn, at the edge of the dark meadow, Maverick sat beside his mother with his eyes wide open, watching the far-off edge of the woods flash and go dark, flash and go dark, like a great slow heartbeat. And he did not close his eyes once, though it was long past an eagle's bedtime, because he had never seen anything like it in all his life, not even from the very top of the sky.


Chapter Nine — Here I Am

They watched for a long time. Nobody wanted to go. But at last, Great-Grandpappy Buck put a gentle paw on the nearest shoulder, and then the next, and everybody knew without being told that it was time.

They did not light the lanterns again. Not right away. They got up very slowly, very quietly, and turned round, and walked away along the mown path in the dark, in a long careful line, with the grown-ups going first, and Buck at the back. Nobody swished. Nobody stepped off the path. Not one of them spoke.

Behind them, the wood went on breathing. Flash, and dark. Flash, and dark.

Only when they were halfway back, near the Great Barn, did Great-Grandpappy Buck light the lanterns again, turned down low, and let a little light back out onto the path. And only then did anybody speak.

"Six," said Benny, in a whisper, holding up his slate in the lantern light. It was covered in chalk marks, in neat little groups. "Six flashes, mostly. Sometimes seven. Then dark for about six or seven seconds. Every time." He looked at the marks with deep contentment. "It's a pattern. A real one."

"I kept still," said Barney, sounding astonished at himself. "For ages. And I didn't tell anybody once."

"You're telling us now," said Charlie.

"That's allowed now," said Barney, and told them all about it, at length, all the way to the barn.

"It was the most beautiful thing I've ever seen," said Maggie Mae softly.

Rusty's father, walking just ahead with his lantern, put a paw on Rusty's shoulder, and left it there.

"They were talking," said Rusty. "The ones in the trees and the ones in the grass. Weren't they? Talking in lights."

"I think so," said Sadie Belle. "I think the ones in the trees were saying, here I am, here I am. All together, so they could see which ones were answering. And the ones in the grass were saying, here I am too." She looked back across the dark meadow at the far-off flickering of the wood.

They came to the Great Barn. Maverick was still sitting in the loft doorway, wide awake, his white head shining faintly in the lantern light.

"I saw it," he said, in a voice quite unlike his usual one. "All of it. From up here. The whole wood, going on and off, all together." He was quiet for a moment. "I've flown over those woods a thousand times. In daylight. Right over the top of them."

Great-Grandpappy Buck stood in the doorway of the Great Barn, with his lantern in his paw, and looked back across the dark meadow at the woods, still breathing in the distance.

Then he said, "The good keeper takes little, gives much, and treads soft."


Chapter Ten — A Plain Brown Beetle

The next morning, the whole Hollow was late getting up.

It was a slow, yawning, rumpled sort of morning, the kind that comes after a very late night. Benny and Barney came down to breakfast with their fur on end. Reba ate her breakfast with her eyes shut. Maverick, who had stayed the night in the rafters of the Great Barn with his mother, because it was far too late and too dark for an eagle to fly home, came out blinking into the sunshine at nearly ten o'clock, and said it was the latest he had ever got up in his life, and that he did not regret a single minute of it.

On Grandpappy's Bridge, Benny was copying the marks from his slate into his notebook, very neatly, in rows, under the heading Fireflies, All Together. And Barney's drum, by general agreement, had been put away at the back of the brothers' lodge, until such time as somebody needed a drum for something that was not looking at fireflies.

And Sadie Belle and Rusty walked down the creek path together, with Sadie Belle's mother not far behind, to the edge of the woods.

In the daylight, it looked like nothing at all. Just trees, and ferns, and the creek chattering over the stones, and the long grass of the meadow running up to the trunks, green and gold and full of bees. You would never have known that, a few hours before, it had been full of light.

Sadie Belle found the big fern by the roots, where Maverick had pointed. She bent down, very gently, and looked underneath it, without touching it.

The little beetle was there. The same one, or one just like it. Plain, and brown, and dark, with its little rosy collar, and its pale waxy tail, clinging to the underside of the frond in the cool green shade.

It did not look like anything very special at all.

Rusty crouched down beside her and looked at it for a long while.

"Last night," he said softly, "that was one of the lights. Wasn't it? One of the hundreds."

"Maybe," said Sadie Belle.

The little beetle shifted, very slightly, on the underside of the fern. It folded its soft wing cases a little tighter. And then it settled, and was still, in the deep green shade where the sun could not reach it, to wait out the long bright day until the dark came round again.

Sadie Belle and Rusty stood up, very slowly, and stepped back onto the path, and walked away along the creek, stepping as softly as they knew how.

Behind them, all along the shady edge of the woods, under hundreds and hundreds of ferns and leaves, hundreds and hundreds of plain brown beetles slept the summer morning away, and dreamed whatever fireflies dream.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
