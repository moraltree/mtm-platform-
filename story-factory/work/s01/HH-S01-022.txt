Chapter One — Through the Hedge

In the first week of June, Charlie found a garden that nobody had seen for years.

She had not meant to find it. She had been playing hide-and-seek with Barney along the edge of the Hickory Woods, and she had squeezed into the thickest hedge she could find, where nobody would ever think to look, and wriggled, and wriggled, and come out on the other side.

And stopped.

On the other side of the hedge there was a garden. A real garden, with a wall round it, and a gate. Or there had been, once. Now it was all overgrown. Brambles had climbed over the wall and tumbled down inside it in great green waves. Ivy had swallowed the gate. Nettles stood as high as Brody. Wild honeysuckle had wound itself round everything, and the long grass was full of buttercups, and right in the middle, half hidden by the tangle, Charlie could see the roof of a little old cottage, with moss on its stones and a chimney with no smoke.

There was a smell, too, drifting through the warm June air: roses, and something green and sharp, like mint, and something sweeter underneath, that Charlie did not know the name of.

It was very quiet. The only sound was a low, steady hum, somewhere in the middle of the tangle, like a kettle just starting to think about boiling.

Charlie stood very still, as she had learned to that winter, and listened to the hum.

Then she wriggled back through the hedge as fast as she could go, and ran all the way to Rose Arch Cottage.

"Maggie Mae!" she said. "Maggie Mae, I've found a secret garden!"

Maggie Mae was in her own garden, tying up sweet peas. She looked up.

"A what?"

"A secret garden," said Charlie, bouncing. "Behind the big hedge, at the edge of the woods. With a wall, and a gate, and a cottage, all grown over. And something humming in the middle."

Maggie Mae put down her string slowly.

"Behind the big hedge," she said. "At the edge of the woods. A cottage with a mossy chimney?"

"Yes!"

"Oh, Charlie," said Maggie Mae softly. "That's the old cottage. That's where the miller's wife grew up. Her mother lived there, years and years ago. I've never been inside the garden. Nobody has, since her mother died. The hedge grew right over the gate."

Charlie stared at her.

"So it isn't secret?"

"It's not a secret," said Maggie Mae. "It's just forgotten. That's a different thing." She stood up, and brushed the earth off her paws. "And it isn't ours. It belongs to the miller's wife. So before anybody goes wriggling through any more hedges, we're going to ask her."


Chapter Two — The Miller's Wife

They found the miller's wife sitting in her big chair by the Mill door, in the sun, with her shawl round her shoulders and a cushion behind her bad back, sorting beans.

Maggie Mae told her what Charlie had found.

The miller's wife stopped sorting beans. She sat very still, with a bean in her paw, looking away up the valley towards the edge of the Hickory Woods.

"Mother's garden," she said at last. "Oh my. Mother's garden." She put the bean down. "It was the loveliest garden in the Hollow, when I was a girl. Roses on the wall, and herbs by the door, and a vegetable bed for every season, and in the middle..." She stopped. "In the middle, Mother kept bees."

"Bees!" said Charlie. "That's the humming!"

"Is it humming?" The miller's wife looked at her. "Still?"

"Lots," said Charlie.

The miller's wife was quiet for a while.

"After Mother was gone, I meant to look after it," she said. "I truly did. But there was the Mill, and the little ones when they came, and then my back went, and I couldn't kneel any more. And every year I said, next year. And the hedge grew over the gate, and I couldn't bear to go and look at what it had become." She shook her head. "And now it's a jungle, I expect."

"It is a bit of a jungle," said Charlie honestly.

Maggie Mae knelt down beside the big chair.

"Would you let us look after it?" she said. "All of us. The youngsters. We'd clear it, and dig it, and plant it, and keep it nice. For you. If you'd let us."

The miller's wife looked at her.

"You'd do that?" she said. "For an old garden that isn't even yours?"

"For you," said Charlie.

The miller's wife reached out and took Charlie's small paw in her big floury one, and held it.

"Then it's yours as much as mine," she said. "Go on. Go and wake it up." And then, as they turned to go: "But the bees. You leave the bees to the grown-ups. Promise me. Not a paw near them."

"Promise," said Charlie and Maggie Mae together.


Chapter Three — Every Paw

By that evening, every youngster in Hickory Hollow had heard about the secret garden that wasn't a secret. And by the next morning, they were all standing outside the big hedge at the edge of the Hickory Woods, with spades and baskets and buckets and very large ideas.

The miller had come too, with his biggest shears and a pair of thick leather gloves, and Brody's father with a saw and a billhook, because nobody else was allowed to cut brambles, or to touch anything with a blade.

Maggie Mae stood in front of them all with a piece of paper, on which she had made a list.

"Grown-ups first," she said. "They cut a way in through the hedge and clear the gate. Nobody goes through until they say. Then..."

But before she could finish, there was a great flapping of wings overhead, and Maverick came gliding down out of the sky and landed on top of the hedge, which sagged.

"I've been up," he said importantly. "Over the top. Had a look. Sharpest eyes in the Hollow, mine." He shook out his feathers. "Do you know what shape that garden is, under all the brambles?"

Nobody knew.

"It's a wheel," said Maverick. "A great round wheel. You can see it from the air, plain as anything. There's a path all the way round the inside of the wall, and then paths going in from the edge to the middle, like the spokes of a wheel, eight of them. And between the spokes, beds. And in the middle, where the hub would be, there's a round flat place with a row of something standing up on stones." He looked at the hedge. "Can't tell what. Covered in ivy. But that's where all the bees are going in and out."

Maggie Mae looked at her list. Then she turned it over, and drew a circle on the back, with eight lines going into the middle.

"A wheel," she said. "Thank you, Maverick."

"Eight spokes," said Maverick, who liked to be thanked properly. "I counted them twice. From a great height. In a strong wind."

Benny was already looking at Maggie Mae's circle with his head on one side.

"If it's a wheel," he said slowly, "and the gate's here, then the first spoke ought to start just inside the gate, and go straight to the middle. So that's where we should dig first. To find the path."

The grown-ups cut a hole through the hedge, wide enough for Brody, and then they hacked and snipped and sawed at the ivy over the gate, until at last, with a long rusty creak, it swung open.

And everybody went in.


Chapter Four — The Wheel

For three days, the whole Hollow worked in the garden.

The grown-ups did the cutting. The miller and Brody's father, in their thick gloves, chopped back the brambles and the nettles with shears and billhooks, and pulled the ivy off the wall, and heaped it all in great green piles outside the gate. And behind them, wherever they had cleared, the youngsters came in, and did everything else.

Barney dug. He had decided that if there were paths under there, he was going to find them, and he dug with such energy that the earth flew out behind him in a fountain, and Benny, who was trying to measure something, had to wear a bucket on his head. On the second morning, Barney's spade went clunk on something hard, and he scraped away the earth, and there it was: a flat grey stepping stone. And then another. And another, all in a line, going from the gate towards the middle.

"A spoke!" shouted Barney. "I found a spoke!"

Benny took the bucket off his head, and measured the stones, and worked out where the next spoke ought to be, and pointed, and Barney dug there, and found it. By the end of the second day, all eight spokes were uncovered, running in from the wall to the middle, just as Maverick had seen.

Brody carried things. Barrows of weeds out, barrows of compost in, the big stones that had fallen off the top of the wall back up into their places, with his father lifting the heaviest. When they found the cottage's old water butt full of leaves and wriggling tadpoles, Brody's father scooped the tadpoles out in a bucket, and Brody carried the bucket, very carefully, all the way to the beaver ponds, so that the tadpoles could go on to be frogs.

On the second afternoon they met the root.

It was the root of the oldest, biggest bramble in the garden, as thick as Brody's arm, and it ran right across the spoke that went to the cottage door. The miller had cut the bramble down, but the root would not come up. Brody took hold of it and pulled. Nothing happened. Brody pulled harder, and the root let go all at once, just a little, and Brody sat down on the path with a thump. Then Barney took hold of Brody, and Benny took hold of Barney, and Rusty and Beau and Reba took hold of Benny, and Charlie took hold of Reba's tail, and they all pulled together, in a long, wobbly, puffing line, while the miller loosened the earth round it with his spade. And at last, with a great tearing noise, the root came up, and the whole line sat down on the path in a heap, one on top of another, with Charlie at the very bottom, saying, "Did we win?"

Rusty found the old path round the inside of the wall, by following the line where the grass grew differently, which he said was the sort of thing a fox notices.

Reba went along the beds on her hands and knees with her nose to the ground, sniffing, and every time she found something that smelled of anything, she shouted. "Mint! This is mint! And this is thyme. And this one's lemon balm, smell it, it smells like a lemon that's been in the sun. And this one..." She sniffed. "This one's onions. Oh, it's chives. Lovely."

"Don't eat them all," said Maggie Mae.

"I'm only testing," said Reba, with her mouth full of mint.

Sadie Belle found the roses. Under the brambles along the sunny wall, there were old rose bushes, half smothered, tall and leggy and wild, but alive, and covered in buds. She cleared the grass round each one, gently, so as not to break anything, and on the third morning, the first of them opened. It was a deep crimson rose, small and flat and frilly, and it smelled so sweet that Reba came running from the other side of the garden with her nose up in the air.

And Charlie, who was the smallest, went into the places nobody else could reach. Under the bushes. Behind the water butt. Into the tangle at the very bottom of the wall, where she found a little rusty trowel with a wooden handle, worn smooth, and a cracked blue jug, and a small stone frog, sitting in the corner of the bed, smiling, as if it had been waiting all those years for somebody to come.

Beau, meanwhile, had been put in charge of carrying the weeds out, and was doing very well, until he picked up a barrow-load of nettles with his bare paws, and spent the rest of the afternoon sitting in the shade with his paws in a bucket of cold water, saying "Ow," quietly, at intervals.


Chapter Five — The Hub

On the third afternoon, the grown-ups cleared the middle.

They did it slowly, and quietly, and carefully, because the humming was louder there than anywhere. The miller put on a broad-brimmed hat with a veil of fine net hanging all round it, and long thick gloves, and he did not hurry, and he did not flap, and he moved as gently as if he were walking through a room full of sleeping babies. Everybody else stood well back, on the far side of the garden, by the gate, as he had told them to.

Charlie wanted to see so badly that she stood on tiptoe on the bottom bar of the gate, and then on the top bar, until Maggie Mae lifted her down and held her paw firmly.

"Why does he have to go so slowly?" whispered Charlie.

"Because bees don't like being surprised," whispered Maggie Mae. "Any more than you do. If he goes slowly and quietly, they'll know he isn't any trouble."

Charlie thought about this, and stood very still, and watched.

The miller cut away the ivy, a little at a time.

And underneath, in the very middle of the wheel, on a round flat place paved with old stones, there was a row of five tall wooden logs, standing on end, each one on its own flat stone, each one with a flat board laid on top of it like a lid. They were hollow logs, grey and old, with a small notch cut near the bottom of each one, like a little door.

Four of them were silent.

But from the little door at the bottom of the fifth, there was a steady, golden, busy coming and going of bees.

"Bee gums," said the miller softly, from under his veil. "That's what they are. Hollow logs from the black gum tree. That's how the old folk kept bees in the mountains, before there were boxes." He looked at the busy log. "This one's been empty for years. But a swarm must have found it. This spring, I'd say. Or last." He stood back, very slowly. "They've moved in all by themselves."

From the gate, everybody watched the bees going in and out of the little door in the old grey log, in the afternoon sun.

"What do we do with them?" whispered Charlie.

"Nothing," said the miller. "Not today. We leave them be, and we let them get used to us, and we don't go near. They'll have a lot of flowers to work, now we've cleared the garden." He came away from the hub, slowly, and took off his veil. "And when they're settled, I'll ask the old folk what's to be done. I'm no beekeeper. But I know who was."

It was at that moment that Great-Grandpappy Buck came through the gate, with his walking-stick, to see how they were getting on.

He stopped just inside the gate, and looked at the garden. At the wheel of stepping-stone paths, and the cleared beds between them, and the crimson roses on the wall, and the herbs by the cottage door, and the five old grey bee gums standing in a row in the middle.

And Great-Grandpappy Buck took off his cap, and held it against his chest, and stood there a while with it in his paws.


Chapter Six — Telling the Bees

When Buck did speak, his voice had gone soft and far away.

"I haven't stood in this garden," he said, "since I was a young beaver."

They all gathered round him.

"It was your grandmother's garden then," he said, turning to the miller's wife, who had just arrived in the cart. "Before it was your mother's. She kept the finest garden in the mountains. And every family in the Hollow had a hand in it. That's what the spokes were for. Eight beds, and every family looked after a bed, turn and turn about, and took home a share of whatever grew. And every so often, of a Sunday evening, whenever there was news enough, everybody came. Old folk and youngsters together. To do the jobs. And to tell the bees."

"Tell the bees?" said Charlie. "Tell them what?"

"The news," said Buck. "That's the old custom. Older than me. Older than the Hollow, maybe. You go to the bees, and you stand quiet by the hive, and you tell them what's happened since you last came. Who's been born. Who's been married. Who's come to live in the Hollow, and who's gone away. All the news. Because the bees are part of the family, the old folk said, and it isn't right to keep things from family." He chuckled softly. "And if you forgot to tell them, the old folk said, the bees might get offended, and fly away."

The youngsters looked at the busy log in the middle of the garden.

"Did you tell them?" said Benny. "When you were small?"

"Every time," said Buck. "I stood right there, by the end log, and whispered. I told them when my little sister was born. I told them when I learned to swim. I told them when I found the biggest chestnut tree on the ridge." He looked at the row of grey logs. "I've thought of it often. I never thought I'd stand here again."

The miller had fetched her in the cart, with her big chair and her cushion, and she was sitting just inside the gate, looking at her mother's garden, with the crimson roses on the wall behind her.

"Mother kept up the telling, after Grandmother," she said. "She told the bees about me, the day I was born. She told them on my wedding day. She'd walk out here in the evening with her shawl on, and stand by the gums, and talk to them, quiet as anything, as if they were old friends." She smiled, a little crookedly. "When she was gone, nobody told the bees. And they went. All of them. Every log empty by the end of that summer." She looked at the one busy log. "And now they've come back."

There was a long, warm, golden quiet in the garden. Only the bees, humming.

Then Charlie said, very quietly, "Can we tell them? The news?"

Everybody looked at the miller's wife.

The miller's wife looked at the five old grey bee gums for a while. Then she smiled.

"I think," she said, "it's high time somebody did."


Chapter Seven — Sunday Evening

They chose a Sunday evening ten days off, in the middle of June, because that was the old way, a Sunday, and because it gave the bees time to settle in their new garden.

In those ten days, the whole Hollow worked. Every family took a bed, between the spokes, just as Buck said they used to, and planted it. Benny and Barney's family planted beans, and Benny measured the rows, and Barney planted the beans so close together that they had to be moved. Reba's family planted herbs, and Reba tested them all. Maggie Mae planted flowers for the bees, the plain open kind with plenty of filling, as Reba had found out that spring. Sadie Belle's family planted lettuces. Brody's family planted marrows, which would grow, Barney said, almost as big as Brody one day. Rusty's family planted strawberries, and Rusty knew a shortcut to them through the hedge which everybody used.

Even Granny Opal came, very shyly, on the third evening, with a basket of seedlings of a kind of mint nobody in the Hollow had ever seen, from the valley she had come from, and planted them by the cottage door. And she stayed, because nobody looked at her twice, and knitted on the cottage step in the evenings while the youngsters worked, and told Charlie the names of all the herbs in the voice of somebody who had grown every one of them herself, long ago, somewhere else.

One evening, Great-Grandpappy Buck showed Benny and Barney the old way to plant leeks, which was to drop each little leek down a deep hole made with a stick, and fill the hole with water, and nothing else, so that only its green tip peeped out, like somebody in a bath up to their nose. Benny measured the distance between every leek. Barney gave every leek a name. By the end of the row, Benny had worked out that they were exactly a paw's width apart, and Barney had run out of names, and was calling them Leek Twelve and Leek Thirteen, and Buck laughed so much that he had to sit down on the wall.

Another evening, Reba's grandmother came, who was very old and very small and almost never went out, and sat on a stool by the herb bed with her eyes closed, and smelled the thyme, and said it smelled exactly like the thyme she remembered from when she had worked in this very bed, as a girl, with the miller's wife's mother. And then she told Reba which herbs went in which of her famous pies, which nobody in the family had known for years, and Reba wrote it all down on a paper bag, and looked as if Midsummer had come early.

And the old folk came too. That was the thing nobody had expected. The miller's wife came every day in the cart, and sat in her big chair by the gate, and told everybody where things used to be, and what her mother had grown, and how. Great-Grandpappy Buck came, and sat on the cottage step, and told the youngsters which beds had been whose, in the old days, and which of their grandparents had grown the best leeks. The old woodchuck from over the ridge, who came back over the low pass the week after the Gathering to visit her cousins, pruned the old roses for them, slowly and carefully, with her own small shears, while Sadie Belle held the basket and learned which canes to take, just by watching.

And all the time, in the middle of the wheel, the bees came and went from their grey log, and grew busier every day, as the flowers opened in every bed.

By the Saturday, the old garden looked as if it had never been forgotten at all.


Chapter Eight — The Telling

On the Sunday evening, just as the sun was going down, every creature in Hickory Hollow came to the garden at the edge of the Hickory Woods.

They came quietly, as Buck had asked them to. They came in through the gate, in twos and threes, grown-ups and youngsters, old folk leaning on younger arms, families carrying stools and cushions, the rabbit family in a long line with the littlest at the back. They walked along the path round the inside of the wall, and stood among the beds, between the spokes of the wheel, all facing the middle, where the five old bee gums stood in a row in the low gold light. The bees were coming home for the night now, slow and heavy with pollen, dropping down out of the evening air in ones and twos, and walking in through the little door at the bottom of the fifth log.

The miller had put on his veil, and stood beside the gums, very still. Nobody else went near. That was the rule.

The miller's wife was sitting in her big chair at the edge of the hub, with Buck beside her on a stool.

"Mother always started," she said quietly. "So I suppose I'd better."

She leaned forward in her chair, towards the busy grey log, and spoke to it, very softly, as if she were speaking to an old friend across a kitchen table.

"Hello, bees," she said. "It's been a long time. I'm sorry nobody came. I want to tell you that Mother's gone, years ago now, and I didn't come and tell you, and I should have. And I want to tell you that the garden's back. And that all these good folk did it. And that we're glad you're home."

She sat back, and nodded to Buck.

Buck leaned on his stick, and told the bees, in his slow, warm, creaky voice, that the old beaver who used to whisper to them by the end log was still here, a bit older, and very glad to see them.

And then, one by one, the youngsters came forward, as far as the line Buck had drawn across the path with his stick, well back, and off to one side of the way the bees flew in and out, and no further, and told the bees their news.


Chapter Nine — The News

Benny told the bees, very precisely, that the Old Mill's wheel had frozen in February and been mended by race-boards, and that it now turned six times a minute.

Barney told the bees about the frozen waterwheel too, but in a great deal more detail, and with a cotton reel in it, and a blanket, and was still going when Maggie Mae gently took his arm and steered him back.

Sadie Belle told the bees, so softly that nobody else could hear, something that made her go pink. Nobody ever found out what.

Rusty told the bees that Beau had found a chestnut tree that wasn't quite dead, and that he, Rusty, had been there, and had seen it with his own eyes.

Beau told the bees that he had tried very hard to be a gentleman, and it had gone wrong, and then it had gone right, and he wasn't entirely sure how.

The littlest rabbit, who had come with her mother and was not entirely sure what bees were for, stood at the line across the path and told them, very loudly, that she could roll down the big hill now, all on her own. Her mother said "Softly," and she said it again, softly, which was nearly as loud.

Reba told the bees that their lemon balm was the best she had ever tasted, and that she would not eat all of it, probably.

Granny Opal came to the line across the path very slowly, holding her shawl round her, and for a moment it looked as if she would not say anything at all, with all the faces behind her. Then she leaned towards the old grey log, and told the bees, in her small creaky voice, that she had come to the Hollow last autumn, a stranger, from over the hills, and that she had planted mint by their door, from her mother's garden, and that she was staying.

Maverick did not come near the gums, because, he said, eagles and bees had an understanding, which was that they kept out of each other's way. But he called his news from the cottage roof, which was that he had flown a message over Weather Ridge twice, and the second time he had got it right.

Beau told the bees that he was sorry about the nettles, which had nothing to do with the bees, but he felt they should know.

Brody stood at Buck's line across the path, very big and very still, and told the bees, in his slow deep voice, that he had made a butterfly garden, and there was bee balm in it, coming into bud, and they were very welcome to it when it flowered. And the bees, as if they had heard, went on humming.

Maggie Mae told the bees about the roses. About the deep crimson one on the sunny wall, and the pale pink one by the cottage door that Sadie Belle had found under the brambles, and the white one that had not flowered yet but was going to, any day now, and how they were all theirs, every one, to do whatever bees liked best with.

And last of all, Charlie came up to the line across the path.

She was the one who had found the garden. Everybody waited to hear what she would say.

Charlie stood very still, as still as she had learned to be on a snowy porch in the winter. She looked at the old grey log, and the bees going in at the little door, one by one, slow and heavy and golden in the last of the light.

And she did not say anything big. She did not tell them about finding the garden, or about wriggling through the hedge. She only leaned forward, a very little way, and whispered, so quietly that only the bees could possibly have heard:

"Thank you for coming back."

Then she went back, and stood beside Maggie Mae, and held her paw.

Great-Grandpappy Buck had heard. Buck heard most things.

He sat on his stool by the miller's wife's chair, and looked round at the old garden in the dusk: the wheel of paths, the eight beds, every one planted by a different family, the roses on the wall, the mint by the door, the five grey gums in the middle, and all the folk of the Hollow, old and young, standing quietly among the flowers.

Then he said, "The Hollow isn't ours to keep. It's ours to hand on."


Chapter Ten — Night in the Garden

The sun went down behind Weather Ridge, and the light went soft and blue over the garden.

Nobody wanted to go home. So for a while, nobody did. The grown-ups lit lanterns and set them on the wall, well away from the bees. The young ones sat on the stepping stones. Reba handed round mint biscuits, made with her family's own mint. The miller took off his veil, and sat down on the cottage step with the little old trowel Charlie had found in his paws, turning it over and over.

Down in the middle of the wheel, the bees had all gone in for the night. The very last one had come home just as the light went, heavy and slow, and walked in at the little door, and stopped on the threshold for a moment, as if to look round at everybody, before it went inside. The humming had dropped to a low, soft, sleepy murmur, deep inside the old grey log, like the sound of somebody singing to themselves in another room.

Charlie sat on the stepping stone nearest to the hub, as close as the rule allowed, and listened to it.

"What are they doing in there?" she whispered to Maggie Mae.

"Fanning," said Maggie Mae softly. "Some of them stay up all night, fanning their wings, to keep the honey just right. The rest of them sleep."

"Do they talk about the news?"

Maggie Mae smiled.

"I expect so," she said. "I expect they're telling each other about Barney's cotton reel."

Charlie giggled, very quietly, so as not to disturb them.

One by one, the families of the Hollow gathered up their baskets and their lanterns and went home through the gate, along the edge of the Hickory Woods, saying goodnight to each other in low voices. The miller lifted his wife into the cart, and her big chair after her, and she looked back at the garden over her shoulder, at her mother's roses, and the five grey gums in the dusk.

"Whenever there's news," she said. "Of a Sunday. Everybody."

And everybody said they would.

Great-Grandpappy Buck walked beside the cart as far as the gate, with his paw on the side of it.

"Your mother would have liked this," he said quietly. "All of them. In her garden."

The miller's wife looked at him for a while in the lantern light.

"Mother told me something about you, you know," she said. "When I was little. She said Grandmother used to tell the bees, there's a young beaver comes of a Sunday and whispers to the end log, and he's ever so serious about it." She smiled. "They knew all about you, those bees."

Buck laughed, and patted the cart, and stood aside to let it go.

Charlie went home with Maggie Mae, last of all, holding her paw, with Charlie's mother walking ahead of them with her lantern along the path. At the gate, Charlie turned round, once, and looked back.

The old garden lay quiet in the dark, inside its wall. The roses smelled sweeter than ever in the cool of the night, and the little stone frog sat smiling in the corner of the bed, exactly where it had sat all those forgotten years.

And in the middle of the garden, deep inside the old grey log, the bees of Hickory Hollow went on humming softly to each other in the dark, as if they had a great deal of news to share, and all night to share it in.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
