Chapter One — The Trouble with Pebble Run

There is a little stream in Hickory Hollow called Pebble Run, and it is the most cheerful nuisance in the valley.

It comes tumbling down out of the Hickory Woods, chattering over its stones, and joins Silver Creek just below the beaver ponds. It is not a big stream. At its edges it would barely cover a chipmunk's knees, and even in the middle, where it runs fastest, it only comes up to a young beaver's tummy. But it is too wide to jump, about as wide as three beavers lying nose to tail, and it lies right across the path between the beaver lodges and the hickory groves. In autumn, everybody in the Hollow wants to cross it with a basket full of nuts.

For as long as anyone could remember, the way across Pebble Run had been four flat stepping stones. And for as long as anyone could remember, those stones had been just a little too far apart, and just a little too mossy, and just a little too wobbly in the middle.

"Every year," said Benny, "somebody falls in."

He was sitting on the bank with his chin on his paw, looking at the stones the way some creatures look at a puzzle. Benny was a young beaver, sturdy and thoughtful, with rich brown fur and a carpenter's pencil tucked behind one ear. He liked things done properly. He liked plans, and measurements, and knowing why.

"Every year," agreed Barney, "and it's usually me."

Barney was Benny's younger brother, rounder and scruffier, with fur that never lay flat and a grin that was always halfway to a laugh. He was standing in the middle of Pebble Run at that moment, up to his tummy in cold water, because he had just slipped off the third stone.

"You don't count," said Benny. "You do it on purpose."

"Not always," said Barney. "Only most of the time."

That very morning, Reba the raccoon had slipped off the second stone with a whole basket of hickory nuts. The nuts were still in their thick green-brown husks, and husks float like little corks, so every one of them had gone bobbing away down Pebble Run towards Silver Creek like a fleet of tiny boats. Reba had chased them along the bank, catching them one by one, and had arrived where Pebble Run meets Silver Creek with her pink bow over one eye and her basket only half full.

"I've decided," Benny announced. "We're going to build a footbridge."

Barney stopped splashing.

"A real one?"

"A proper one. Measured. Planned. A plank bridge, from this bank to that one. No more stepping stones. No more nuts going down the stream. No more Barney falling in."

"Oh," said Barney, a little sadly. "That last bit's a shame."

But his eyes had begun to shine, because a bridge is a very exciting thing to build. Beavers have been building beside water for as long as there have been beavers, and their own Great-Grandpappy Buck had helped to build half the bridges in the valley.

"Can it have a slide?" said Barney.

"No."

"Can it have a swing underneath?"

"No."

"Can it have a little bell, so you can ring it when you get to the middle?"

Benny opened his mouth to say no, and then paused.

"I'll think about the bell," he said.

From the far bank came the sound of somebody crunching. Reba was sitting under a hickory with her half-basket, picking the husks off the nuts and peering at each one as if it might have a secret inside.

"A bridge?" she said. "Ooh. Can I help? What's it made of? How does it stay up? Does it go all the way across, or just most of the way? Can I see the plan?"

Reba was a young raccoon with soft grey-brown fur, a black mask across her bright eyes, a beautiful ringed tail and a red-and-pink flowered neckerchief. She was the most curious creature in the Hollow, and quite possibly the hungriest, and she had never in her life seen a cupboard, a basket or a plan she did not want to look inside.

"You could test it," said Benny, who had been wondering how to answer all of her questions at once. "If you can get across without falling in, anybody can."

"I'm a very good faller-in," said Reba proudly. "I'll be the best tester you ever had."

"Then that's settled," said Benny, taking the pencil from behind his ear. "Reba tests. Barney helps. And I plan."


Chapter Two — Benny's Plan

That evening, Benny sat at the kitchen table in the brothers' lodge and drew the bridge.

He drew it from the side and from the top. He wrote down how wide Pebble Run was, because he had measured it that afternoon, twice. He drew one long, strong plank running from bank to bank, resting at each end on a flat stone. He drew a little rail along one side for holding on to. And at each end of the plank he drew a short, gentle slope with small wooden slats across it, like the rungs of a ladder laid flat, so that little paws and wet boots would never slip getting on or off.

"Slats," he said to himself, pleased. "Nobody's thought of slats."

Barney, leaning on his elbow, watched him.

"Why's there nothing in the middle?" he asked.

"Because of the spring," said Benny. He had thought about this very carefully. "When the snow melts on High Ridge, Pebble Run turns brown and fast and roaring. It rolls stones along the bottom like marbles. Anything standing in the middle of it gets knocked flat. So the bridge has to jump right over, bank to bank, with nothing in the water at all."

"Oh," said Barney. It was a good reason. He could not think of anything to say back.

So he drew his own bridge instead, on the back of an old seed packet. It had a slide, a swing, a bell, three flags, a little roof for when it rained, and a trapdoor in the middle that dropped you into the water if you said the secret password.

Benny looked at the seed packet for a long moment.

"The bell's not bad," he admitted.

"Can we start building now?" said Barney.

"Tomorrow. I've still got to work out how thick the plank needs to be."

"Can't we just get a big plank and see?"

"No," said Benny. "That's how you get a bridge that breaks."

"But it's so slow," said Barney, and he flopped his head down on the table with a groan, and lay there while Benny did sums.

The next morning they went to Great-Grandpappy Buck's workshop, which stood off the side of his lodge beside Silver Creek. It was full of the good clean smell of sawdust and wood-oil, and the tools hung in rows on their pegs, every one in its place.

Buck was at his workbench in his dungarees, with his spectacles on the end of his nose, mending a little stool. He looked at Benny's drawings for a long time without saying a word.

"Slats," he said at last. "Well now. I wish I'd thought of slats."

Benny glowed.

Buck went to the back of the workshop and brought out a long, pale, beautiful plank of seasoned hickory.

"I've been saving this for something worth building," he said. "I'll cut it to your length, because the saw stays in my paws. And I'll come down when you lay it."

He measured it twice, exactly to Benny's numbers, and sawed it slowly and steadily while the brothers stood well back. When it was done, it was so long that it took both of them, one at each end, to carry it down to Pebble Run.

Reba was waiting on the bank with a basket heaped right to the top.

"For testing," she said. "If they get across, the bridge works."

"And if they don't?" said Barney.

Reba patted the basket thoughtfully.

"Then we'll have learned something," she said. "And I'll have had a snack."


Chapter Three — The Bridge That Bounced

Laying the plank went beautifully.

Benny set a flat stone on each bank, exactly where the drawing said. With Barney at one end and Benny at the other and Buck watching from the bank, they lowered the plank across Pebble Run until it rested on both stones, as neat and straight as a ruler laid across a page.

"Measured twice," said Benny, very quietly, and he could not stop smiling.

"Can I test it?" said Reba. "Now?"

"Go on," said Benny proudly. "Right across."

Reba stepped up the little slatted slope, which did not slip at all, and walked out along the plank with her ringed tail held out behind her for balance.

For the first few steps, all was well.

Then she reached the middle.

The plank went down. Not far. Not dangerously. But it bent in the middle like a branch with a bird on it, and when it came back up it came up a little too fast, and Reba went up with it. Her feet left the plank for a moment. When she came down, the plank went down. When it came up, she went up. And so she went boing, boing, boing across Pebble Run like a very surprised ball.

"Oooh!" said Reba. "Oooh! Oooh!"

Hickory nuts flew out of the basket at every bounce, and came down in the water, plip, plop, plip, and went bobbing off downstream in their husks.

Reba stepped off at the far end and stood very still with her fur standing on end.

Barney was lying on the near bank, laughing so hard he could not breathe.

"Do it again!" he gasped. "It's the best bridge in the whole world!"

"It is NOT," said Benny. His ears had gone hot. "Bridges don't bounce."

"This one does," said Reba, peering into her basket. There were six nuts left in it. Then she put the basket down and lay on her tummy on the bank and looked underneath the plank, very hard, with her nose almost touching the water. "Why does it go down in the middle and not at the ends?"

Benny stared at the plank. He knew the answer. He had known it all along, in a way.

"Because the ends are resting on stones," he said slowly, "and the middle isn't resting on anything. I worked out how thick it had to be so it wouldn't break. I didn't work out how thick it had to be so it wouldn't bend." He sat down on the bank. "They're not the same."

"Make it thicker?" said Reba.

"Twice as thick is twice as heavy," said Benny gloomily. "We could hardly carry this one."

Great-Grandpappy Buck said nothing. He only sat on the bank with his paws folded on top of his walking-stick and waited, the way he did when he thought somebody was about to find something out.

Barney had stopped laughing. He sat up and looked at the bridge, and at the water, and at the low place in the middle of the plank, with his head on one side.

"What if," he said, "we put a stone in the middle? Under the plank. So the middle's got something to rest on too."


Chapter Four — Two Ways of Thinking

Benny shook his head at once.

"No. That's the whole point. I told you. In spring, Pebble Run rolls stones like marbles. A pile of stones in the middle would be gone by the Blossom Gathering."

"Not if beavers build it," said Barney.

"Beavers build dams. Dams are wide and low and full of sticks and mud. A pier's tall and thin and stands all on its own in the fast part. It's not the same."

"You haven't even tried it!"

"I don't need to try it. I've thought about it."

"You always want to think about things," said Barney, and his grin had gone. "You think and think and measure and measure, and you never just try anything."

"And you always want to just try things," said Benny, getting up, "and you never think about them at all. That's why you're always falling in!"

They stood glaring at each other across the end of the plank.

Reba looked from one brother to the other and quietly ate a nut, because it seemed like the sort of moment where it was better to have your mouth full.

At last Great-Grandpappy Buck cleared his throat.

"Benny," he said mildly. "When the spring flood comes down Silver Creek, what happens to the big dam below the ponds?"

Benny frowned. "Nothing. It holds. It always holds."

"And why's that, do you suppose?"

Benny opened his mouth to answer, and stopped. He thought about the big dam. He had walked along the top of it a hundred times. He pictured it in his mind, the way it sat in the creek.

"Because it isn't straight across," he said slowly. "It's curved. It bends upstream. So the water pushes against it and slides along it, instead of hitting it flat."

Buck said nothing. He only waited.

"So if a pier was shaped like that," said Benny, and now he was talking more to himself than to anybody, "pointed at the upstream end, like the front of a boat, then the water wouldn't hit it. It would split round it. It'd slide past both sides." He looked at the water. "And if it was built heavy at the bottom and packed tight, beaver-fashion, the flood couldn't roll it."

"That's what I said," said Barney.

"You said put a stone in it."

"Well, a pointy one."

Benny looked at his brother. Then, despite himself, his mouth twitched.

"All right," he said. "We'll try it. But we'll do it properly. Pointed upstream. Packed tight. Measured."

"And we'll start today," said Barney.

"And we'll start today," said Benny.

They did not quite shake paws on it. But Barney bumped Benny with his shoulder, and Benny flicked a little splash of water at Barney with his tail, which with beaver brothers is very nearly the same thing.


Chapter Five — Barney's Shortcut

Building a pier is cold, hard work, even for beavers, and Great-Grandpappy Buck was very clear about the rules. They could work only in the shallow part, never deeper than their tummies. They could work only while he was sitting on the bank. And nobody was to put their head under the water.

"Feel for the stones with your feet and paws," he said, settling onto his folding stool with a flask of hot cordial. "I'm not here to tell you how. You've got beaver paws. I'm only here in case you need an old beaver's opinion. And to make sure nobody catches cold."

Benny went off to the clay bank further up Pebble Run, to fetch good sticky clay for packing.

Barney was meant to wait.

He did not wait.

He waded out to the middle of Pebble Run and began to find stones. He had a gift for it. He would feel about on the bottom with his toes and then reach down with both paws and come up with exactly the right stone: flat on the bottom, flat on the top, heavy enough to stay put. He did not measure anything. He simply piled them, one on another, as fast as he could find them, faster and faster, until by the time Benny came back with his arms full of clay, there was a tall, proud, wobbly tower of stones in the middle of the stream.

"Look!" cried Barney. "I've done it! I've done it all!"

Benny put down the clay.

He looked at the tower. He took the measuring stick from his belt, waded out, and held it up beside the stones. Then he looked at the plank on the bank, and back at the tower.

"It's too tall," he said. "By about the height of four stones. If we put the plank on that, it'll go uphill from both banks, like a little hill. And it isn't pointed at the front. And it isn't packed. It's just a heap."

Barney looked at his tower. Now that he looked properly, it was rather a lopsided heap.

"Oh," he said. "I was just trying to get on with it."

"I know," said Benny. And then, a little more gently, "It's good stones, though. They're all good stones."

So they took the top four stones off. Benny measured the depth of the water and the height of the banks and worked out exactly how tall the pier must be. Barney rebuilt it, more slowly this time, putting each stone where Benny's measurements said, and shaping the upstream end into a point, like the prow of a boat. And Reba packed the gaps.

Raccoons have the cleverest paws in the Hollow, and Reba could press handfuls of clay and little pebbles into the cracks far more neatly than either brother. "Like stuffing a pie," she said happily, with clay up to her elbows.

Then she stopped stuffing, and lay down on the bank, and watched the water.

"Look," she said. "Look at the leaves."

Leaves were coming down Pebble Run, red and yellow, riding the current. When they reached the pointed end of the pier, they did not bump into it. They slid gently to one side or the other, and went sailing on past.

"It's going round," said Reba, wonderingly. "The water isn't pushing it. It's just going round."

Benny and Barney stood up to their tummies in Pebble Run and watched the leaves slip past their pier, and neither of them said a word, because there was nothing that needed saying.

When the pier was done, they swung the long plank back out over the stream, one brother at each end, and lowered it down onto the bank stones and the top of the pier. It settled with a soft, solid thunk.

"Reba," said Benny. "Test it."

Reba walked to the middle. She stopped. She did a small hop.

Nothing happened. The plank did not bounce. It did not sag. It simply lay there, being a bridge.

"Not a wobble!" said Reba.

Then she stepped off the far end, and the plank gave a long, slow, squeaky creeeeeak, and quietly tilted sideways.


Chapter Six — The Bridge That Tilted

The plank had not fallen. It had simply rolled a little on the top of the pier, so that now it sat at a slant, like a picture hung crooked on a wall.

Reba stepped back onto it. It rocked under her. She stepped off again quickly.

"That's a new kind of wobbly," she said. "Sideways wobbly."

Benny waded out and looked at the top stone of the pier. It was the very last one, the one that had needed to be exactly the right height. All the flat stones of that thickness had been used lower down. This one was the right height, but its top was rounded, like a loaf of bread.

"It's the top stone," he said. "The plank's balancing on it like a seesaw."

"We'll find a flatter one," said Barney.

"There isn't a flatter one that's the right height. You've brought up every stone in Pebble Run."

Benny waded back to the bank and sat down hard and put his head in his paws. All the happy, buzzing feeling of the leaves sliding past the pier had drained out of him, and left him cold and tired and fed up.

"It bounced," he said into his paws, "and now it tilts, and tomorrow it'll probably get up and walk off down the stream on its own. This bridge doesn't want to be built."

The water went on chuckling round the pier. The sun went on sliding down behind Weather Ridge, turning everything orange.

Barney climbed out of the stream and sat down next to his brother, dripping. He did not splash. He did not say anything funny. He just sat with his wet shoulder against Benny's dry one.

"You know Reba's tummy?" he said, after a while.

Benny lifted his head. "What?"

"Reba's tummy," said Barney. "When it rumbles. It's not being rude. It's just saying, feed me! It's telling you what it wants." He waved a paw at the bridge. "So that's the bridge's tummy rumbling. First it said, give me something in the middle! So we did. Now it's saying, give me something flat on top! It's not being naughty. It's just hungry for a flat bit."

There was a pause.

"My tummy doesn't rumble that loudly," said Reba, with dignity.

"It does," said Barney. "It rumbled all through the building."

Benny looked at his brother, all wet and scruffy and pleased with himself, and in spite of everything, he began to laugh.

"A flat bit," he said, wiping his eyes. "Between the round stone and the plank."

"Like a plate," said Reba, "between a pie and a table."

Benny sat up very straight.

"A cap," he said. "That's what it's called. Great-Grandpappy told me once. A block of wood on top of a pier, shaped underneath to sit on the stone and flat on top for the plank."

He jumped to his feet.

"But it'll have to fit perfectly," he said. "Exactly the shape of the stone."

"And who's good at exactly?" said Barney.

Benny grinned at him.

"And who's good at feeling stones?" he said.


Chapter Seven — You Measure It, I Feel It

It was too late to finish that day. The light was going, and Great-Grandpappy Buck said firmly that nobody worked in water after sundown. So they lifted the plank back onto the bank, out of harm's way, and walked home in the dusk with their tails dragging and their heads full of caps.

The next morning, early, they were back.

Making the cap was the hardest part of all.

Benny measured the top of the round stone. He measured it across, and from front to back, and every way he could think of. He drew its shape on a piece of bark. Then Great-Grandpappy Buck cut a block of seasoned oak to that shape with his small saw, while the brothers stood back, and hollowed its underside with his chisel, a shaving at a time, until it matched Benny's drawing exactly.

Benny carried it out to the pier and set it on the stone.

It rocked.

"But I measured it," said Benny. "Three times."

"Can I try something?" said Barney.

Benny hesitated. Then he handed him the block.

Barney did not measure anything. He set the block on the stone and put his paw flat on top of it, and closed his eyes, and rocked it, very gently, side to side and back and forth.

"There," he said, with his eyes still shut. "It's touching here, and here. But not there. There's a little bump on the stone, right under this side, in the middle where you can't see it. Your stick went over the top of it. My paw can feel it."

He opened his eyes.

"You measured it," he said, "and I felt it. That's both halves."

Benny waded out and put his own paw on the block. He rocked it the way Barney had. And there it was: a tiny catch, a little hump, right where Barney had said. He would never have found it with a measuring stick.

"Great-Grandpappy," he called. "Could you take a whisker off this side?"

"Show me where," said Buck.

So Barney showed him where, and Benny measured how much, and Buck shaved exactly that much away. They tried it again. It still rocked, but less.

They tried again, and again. Barney felt for the bumps. Benny measured them. Buck took off a whisker of wood at a time. Reba tested each try by pressing on it with her clever paws and calling out "warmer!" or "colder!" like a game, and asking at every single try why that bit was higher than that bit, until Benny was explaining the whole shape of the stone to her, bump by bump, and found that he understood it better himself for saying it out loud.

On the sixth try, Barney set the block on the stone and rocked it, and it did not move at all. It sat there as snug as a hat on a head.

Benny laid his measuring stick across the top and sighted along it from every side, with one eye shut.

"Flat," he said, and his voice wobbled a little. "Perfectly flat."

"I felt that," said Barney. "It feels like it's sitting down."

Then Benny turned to his brother, and said the thing he had been carrying around since the leaves slid past the pier.

"The stone in the middle was a good idea, Barney. A really good one. I should have listened."

Barney went quite pink under his scruffy fur.

"The measuring was a good idea too," he said. "If I'd done it my way, it'd have been about three stones too tall and pointing the wrong way."

"Four," said Benny. "I measured."

And the two brothers burst out laughing together.


Chapter Eight — Not One Nut

They laid the plank one last time.

It settled onto the bank stones and the oak cap with a soft, solid sound, and sat there as if it had grown there.

Then Benny took a coil of rope from his tool bag.

"Tie the ends to the trees," he said. "So it can't wriggle."

Barney stared at him. "That was my idea! From the first day! You said we'd start with the stone!"

"I did start with the stone," said Benny. "Now I'm finishing with the rope."

They tied each end of the plank to the two hickories on either bank, with good firm knots that Great-Grandpappy Buck checked twice. Then they fitted a smooth rail of willow along one side for holding on to.

"What about my rainbow?" said Barney. "The curved plank? Going up in the middle?"

Benny looked at the bridge. Then he looked at his brother.

"Let's save the rainbow," he said, "for the next bridge."

"There's going to be a next bridge?"

"There's always a next bridge," said Benny.

Then they stood back.

It was not the bridge Benny had drawn. That had been one straight plank, with nothing in the water. It was not the bridge Barney had drawn either. That one had a trapdoor. It was a straight hickory plank with slats at each end, resting on a pointed stone pier with an oak cap, tied snug to the trees, with a willow rail to hold.

"Reba," said Benny. "Please test it."

Reba had saved her fullest basket of all for this moment. It was heaped so high with hickory nuts that she had to peer round the side of it.

She stepped up the slatted slope. She walked to the middle. She stood still, right over the pier, and did a small dance.

The bridge did not bounce, or sag, or tilt, or creak. It only stood there, solid and calm, while Pebble Run chattered away underneath.

Reba walked to the far side, stepped off, turned round, and held up her basket. It was still heaped to the very top.

"Not one nut," she said. "Not one!"

Barney whooped. Benny did not whoop, because Benny did not whoop, but he sat down on the bank very suddenly, as if his legs had decided they had done enough, and smiled at the bridge until his cheeks ached.

News travels quickly in Hickory Hollow, and by the middle of the afternoon half the valley had come to cross the new bridge over Pebble Run. The rabbit family from the end of the lane came, with their ears bobbing. Maggie Mae came with a basket of apples and declared it the most elegant footbridge she had ever seen, and especially admired the slats. Brody crossed it three times, very slowly, because he was the biggest creature there and wanted to be sure it would hold him. It did, and he beamed. Charlie crossed it at a run, and back, and across again, because it was so nice not to fall in.

And Barney, who had fallen into Pebble Run more often than anyone in history, crossed it without getting wet at all, and stood on the far bank looking rather puzzled.

"It's very dry," he said. "I'm not sure I like it."

"You can still go in the stream," said Benny. "Just on purpose now."

"Oh, good," said Barney. And he waded into the shallows below the bridge, up to his knees, and lay back in the water with his paws behind his head, and looked up at the underneath of the bridge he had helped to build.


Chapter Nine — The Bell

That evening, when the last of the nut-carriers had gone home and the light over Weather Ridge had turned the colour of honey, Benny went back down to Pebble Run on his own.

In his paw he was carrying something small and round and shiny. It was a little brass bell. It had sat at the back of a drawer in Great-Grandpappy Buck's workshop for years and years, and that morning, when Benny had gone to fetch the oak for the cap, Buck had taken it out and pressed it into his paw, and said he had been saving it for something worth ringing about. Benny had been turning it over ever since.

He went out to the middle of the bridge, knelt down, and fixed the bell to the willow rail with a twist of wire, right above the pier, where you could reach out and ring it as you went by.

Then he rang it, once, to see.

Ting.

It was a small, bright, happy sound, and it went skipping off across the water like a pebble.

"I knew it!" said a voice from the bank.

Barney was standing in the shadow of the hickory, with Reba beside him. They had come to look for him, and they had seen the whole thing.

"You said no bell," said Barney, delighted. "You said no bell, and no slide, and no swing, and no flags."

"I said I'd think about the bell," said Benny. "I thought about it."

Barney came out onto the bridge. He rang the bell. Ting. Then he rang it again, ting-ting, and then he rang it in a little tune, and Reba laughed, and the sound went floating off down Pebble Run into the dusk.

"Why that bell?" Reba asked. "Out of everything in the workshop?"

"Great-Grandpappy said he'd been saving it," said Benny, "for something worth ringing about."

The three of them stood together in the middle of the bridge and looked down at the water running underneath their feet. The pier split it neatly in two, and it joined up again on the other side, as if nothing had happened.

"Benny," said Barney. "Next bridge. Can it have a slide?"

"No."

"A swing?"

"Probably not."

"A trapdoor?"

"Definitely not."

"Flags?"

Benny thought about it.

"One flag," he said.

Behind them, up the path, a lantern had begun to move. It came bobbing slowly down from Buck's lodge, golden in the blue dusk, and as it came closer, they could see the old beaver himself, in his cap and his dungarees, with his walking-stick in one paw and the lantern held carefully in the other.


Chapter Ten — Two Ears and One Mouth

Great-Grandpappy Buck did not cross the bridge. He settled himself on the big flat stone on the bank, set the lantern down on the rock beside him where it could not tip, and looked at the bridge in the lamplight for a long, long time.

"Well now," he said at last.

The others came drifting down too, one or two at a time, the way friends do in the Hollow when a lantern is lit somewhere. Brody and Charlie came, and Maggie Mae, and Sadie Belle and Beau, and Rusty by a shortcut through the reeds. Maverick came gliding low over the stream and landed on the bridge rail, and the bell went ting all by itself, and he looked very pleased with himself, as if he had meant it.

Barney told them the story. He did the bounce. He did Reba going boing-boing-boing across Pebble Run with nuts flying out of her basket, flapping his arms and bobbing up and down on the bank until he fell over.

Reba laughed so hard she had to hold on to the hickory.

"I didn't go that high," she gasped.

"You went higher," said Benny. "I measured."

Great-Grandpappy Buck waited until the laughing had settled, the way you wait for ripples to settle on a pond.

"You know," he said, "when I was a young beaver, I knew everything. Ask anybody. I was a terrible one for knowing everything." His whiskers twitched. "And my grandpappy used to look at me over his spectacles, just like this, and say: You've got two ears and one mouth, little one. Use them in that order."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Benny looked at Barney. Barney looked at Benny. And then they both looked down at their paws, and smiled.

The light had nearly gone. Over Sunrise Ridge, the first stars had come out, and down in Pebble Run, the same stars were shining in the water, wobbling a little in the current, then steadying, then wobbling again. A cool breeze came up the valley, and the hickory leaves whispered, and somewhere far up in the woods, an owl called once, softly, and was quiet.

"Time we were all home," said Buck, and he picked up the lantern.

So they followed him up the path to his lodge, in a long, slow, sleepy line behind the swaying circle of lamplight. Brody carried Charlie on his shoulders. Maggie Mae and Sadie Belle walked arm in arm. Reba yawned so widely that her mask went crooked. Barney walked on one side of Benny and leaned on him, and Benny let him.

On the porch, Buck hung the lantern on its hook and lowered himself into his old hickory rocking chair. It gave its first slow creak, the creak every youngster in the Hollow knew, the one that meant the day was done. Bramble's quilt went over the smallest knees. Buck took his banjo from its corner and played a few slow notes, no more than that, soft as water going under a bridge, while the families of the Hollow came along the creek path one by one with their own lanterns to fetch their youngsters home.

The brothers were the last to go. They lived nearest the ponds, and Buck walked them to the end of the porch and watched until their lodge door closed behind them.

Down in the dark, the little bridge over Pebble Run stood steady on its pointed pier, with the water sliding past on both sides. And once, just once, as the last lamp went out along the creek, the night breeze found the brass bell on the willow rail and rang it, very softly, all the way up to Buck's porch.

Ting.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
