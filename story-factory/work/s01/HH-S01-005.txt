Chapter One — Leave It to Brody

Every autumn, in the week before the first hard frost, the whole of Hickory Hollow carried its harvest into the Great Barn.

It was a great deal of harvest. There were baskets of apples, red and gold and green. There were heaps of fat orange pumpkins and squashes. There were sacks of corn and sacks of beans, sunflower heads as big as dinner plates, and crates of potatoes still smelling of the earth. All of it had to be carried from the fields and the orchards and the gardens, across Fern Meadow, and in through the tall double doors of the Great Barn, where it would stay safe and dry all winter.

And this year, it seemed to everybody that most of it was being carried by Brody.

"Leave it to me!" Brody would cry, whenever he saw somebody struggling. "I've got it!"

Brody was a young black bear cub, and he was strong. Very strong. He had soft, dark fur, a creamy chest and muzzle, a red neckerchief, and a small green rucksack that he never went anywhere without. He was the gentlest creature in the whole valley, and the friendliest, and he loved nothing more than being useful.

On the first day he carried three sacks of corn at once, one under each arm and one on his head.

On the second day he rolled the biggest pumpkin in the Hollow all the way from the pumpkin patch by the Beaver Ponds to the Great Barn, which took him the whole morning and made him very muddy and very proud.

On the third day, when the rabbit family's cart got stuck in a rut on the meadow path, Brody put his shoulder to the back of it and heaved, and the cart came free with a great squelch, and the rabbits all cheered.

"Brody, you're a hero!" squeaked the littlest rabbit.

Brody went pink under his fur. He had never been called a hero before.

He liked it very much indeed.

On the fourth morning he was out in the orchard before anybody else, lifting baskets of apples onto the cart. And it was there that Charlie found him.

Charlotte the chipmunk was the smallest of the friends, and the youngest, and the most eager. She was carrying an acorn cap full of beans, very carefully, in both paws.

"Brody!" she called. "I'm helping too! Look! Beans!"

Brody looked down at the acorn cap. It held eleven beans.

"That's lovely, Charlie," he said kindly. Then he picked up a whole sack of beans from the orchard wall and swung it onto the cart with one paw. "But don't you worry. I've got the beans."

Charlie looked at the sack, and then at her acorn cap.

"Oh," she said.

She tipped her eleven beans into the top of Brody's sack, so they would not be wasted. Then she stood beside the cart and did not quite know what to do with her paws.


Chapter Two — Too Little

All that day, Charlie tried to help.

She tried to carry an apple. It was nearly as big as she was, so she rolled it, and it rolled downhill and away into the long grass, and Brody had to fetch it.

"Don't worry, Charlie," he said cheerfully, scooping up six apples. "I'll do the apples."

She tried to push the smallest pumpkin. Her paws slipped on its shiny side, and she sat down hard.

"Don't you worry," said Brody, lifting the pumpkin onto his shoulder. "I'll do the pumpkins. They're heavy."

She tried to carry a sunflower head on her back. She staggered three steps before the seeds began to rain down all over her, and she had to stop and pick them out of her fur.

"Charlie," said Brody, very gently, the way you might speak to a much younger cousin, "why don't you let me carry things? You're too little for the heavy stuff. You could cheer instead! I like it when people cheer."

He meant it kindly. Brody never meant anything any other way. He looked at Charlie, who was so small, and at the harvest, which was so big, and thought the kind thing would be to carry it all for her.

So Charlie cheered.

She sat on the orchard wall and cheered every time Brody went past with something enormous. "Hooray for Brody! Hooray!" The rabbits cheered too, and Barney, and soon there was quite a crowd, and Brody grew pinker and prouder and carried bigger and bigger loads.

But after a while, Charlie's cheering got quieter.

She sat on the wall with her chin on her paws and watched everybody else working. Benny and Barney stacked crates on the cart, Benny counting and Barney turning it into a game. Reba sorted apples into baskets with her clever paws, the sound ones in one basket and the bruised ones, for eating straight away, mostly in Reba. Sadie Belle carried bundles of corn on her back. Beau scrambled up the apple trees to shake down the last fruit. Maggie Mae, her very best friend, tied up the bean sacks with neat, careful knots.

Everybody had a job. Everybody except the very smallest one.

Maggie Mae noticed. Maggie Mae always noticed. She came and sat beside Charlie on the wall and straightened her purple gingham bow.

"You've gone quiet, sugar."

"Brody says I'm too little," said Charlie. "For the heavy stuff. And everything's heavy stuff."

Maggie Mae looked across the orchard at Brody, who was at that moment carrying a full basket of apples on his head and waving at the rabbits with both paws.

"Brody's a very good friend," she said slowly. "And a very strong one. But I don't believe strong is the only way to be useful. Not by a long way."

"Then what's my way?" said Charlie.

Maggie Mae did not know the answer. But she put her arm round Charlie, and they sat together on the wall until it was time to go home.


Chapter Three — The Last Day

On the fifth day, Weather Ridge put on its grey cap.

Great-Grandpappy Buck saw it first thing, from his porch. He stood at the rail in his dungarees and his cap, with his spectacles on his nose, and sniffed the air for a long time.

"Hard frost tonight," he said. "First of the year. Sure as sunrise."

By breakfast the whole Hollow knew. Apples can bear a touch of frost, but a hard one would ruin the pumpkins and squashes and spoil the potatoes, and there were still a great many of all three out in the fields. Everything had to be inside the Great Barn before dark.

"Leave it to me!" cried Brody, and he set off for the fields at a run.

It was a long, hard, busy day. Everybody worked. Great-Grandpappy Buck sat at the barn door with a slate and chalk and counted everything in. The littlest rabbit carried one bean at a time and was very solemn about it. Brody carried more than anybody. He did not stop for lunch. Every time somebody went to lift something, Brody was there first.

By the time the sun was sliding down towards Weather Ridge, the fields were bare and the Great Barn was nearly full. The very last cart was coming up the meadow path, heaped with the last of the pumpkins and squashes, the late potatoes, and the big winter apples that kept best of all.

"One more cart," said Buck, ticking his slate. "Then we close up and we're safe for the winter."

He showed Brody the inside of the great double doors. Across the middle of them lay a long wooden latch, fixed on a peg at one end so that it could swing up and down. When the doors were shut, the free end dropped into a wooden notch on the other door, and held them closed. Above it, a loop of cord hung from a nail.

"That loop holds the latch up, out of the way, while the doors stand open," said Buck. "Always keep it looped while we're working. If it slips off, and somebody shuts the doors, the latch drops into the notch. And it's an old latch. It's swollen with the damp this autumn, and it sticks."

"I'll be careful," said Brody.

But first there was a job to do. The barn was so full that the last cart would not fit until the sacks near the door had been moved further back. So Brody went inside to shift them.

The evening wind had come up. It came gusting in through the open doors, whirling straw about, flinging dust into Brody's eyes and tumbling a basket of sunflower heads over and over across the floor. Brody grabbed the doors and swung them nearly shut to keep the wind out, so that only a narrow gap was left. Then he set to work.

He shifted the sacks fast. He shifted them hard. He was tired and hungry and wanted very much to be a hero one last time before dark. He heaved the last sack into place, and as he swung it, the corner of it caught the loop of cord and flicked it off its nail.

Brody did not notice.

He turned, squeezed out through the gap, and pulled the doors closed behind him, all the way, with a tremendous, triumphant BANG.

Inside the barn, something went clunk.


Chapter Four — The Locked Barn

For a moment, nobody moved.

Then Great-Grandpappy Buck put down his slate, very slowly, and came across and laid his paw flat on the doors.

"Brody," he said quietly. "Try them."

Brody tried them. They did not open. He pulled harder. Nothing. The great doors of the Great Barn, which had stood open all week, were shut tight.

"It's stuck," said Brody. "I'll get it."

He put both paws on the handle and pulled with all his strength. The doors creaked. The frame creaked. Somewhere high in the roof, a pigeon took off.

"Brody. Stop now," said Buck, firmly.

Brody let go at once.

"Those hinges are older than I am," said Buck, more gently. "Pull like that and you won't open the doors. You'll pull them off their frame, and the barn will stand open all winter with the snow blowing in. That would be a good deal worse." He laid his paw on the wood. "It's latched. The cord must have come off its nail. You heard it drop."

Brody had heard it drop. Clunk.

"Then we lift it from out here," said Brody.

"Can't," said Buck. "That's the point of a latch. It's on the inside."

The others had gathered round. Benny was frowning at the hinges. Barney was peering through the crack between the doors with one eye.

"How does anybody get in when it's latched?" asked Sadie Belle.

"The latch-string," said Buck. He pointed to a small round hole in the door, about as high as his shoulder. "There's a leather string tied to the latch, and in the old days it hung out through that hole. Pull the string from outside, and up comes the latch, and in you go. But all through harvest week, with the doors standing open, we've kept the string tucked up inside, out of the way of the carts. So it's hanging on the inside now."

Nobody said anything.

Behind them on the meadow path stood the last cart, heaped high with pumpkins and potatoes, glowing in the low red light. Over Weather Ridge the grey cap was coming down. The air had already begun to smell of cold.

"The frost," said Maggie Mae softly.

Brody looked at the doors, and then at his big, strong paws. He had carried more than anybody all week. And with one bang, he had locked the whole harvest away, with the last cart outside it, and there was nothing his strength could do.

"It's my fault," he whispered.

Buck put a paw on his shoulder.

"It was an accident, Brody," he said. "That'll mend. Things that are done by accident usually can be. Let's all think."


Chapter Five — Everybody Tries

They tried everything.

Benny looked at the hinges. "We could take the doors off," he said, "with the right tools, and a ladder, and a whole day." He looked at the sky. "We haven't got a whole day."

Maverick flew up to the hay-loft door, high in the gable end. He pushed at it with his beak.

"Bolted," he called down. "From inside."

"That'd be me," said Buck. "I bolted it this morning, against the wind."

Beau climbed up the corner of the barn, nimble as a squirrel, to the narrow shuttered windows under the eaves. But the gaps between the slats were barely as wide as his paw.

"I can see in!" he called. "It's very full of pumpkins. I can't fit through, though. My head's the wrong shape."

"Your head's a lovely shape," called Sadie Belle, and then went pink and pretended to be looking at something else.

Rusty ran all round the barn twice, looking for gaps at the bottom. He went along the front, and along the sides, and past the great wooden rain-barrel that stood at the back corner. Nothing. The rabbits dug a very small hole and found a very large stone.

The sun touched Weather Ridge and began to sink behind it. The cold came creeping down off the mountains.

"We'll have to cover the cart, then," said Buck quietly. "Blankets and sacking. Every one we can find. It won't keep all the frost off, but it'll keep off some."

Everybody went to fetch blankets.

Everybody except Brody.

He sat down on the step of the Great Barn with his back against the shut doors, pulled his rucksack into his lap, and put his chin on top of it. All week he had said leave it to me. All week he had been the hero. And now there was a problem that strength could not solve, and it was his fault.

He had never felt so big and so useless in his life.

Something small sat down beside him on the step.

He did not look round. He knew who it was from the size of the shadow.

"Hello, Charlie," he said.

"Hello, Brody," said Charlie.

She did not say anything else. She just sat there, swinging her feet, being small and warm and there.

After a while, Sadie Belle came and sat on his other side, folding her long legs under her.

"Everybody makes mistakes, Brody," she said gently. "Even the strongest. Even Grandpappy Buck."

"Not this big," said Brody.

"Bigger," said Sadie Belle. "He told me once about a whole bridge."

Brody almost smiled.


Chapter Six — Behind the Rain-Barrel

After a while, Charlie got up. Sitting still had never been easy for her. She wandered off along the wall of the barn, round the corner, and down the side, trailing one paw against the old grey boards.

At the back corner stood the great wooden rain-barrel, catching water off the roof. Between the barrel and the barn wall there was a gap, much too narrow for a fox or a bear, but just wide enough for a chipmunk. Charlie squeezed in behind it, because it looked like a good place to be on your own for a minute.

And there, low down on the barn wall, hidden from everybody else by the bulge of the barrel, the bottom of one old board had gone soft from years of splashing and crumbled away, leaving a small, dark, ragged hole.

It was exactly the size of a very small chipmunk.

Charlie knelt down and peered in. Thin stripes of red evening light came through the cracks in the walls. She could see straw on the floor, the side of a sack, and the round orange edge of a pumpkin.

She ran back round the barn as fast as her legs would carry her.

"Brody! Grandpappy! Everybody! There's a hole! Behind the rain-barrel! You can't see it unless you're behind it!"

Everybody came running with their arms full of blankets. Great-Grandpappy Buck came last, slowly, and bent down beside the barrel, and looked for a long time.

"Too small for any of us," said Benny.

"Too small for me," said Rusty, measuring it with his nose. "I ran right past it. Twice."

"Not too small for me," said Charlie.

Brody, who had followed her, opened his mouth.

"No, Charlie, you're too lit..." he began.

And then he stopped.

The words hung in the cold air. Too little. He had said them all week. He had said them kindly, and meant nothing but good by them. Too little for the apples. Too little for the pumpkins. Too little for the heavy stuff.

He looked at the hole.

"Oh," said Brody slowly. "You're not too little at all. You're exactly the right size."

Charlie stood up very straight, and her whiskers quivered.

"But what can she do in there?" said Reba. "That latch is stuck. Swollen with the damp. Could you push it up from inside, Charlie?"

Charlie thought about the stuck latch, and about her own small paws.

"I don't think so," she said honestly. "Not if Brody couldn't shift the doors."

Benny was frowning hard at the hole, and then at the front of the barn, and then at the hole again. Suddenly he snapped his fingers.

"She doesn't have to push it," he said. "She only has to get the latch-string. If she can find it, and climb up, and push the end of it back out through its hole in the door..."

"Then somebody can pull it from outside," said Barney. "Somebody really strong."

Everybody looked at Brody.

Great-Grandpappy Buck looked at all of them, and then at the hole, and did not say a word about the plan at all. He only said, "Well now. Let's see if it's safe."

Brody looked at Charlie.

"Together?" he said.

"Together," said Charlie.


Chapter Seven — Inside the Barn

Great-Grandpappy Buck made very sure first.

He sent Beau back up to the shutters to look in and check that nothing in the barn could topple or fall. He asked Maverick to fly up beside Beau, because an eagle's eyes would see best in the dim light, and Maverick reported that the floor by the doors was clear, with a soft pile of loose hay below the latch, and a stack of low potato crates beside it like a staircase.

Then Buck sat himself down right beside the hole, on the cold ground behind the rain-barrel, with a lantern beside him.

"I'll be right here, little one, the whole time," he said. "If you want to come straight back out, for any reason, you come. Being a little bit frightened is perfectly all right. And Brody will be at the doors, on the other side, so you can hear him."

Reba knelt down and tied a loop in the end of a short bit of string from her pocket.

"For luck," she said, and tied it round Charlie's wrist like a bracelet. "So you've got something to hold."

Barney did his best face, which was his eyes crossed and his teeth out and his ears flat, and Charlie giggled, and felt a little braver.

Then she took a deep breath, straightened her bow, and wriggled into the hole.

It was dim inside, and big. Much bigger than it looked from outside. The sacks and crates and baskets towered over her like hills. The stripes of red light came through the cracks and lay across the straw in long thin lines. It smelled of apples and dust and dry corn.

Charlie stood very still. Her heart was going pitter-pat.

Then, from far away across the barn, through the crack in the great doors, came a sound.

It was Brody. He was humming.

It was a slow, gentle tune, one that Great-Grandpappy Buck played on his porch of an evening. Brody hummed it low and warm through the crack, so that it came into the dim barn like a little thread of light.

Charlie followed the humming.

"Left!" called Maverick's voice from up at the shutters. "Round the pumpkins! Now straight on! You're nearly there!"

She went along the gap between the sacks and the wall. She climbed over a pumpkin. She went round a basket of apples. And there in front of her were the great double doors, tall as trees from where she stood, with the wooden latch lying across them in its notch.

And hanging down from the latch, swinging gently, was a little leather string.

"I see it!" Charlie called. "I see it!"

"Well done!" called Brody through the doors. "Can you reach?"

The latch was too high. But beside the doors, just as Maverick had said, stood a stack of low potato crates, like a staircase, with the hay piled soft at the bottom.

Chipmunks are very good at staircases.

She climbed one crate. Two. From the top of the second, standing on her toes, she could just reach the string. She took the end of it in her teeth, and looked for the little round hole in the door, a small bright circle of red evening light, just above her nose.

She pushed the end of the string into the hole.

It went a little way, and stuck.

She pushed harder. It went a little further.

"I can see it!" came Brody's voice. "The tip! It's poking out! Just a bit more!"

Charlie took a breath, and gave one last push with her nose.

The string slid through.


Chapter Eight — Brody Pulls

On the outside of the Great Barn, everybody saw the little leather string come wriggling out through the hole in the door.

"Charlie!" called Brody. "Get down now, into the hay, and go and stand by the wall. Tell me when you're there."

There was a scramble and a soft flump.

"I'm in the hay!" called Charlie. "Now I'm by the wall! Go on!"

"Now, Brody," said Great-Grandpappy Buck, who had come round from the rain-barrel the moment Charlie was clear of the doors. "Steady. Not a yank. A long, slow, steady pull."

Brody took the string in his big paw. It was very thin.

"What if it breaks?" he whispered.

"It's an old string," said Buck. "But it's a good one. Pull steady, and it'll hold."

Brody wrapped it round his paw, once, twice. He planted his feet. He took a deep breath.

And he pulled.

Not hard. Not fast. Not the way he had pulled the doors and the sacks and the carts all week. He pulled the way Buck had said: slowly, steadily, letting his strength go into it a little at a time, as gentle as he was strong.

On the other side of the door, there was a long, low creak.

The string went tight. The swollen latch groaned in its notch. Brody leaned back a little more, and a little more, his arms shaking.

"It's moving!" came Charlie's voice from inside. "It's coming up! Keep going!"

There was a clunk.

The same clunk as before, only backwards.

Buck took hold of the handle and drew the doors towards him, and the great doors of the Great Barn swung slowly open. And there, standing well back by the wall with straw stuck all over her fur and her gingham bow knocked sideways, grinning so hard that her cheeks puffed out, was Charlie.

"I DID IT!" she shouted. "WE DID IT!"

Everybody cheered so loudly that the pigeon took off again.

Brody did not cheer. He stood in the doorway holding the end of the string and looked at the small chipmunk by the wall, and found that he could not say anything at all for a moment, because he was too busy being glad.

"Charlie," he said. "You're a hero."

Charlie looked up at him.

"So are you," she said. "I couldn't have pulled it in a hundred years."

Maggie Mae came running through the doorway and scooped Charlie up and turned round and round with her, straw and all, until they were both dizzy. Rusty whistled every birdsong he knew at once, which sounded like an entire wood waking up. And Barney did a little dance on the barn step that he said was called the Latch-String Jig, and that nobody else could follow, because he kept changing it.

Then everybody hurried, because the light was nearly gone. Benny counted the crates. Sadie Belle and Beau pulled the blankets off the cart. Brody took the shafts.

They brought the last cart in just as the light went. The pumpkins and potatoes were safe. And as Great-Grandpappy Buck closed the doors behind it, gently this time, and hung the latch-string carefully out through its hole where it belonged, the very first hard frost of the year began to sparkle on the grass of Fern Meadow.


Chapter Nine — Harvest Scout

Afterwards, when the blankets had been folded and the cart put away and the rabbits sent home, Brody went and found Charlie.

She was sitting on the step of the Great Barn, picking straw out of her fur. The frost was making everything glitter.

Brody sat down beside her. The step creaked.

"Charlie," he said. "I'm sorry."

"What for?"

"All week, I kept saying you were too little. Too little to carry things. Too little to help." He looked at his big paws. "I didn't mean it unkindly. I just thought helping meant carrying. And I'm good at carrying. So I thought I'd do all of it."

Charlie thought about that.

"I didn't mind you carrying things," she said. "I just minded not having anything to carry."

Brody nodded slowly. All week he had not understood that. Now he did.

They sat quietly for a while, watching their breath turn to little white clouds.

"Charlie," said Brody. "Next year, at harvest. Will you be my partner?"

Charlie turned and looked up at him.

"What would I do?"

Brody had been thinking about it all the way through the long, slow pull.

"You could go first," he said. "Into the rows, and under the hedges, and in behind the barrels, where I can't fit. You can see things close to the ground. You could find all the apples that roll into the long grass. And the beans that fall out of the sacks. And the little squashes hiding under the big leaves, the ones everybody misses. And then you shout, and I come and carry them."

Charlie sat up so suddenly that she nearly slid off the step.

"Like a scout," she said. "A harvest scout."

"The best harvest scout in the valley," said Brody. "And you could do the counting, too. You're much better at counting than me. I always lose my place after about twelve."

"I can count to a hundred and forty," said Charlie proudly. "Nearly."

"Then that's settled," said Brody, and he held out his big paw.

Charlie put her small paw in it. It disappeared completely. They shook on it very solemnly, and then both of them laughed, because it looked so funny.

Great-Grandpappy Buck had come up behind them with his walking-stick and his lantern, and he lowered himself onto the step on Charlie's other side with a sigh.

"Do you know," he said, "what the old folk used to say, when they wanted to tell a neighbour they'd always be welcome?" He nodded at the little leather string, hanging out through its hole in the door, glinting with frost. "They'd say, the latch-string is always out. Meaning, you needn't knock. Just pull the string and come in."

Charlie looked at the string for a long time.

"I like that," she said.


Chapter Ten — The Small Trees and the Tall

Nobody wanted to go home quite yet. So the friends followed Great-Grandpappy Buck and his lantern across the frosty meadow and along the creek path to his lodge, and crowded onto his porch to get warm.

Buck hung the lantern on its hook. Maggie Mae went straight into his kitchen and came out with a big jug of hot cordial and a stack of wooden mugs, and poured for everybody. Reba brought out the basket of bruised apples she had been saving all week, and they turned out to be the sweetest of the whole harvest. Benny and Barney sat back to back on the top step, arguing happily about exactly how much the latch weighed. Maverick perched on the woodpile. Beau stretched out along the boards with his chin on his paws, and Sadie Belle tucked her long legs under her beside him and listened to him tell the story of the barn as if she had not been there herself. Rusty sat on the bottom step, nearest the dark, sniffing the cold air.

Charlie sat in Brody's lap, and Buck tucked a corner of Bramble's patchwork quilt round both of them.

Then Great-Grandpappy Buck lowered himself into his old hickory rocking chair. It gave its first slow creak, and the porch went quiet.

He took his banjo from its corner and played a few slow notes, soft in the cold air. Then he stopped, and looked out past the porch rail towards the dark line of the Hickory Woods, silver now with frost under the stars.

"Out in the deep woods," he said, in his low, slow voice, "there are great tall trees, so tall you can hardly see the tops of them. And there are small ones too, down at their feet, no higher than a rabbit. And folk sometimes think the tall trees are the only ones that matter." He rocked gently. "But the small trees hold the soil. They shelter the young things. They fill the spaces the tall ones can't reach. The forest needs the small trees and the tall ones, young'uns. So does everywhere else."

He began to play again.

It was the same slow, gentle tune that Brody had hummed through the crack in the barn doors. And after a moment, very softly, Brody began to hum along with it, low and warm, and Charlie felt the humming go right through her, from his big chest into her small back, like a kettle singing quietly on a stove.

Her eyes closed.

Buck played the tune through twice, and then a third time, slower. The frost crept further across the meadow. Down below the porch, Silver Creek ran on in the dark, talking quietly to its stones.

Then, along the creek path, lights began to appear: lanterns, bobbing gently through the dark, as the families of the Hollow came to fetch their youngsters home after the longest day of the harvest.

Brody did not move. He went on humming, very softly, so as not to wake the chipmunk asleep in his lap, until her family's lantern stopped at the foot of the porch steps. Then he lifted her up, quilt and all, as gently as if she were made of frost, and passed her down into waiting arms, and she did not stir.

"She was the hero today, I hear," said a soft voice from behind the lantern.

"She was," said Brody. "We both were. Together."

One by one the lanterns went away along the creek path, and the porch grew quiet, and Buck's chair creaked slowly in the dark.

Down in Fern Meadow, the doors of the Great Barn stood shut and safe against the cold, with the whole of the Hollow's harvest tucked up inside. And out through its little hole, glinting in the starlight, the latch-string hung ready for whoever might come next.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
