Chapter One — The First Snow

Barney woke up because the light was wrong.

It was not the grey light of a December morning. It was white. Bright, soft, glowing white, coming in through the little round window of the brothers' lodge and lying across the floor like spilled milk. And the sounds were wrong too. There were no sounds at all. No creek chattering. No birds. Just a thick, deep, cotton-wool hush, as if the whole valley had been tucked up under a blanket and told to be quiet.

Barney sat up in bed. His fur stood straight up on end, which it did every morning, but this morning it was for a reason.

"Benny," he whispered. "Benny. Benny. BENNY."

"Mmf," said Benny, from under his quilt.

"It snowed."

Benny's head came out of the quilt. His carpenter's pencil was still behind his ear, because he slept with it there. He looked at the window. His eyes went wide.

Then both brothers were out of bed and at the door at once, getting in each other's way, and the door was flung open, and there it was.

Hickory Hollow had gone white.

Every roof, every fence, every branch of every bare hickory tree was heaped with snow. The beaver ponds were white. Grandpappy's Bridge was white. Silver Creek ran black and silver between two thick white banks, steaming a little in the cold. The snow lay soft and smooth and unbroken right across the valley, without a single pawprint on it, and it sparkled in the low sun as if somebody had scattered it with salt.

"Oh," breathed Barney. "Oh, Benny. It's the first snow. It's the very first snow."

He took one step out of the door and sank in up to his middle.

"It's deep!" he cried, delighted.

"It's very powdery," said Benny, scooping some up in his paw. It ran through his fingers like sugar. "It must have been very cold last night."

But Barney was not listening. Barney was standing up to his middle in the first snow of the winter, with his whiskers frosting over and his tail thumping the snow, and an idea was growing in his head. It was the biggest idea he had ever had. It was so big that it nearly lifted him out of the snow.

"Benny," he said. "I'm going to make a surprise."

"What sort of surprise?"

"The biggest surprise in the whole history of Hickory Hollow," said Barney. "I'm going to build a palace. A snow palace. Today. Round behind the Great Barn, at the far end of Fern Meadow, where nobody ever goes in winter. With towers. And a gate. And a snow statue of every single creature in the Hollow, standing all round it. And tomorrow morning I'll bring the whole Hollow round the corner of the barn, and there it'll be." He spread his arms so wide he nearly fell over. "The Snow Palace of Hickory Hollow!"

Benny looked at him.

"That's a very big palace," he said slowly.

"It's going to be enormous."

"With towers?"

"Six towers."

Benny was quiet for a moment. Then, despite himself, the builder in him began to stir. A palace. With towers. A structure.

"Towers would need a proper base," he said. "And thick walls. Tapering at the top."

Barney grinned.

"So you'll help?"

Benny sighed. "I'll help," he said. "Somebody has to make sure the towers stand up."


Chapter Two — Snow That Won't Stick

By the middle of the morning, Barney had rounded up nearly everybody.

It was not difficult. Everybody was out in the snow anyway, and the moment Barney said the words "snow palace", they all came running.

Brody came, with his red neckerchief wound twice round his neck against the cold. Rusty came, because he said he knew where the deepest drifts in the Hollow were. Reba came, because she had heard there might be snow pastries, which there were not. Beau came bounding, in great leaps through the drifts, disappearing completely every time he landed and popping up again a moment later with snow on his nose. Sadie Belle came picking her way delicately on her long legs. Maggie Mae came with a sketchbook and a pencil, to design the towers. Charlie came riding on top of Maggie Mae's head, because the snow was deeper than she was.

And Maverick came gliding down out of the white sky and landed in a drift by mistake, and sank in up to his white head, and said, "Meant to do that," in a muffled voice.

"Right," said Barney, standing on a tree stump in the middle of Fern Meadow. "Here's the plan. Big snowballs for the walls. Rolled. Then we stack them up. Then we carve the towers. Then we make everybody's statue. By tonight it's finished, and nobody else knows, and tomorrow morning: SURPRISE!"

Everybody cheered.

"Brody," said Barney. "You're the strongest. You roll the first snowball."

Brody beamed. He scooped up a big double pawful of snow, pressed it together into a ball, set it down, and began to roll it across the meadow.

It did not get bigger.

It got smaller.

The snow was so dry and so cold that it would not stick to itself at all. Every time the ball rolled over, a little more of it crumbled off, until by the time Brody had pushed it ten paces, it had shrunk to the size of an apple, and then the size of an acorn, and then it was not there at all.

Brody looked at his empty paws.

"Where did it go?" he said.

"Try a bigger one," said Barney.

So Brody gathered up an enormous armful of snow, so big that he could not see over it, and squeezed it with all his strength.

It burst. It exploded out of his arms in a great white puff, like flour out of a dropped sack, and came down all over him, so that for a moment there was no bear at all, just a large, surprised, snow-shaped lump with two eyes blinking out of it.

Charlie laughed so hard that she fell off Maggie Mae's head into a drift and had to be dug out.

Beau tried next. He jumped into the air and landed on a heap of snow to pack it down flat. The snow did not pack. It sprayed out in every direction, and Beau went straight through it and disappeared, and there was a pause, and then a muffled voice from somewhere underneath said, "I'm fine! Nobody look!"

"It's too cold," said Benny, crumbling a pawful of snow and watching it trickle away. "Dry snow won't pack. It's like trying to build with sugar. It needs to be a bit warmer, so it's a bit wetter, so it sticks."

"How much warmer?" said Barney.

Benny looked at the sky.

"This afternoon, maybe," he said. "When the sun's been on it."

Barney looked at the sun, which was very low and very pale and did not look as though it was trying very hard.

"Then we'll wait," he said, with great determination. "And then we'll build twice as fast."


Chapter Three — The Palace Grows

Benny was right.

By the early afternoon, the low winter sun had warmed the top of the snow just enough. When Brody scooped up a pawful and squeezed it, it did not crumble. It held. It made a firm, cold, satisfying ball.

"It sticks!" shouted Brody. "It sticks!"

And then they built. They built as fast as anybody in Hickory Hollow had ever built anything.

Brody rolled the great snowballs for the walls, round and round across the meadow, growing bigger and bigger until they were taller than he was, leaving long dark trails behind them where they had picked up all the snow down to the frozen grass. Rusty, who knew every drift in the valley, led the way to the deepest snow, so that the balls grew fastest. Benny stacked them, and measured them, and made the walls thick at the bottom and thinner at the top so they would not topple, and packed the gaps with snow.

Maggie Mae drew the towers in her sketchbook, and then helped to carve them, with a flat wooden spoon from her kitchen, into tall, graceful points, with little windows. Sadie Belle smoothed the walls with her soft nose until they shone. Reba made the gate, and an arch over it, and decorated the arch with a row of pine cones, and then a row of holly berries, and then a row of snow pastries, which were snow, and which she was very disappointed about.

Charlie made the snow statues, because she said she was the best at faces. She made a snow Benny with a twig for a pencil. She made a snow Brody with a red berry neckerchief. She made a snow Maggie Mae with a bow of dried leaves, and a snow Great-Grandpappy Buck sitting in a snow rocking chair, with a snow banjo on his knee.

And Barney ran about everywhere at once, shouting "Higher!" and "Bigger!" and "More towers!" and adding things. A snow flag on top of the tallest tower. A snow bench by the gate. A snow moat, which was mostly just a ditch. A snow staircase, which went up to nowhere.

Maverick flew round and round the whole thing, calling out what it looked like from above. "It's lopsided on the left!" "The third tower's leaning!" "It looks like a cake from up here! A very big cake!"

All that day, the grown-ups of the Hollow were busy clearing paths and carrying firewood and minding fires, and not one of them came round behind the Great Barn. So by the time the sun went down behind Weather Ridge, the Snow Palace of Hickory Hollow stood at the far end of Fern Meadow, and nobody but its builders had seen it.

It was not finished. It had four towers, not six, and one of them leaned like a tired heron. The moat was a ditch. The staircase went nowhere. Only half the snow statues had faces, and the snow Buck's banjo had fallen off twice.

But it was enormous. It was glorious. It shone pink and gold in the last of the sunset, and it was the biggest, grandest, most magnificent thing that anybody in the valley had ever built out of snow.

Barney stood in front of it, panting, with snow up to his ears.

"Tomorrow morning," he whispered, "I'll bring everybody round the corner. And it'll be the best surprise ever."

They all went home in the blue dusk, tired and wet and happy, leaving the palace alone in the dark meadow.

That night, the wind changed.


Chapter Four — The Thaw

It came from the south in the middle of the night, a warm, damp, soft wind, the kind that sometimes comes in early winter and undoes everything.

Barney did not hear it. He was asleep, dreaming of towers.

But in the morning, when he flung open the lodge door to run to Fern Meadow, he stopped on the step.

Everything was dripping.

Drip from the roof. Drip from the branches. Drip, drip, drip from every twig and fence post and bush in the Hollow. The snow on the ground had gone grey and soft and lumpy, and there were patches of brown grass showing through it, and Silver Creek was running fast and full and muddy. The air smelled of wet earth.

"Oh, no," said Barney. "Oh, no, no, no."

He ran all the way to Fern Meadow.

The Snow Palace was still there. Sort of.

The walls had sagged. They were not walls any more so much as long, low, grey, slumped heaps, like sleeping cows. The four tall towers had melted down into four short, round lumps. The leaning tower had leaned all the way over in the night and fallen right across the gate, and Reba's arch had collapsed beneath it in a heap of pine cones. And the snow statues had all melted at different speeds, so that the snow Benny had lost his head, and the snow Brody had shrunk to the size of a rabbit, and the snow Buck's head had slid right off and landed in the snow Maggie Mae's lap, where it sat looking up at her with an expression of great surprise.

Barney stood in front of it with snow-water dripping off his ears.

Nobody had seen it. Not one creature but its builders had come round the corner of the Great Barn and seen the Snow Palace of Hickory Hollow standing there, all towers and gates. The thaw had come in the night and taken it, and now it was just a heap.

"I can fix it," said Barney. "I can fix it. I'll build it back up. Quickly. Before anybody sees."

He started scooping up the wet grey snow and piling it back onto the walls, as fast as he could, paw over paw. But the snow was so soft now that it slid straight off. He piled it up, and it slumped down. He piled it up again, and it slumped again. A big drip from the hickory tree above landed right on his nose.

"Barney."

It was Benny. He had followed him from the lodge, and had been standing at the edge of the meadow for some time, watching.

"It won't hold," said Benny gently. "It isn't snow any more. It's slush, and it's still melting. Nothing will stick to that."

"Then I'll wait till it freezes again," said Barney. "And then I'll build it all over again. Bigger."

"Barney," said Benny. "Come on. You're soaked. Come and get warm."


Chapter Five — The Window at the Mill

They went to the Old Mill, because it was the nearest warm place and because the miller always kept a fire going.

The great waterwheel was turning slowly in the swollen creek, rumbling deep and steady, and the big warm room inside smelled of flour and woodsmoke. The miller gave them each a blanket and a mug of hot milk and went back to his work, whistling. Barney's milk left a white foam moustache on his upper lip. Benny pointed it out. Barney said he was keeping it, on purpose, for warmth, and kept it for the rest of the afternoon.

Barney sat by the fire with his blanket round his shoulders and his chin on his knees and did not say anything at all, which for Barney was very unusual.

Benny sat beside him, and drank his milk, and looked round the room.

In the corner, by the big front window that looked out over the creek and the meadow, the miller's wife was sitting in her armchair. She had a thick shawl round her shoulders and a rug over her knees and a cushion behind her back, because her back was still bad from the autumn, and the cold made it worse. She was not doing anything. She was just looking out of the window at the last of the snow, melting grey and soft on the bank outside.

"Lovely, wasn't it?" she said, without turning round. "Yesterday. When it was all fresh."

Benny looked at Barney. Barney did not look up.

"Did you go out in it?" asked Benny politely.

The miller's wife shook her head.

"Not with this back," she said. "First snow I've missed since I was a girl. I always go out in the first snow. Every year. Always." She looked out at the melting bank. "And now it's nearly gone." She smiled a little. "When I was a girl, I used to go out in the very first snow every year with my mother's old black mitten, and catch the snowflakes on it, and look at them. You'd never believe it, young'uns, if you've never looked close. Every one's a little star. Six arms, every one. And no two the same." She sighed. "You can't see them through a window. Too small. They're just white."

She went quiet again, watching the drips fall from the roof outside.

Benny looked at her, and then at his brother.

Barney was still staring at the fire. He had not heard a word, or had heard it and thought nothing of it. He was busy thinking about towers.

"Barney," said Benny quietly. "Did you hear that?"

"What?"

"The miller's wife. She's missed the first snow. The first time ever. She just sits at that window."

"Oh," said Barney. Then: "That's sad." Then, after a moment, quite crossly: "Nobody saw my palace either. Not one single creature. It was the biggest surprise in the whole history of Hickory Hollow, and nobody saw it, and now it's a heap."

Benny did not say anything for a while.

"Who was it for?" he asked at last.

"Everybody," said Barney. "The whole Hollow."

"Did anybody need a palace?"

Barney opened his mouth to say yes. Then he shut it again.


Chapter Six — Who It Was For

"That's not the point," said Barney, a little too loudly. The miller looked round from his flour sacks. Barney lowered his voice. "Surprises aren't about needing. They're about being amazing. That's what makes them surprises."

"I know," said Benny. "It was amazing. I helped build it. It was the best thing I've ever measured." He turned his mug round in his paws, and did not say anything else.

Barney scowled at the fire.

"I just wanted to give everybody something big," he said. "Something nobody would ever forget."

They sat in silence, the two brothers, with the fire crackling and the great wheel rumbling outside. Over by the window, the miller's wife shifted in her chair, and winced a little, and pulled her shawl closer, and went on looking out at the grey, dripping world.

And slowly, very slowly, Barney turned his head and looked at her.

He looked at her for a long time.

He remembered what she had said. Six arms, every one. And no two the same. You can't see them through a window.

"Benny," he said slowly. "If it snowed again. If it snowed fresh snow. Could we bring her some?"

Benny looked at him.

"Bring her snow?"

"Snowflakes," said Barney. His voice had gone quiet and fast. "On something dark. Like her mother's black mitten. So she could look at them close. Without having to go out."

Then he stopped. His face fell.

"No," he said. "They'd melt. The second you brought them inside, in the warm, they'd melt. Before she could even look."

Benny was already frowning at the problem, very interested.

"Not if the dark thing was cold first," he said slowly. "Really cold. Left out in the snow. And if you didn't breathe on it. And if you were quick."

"How quick?"

"Very quick," said Benny. "And she'd need to be right by the window. With it open. And she'd need something to look through, to make them bigger. They're tiny."

Barney looked at the window, and at the miller's wife, and at Benny, and then down at his own paws, which were still red from trying to rebuild the palace.

"It's very small," he said doubtfully.

"It's very small," Benny agreed. "Do you want to do it?"

Barney looked into the fire.

"Yes," he said.


Chapter Seven — Waiting for Snow

The trouble was, there was no snow to bring. The thaw had taken nearly all of it.

So they waited for it to come back. And while they waited, they got ready.

Great-Grandpappy Buck looked at Weather Ridge from his porch that evening and told them that the cap on it meant cold coming, and snow with it, by the next afternoon, if the wind held. That was all he told them. He was very good at weather.

Maggie Mae turned her whole sewing basket out onto her kitchen table, looking for something dark enough.

"Not brown," she said, holding up a scrap. "Snow's too pale against brown. Not blue. Not green." She dug deeper. "Aha."

It was a piece of black velvet left over from a winter cloak, soft and dark as a night with no moon.

"Will snowflakes show up on that?" asked Barney.

"Sugar," said Maggie Mae, "snowflakes will look like diamonds on that." She cut it into six small squares, and a seventh for luck, and stitched each one neatly onto a thin flat board so that it would lie perfectly smooth.

Benny said the boards would need to go out in the snow an hour before, to get properly cold, all the way through, so the flakes would not melt the moment they landed.

Charlie said they must not breathe on them. "Breath is warm," she explained gravely. "Even little breath. I'll remind everybody." She practised holding her breath, and went pink, and had to stop.

Great-Grandpappy Buck, when they asked, went into his workshop and came out with the round glass he used for very fine carving, the one that made small things big, in its little wooden frame. He handed it to Barney carefully.

"It goes back on its hook when you're done," he said. "And mind you don't drop it."

Sadie Belle said that the best snowflakes came down slowly, on still days, and that you had to catch them where nothing else had touched them, out in the open meadow, not under the trees, where they got knocked about.

Rusty knew the quickest path from the open meadow to the mill window, without going round by the bridge. "It's a shortcut," he said, "but a proper one. I've tested it. And it doesn't go anywhere near the creek."

Beau said that he would carry the boards, because nobody balanced better than a bobcat. To prove it, he carried a tray of Buck's teacups up and down the porch steps four times without a rattle. On the fifth time, Maverick landed on the rail beside him with a thump, and Beau jumped, and the teacups did not fall, because Brody caught every one, and Beau said that had been part of the plan.

And Brody went down to the mill that very evening, with the miller, and cleared every last bit of slush away from under the big front window, and laid down a path of flat stones, so that whoever carried the boards there would not slip.

Reba wanted to know if there was anything she could do. Barney thought about it.

"Could you make something warm?" he said. "For afterwards? For the miller's wife. And for everybody."

Reba was already halfway to her pantry before he had finished the sentence.

Maverick, who had been listening to all of this from the top of a fence post, fluffed up his feathers.

"What can I do?" he said. "I want a job too."

"Can you tell us," said Barney, "the second it starts snowing? From up high? You'll see it coming over the ridge before anybody."

Maverick puffed out his chest so far he nearly fell off the post.

"I," he said, "will be the snow lookout."


Chapter Eight — The Snowflake Surprise

The next afternoon, just as Buck had said, the cold came back, and the sky over Weather Ridge went heavy and grey and soft.

The black velvet boards had been lying out on the frozen ground in the shady corner of Brody's garden since lunchtime, where the frost had come back hard, getting cold all the way through.

"SNOW!" called a voice from very high up. "SNOW COMING OVER THE RIDGE! I SEE IT! I SAW IT FIRST!"

And it came.

It came drifting down out of the grey sky slowly, the very best kind of snow for looking at: small, separate flakes, the kind that come in cold, still air, falling straight down one at a time. Everybody went out into the open middle of Fern Meadow, well away from the trees. Beau carried the cold boards on a tray, so smoothly that nothing on it so much as trembled.

"Now," whispered Charlie. "Nobody breathe."

They held the boards up to the falling snow. One flake landed on the black velvet. Then another. Then three more. And there, against the dark, even without the glass, you could see them: tiny white shapes, each one six-sided, sparkling.

"Oh," breathed Sadie Belle. "Oh, look."

"Quickly now," said Benny. "Before they go."

Beau took the tray. Rusty led the way along his proper shortcut, which really was quick. Everybody followed in a long, careful, breath-holding line across the snowy meadow and down to the mill, where the miller was standing at the big front window, holding it open, and the miller's wife was sitting right beside it in her armchair, wrapped in her shawl, looking very puzzled indeed.

Barney took the first board off the tray and held it up to her, right outside the open window, in the cold, where the flakes would not melt. He held out Buck's round glass in the other paw.

"We brought you the first snow," he said. "The fresh snow. Up close."

The miller's wife looked at him. Then she took the glass, slowly, and held it over the black velvet, and looked through it.

She did not say anything at all.

There, on the dark cloth, through the glass, lay five tiny perfect shapes of ice. Every one had six sides or six arms. One had arms like feathers. One had arms like little fir trees. One had arms with tiny plates on the ends, like spoons. And one was just a plain, small, perfect hexagon, like a window.

No two the same.

"Oh, my," whispered the miller's wife. "Oh, my, my, my."

And then, as they watched, the flakes began very slowly to soften at the edges, and shine, and turn to tiny drops of water, and were gone.

"Next board," said Barney.

There were six boards. Then Maverick flew back out to the meadow with an empty one held carefully in his talons and caught fresh flakes on it high up, and flew back, and Sadie Belle took another out, and then another, and they kept going, round and round, from the meadow to the window and back, as the snow fell softer and thicker. And every board brought new stars, and no two of them were ever the same, and the miller's wife looked at every single one.

"That one's got a little star in the middle of a bigger star," she said. "And look at that one, like lace."

Her cheeks had gone pink, and not from the cold.


Chapter Nine — What Was Left of the Palace

When the light began to go, the miller wrapped his wife up in two more shawls and a blanket and a woolly hat. Brody carried her armchair out, empty, and set it at the top of the mill step, and the miller helped her walk to it, slowly, one careful step at a time, leaning on his arm, and settled her in it, under the eaves, where the snow could not land on her but she could feel the cold fresh air and watch it falling all around.

She sat there in her chair, out in the snow after all, with Buck's round glass in her lap, and laughed out loud.

Reba brought out what she had made. It was a great jug of hot spiced apple and honey, and a plate of warm oat biscuits, and it was exactly right. Everybody sat on the mill step and the bench beside it and drank it out of the miller's mugs, with the snow falling softly in front of them and the great wheel rumbling behind.

Barney sat on the end of the bench next to Benny, watching the miller's wife.

She had caught a snowflake on the seventh square of black velvet, the one Maggie Mae had made for luck, and was peering at it through the glass with tremendous concentration.

Barney watched her, and did not say anything at all.

Barney thought about the grey heaps of the Snow Palace, out behind the Great Barn, turning white again under the fresh snow. Soft and round and smooth.

He looked towards the barn. Then he grinned. It was the first proper grin since the thaw.

"I've had an idea," he said.

"Oh no," said Benny.

"A small one," said Barney. "I promise."

And before it got dark, while the grown-ups were still on the mill step with their mugs, Barney and the little rabbits and Charlie went out behind the Great Barn, with the miller coming along to stand at the edge and watch, and found that the biggest of the slumped palace heaps, with the fresh snow over it, made the most perfect little sledging slope for very small creatures. Not too steep. Not too high. Just right for a rabbit on a tea tray. The littlest rabbit went down it eleven times, squealing all the way, and it was the best thing that had ever happened to her.

"It's not a palace," said Barney, watching. "But it's still a good heap."


Chapter Ten — Gentle

By the time Great-Grandpappy Buck came stumping along the creek path with his walking-stick and his lantern, to collect his round glass, it was nearly dark.

The snow had stopped. The clouds were thinning, and a few hard little points of light pricked through the gaps. Everything was white and still and new again, just as it had been on the first morning, and the whole valley glimmered softly in the dusk.

The miller's wife held the round glass out to him from her chair on the mill step.

"Thank you for the loan of it," she said. "I've seen more snowflakes this afternoon than in my whole life since I was a girl."

Buck took it, and turned it in his old paws, and smiled.

"Whose idea was it?" he asked.

Everybody looked at Barney. Barney went slightly pink under his scruffy fur.

"It was supposed to be a palace," he said. "The biggest surprise in the whole history of the Hollow. But it melted. So we brought snowflakes instead."

Buck looked out across the meadow towards the Great Barn, where the last of the little sledgers were trailing back, wet and happy, with the miller behind them. He looked at the miller's wife, wrapped in her shawls, with pink cheeks and a square of black velvet in her lap. He looked at Barney.

"The world gets a little warmer," said Buck, "every time somebody chooses to be gentle."

He tucked the round glass into his pocket, and lifted his lantern, and the miller helped his wife back inside to the warm, and the friends said goodnight and set off home along the creek path, with Buck and his lantern leading the way.

One by one, the others were seen to their own doors, where family lanterns were waiting. Benny and Barney went last, because their lodge was furthest.

They walked side by side through the new snow, leaving two lines of pawprints behind them, one neat and straight, one wandering from side to side. It had begun, very lightly, to snow again. Just a few flakes, drifting down out of the dark, one at a time.

One landed on Barney's sleeve.

He stopped. Up ahead, Buck stopped too, and came back, and held the lantern up beside him. Barney held his arm into the light, very still, without breathing on it.

"Six arms," he whispered.

Another landed beside it.

"Six arms. Different ones."

Benny stopped too, and held out his own arm. Two flakes landed on it, and then a third. The brothers stood together in the falling snow on the path by the creek, counting arms, while Buck held the lantern for them and waited, and did not hurry them at all.

"Benny," said Barney. "Next winter. When it snows the first time."

"No palaces," said Benny.

"No palaces," Barney agreed. "But maybe a very small heap."

"A very small heap," said Benny, "is fine."

Then they walked on together, the old beaver with his lantern and the two young ones beside him, leaving three lines of pawprints in the new snow: one slow, one straight, and one wandering happily from side to side, all the way home.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
