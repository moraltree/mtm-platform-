Chapter One — The Bottom Drawer

At the end of April, when the rain had stopped at last and the whole Hollow had turned green almost overnight, Great-Grandpappy Buck decided to clear out his desk.

He did not do it often. The last time, according to Buck himself, had been "before your mothers were born, and possibly before mine was". So he asked for help, and Rusty came, because Buck had promised cocoa, and Beau came, because Beau went wherever Rusty went.

The desk stood in the corner of Buck's front room, a big old wooden thing with a great many drawers, all of them stuck. Inside the drawers there were letters, and pencil stubs, and buttons, and string, and pressed leaves, and a broken pocket-watch, and seventeen spoons, which nobody could explain.

"Bin," said Buck, holding up a very old boot-lace. "Keep," holding up a feather. "Bin. Bin. Goodness me, I wondered where that went. Keep."

The bottom drawer was the most stuck of all. Beau pulled. Rusty pulled. Beau and Rusty pulled together, and the drawer came out all at once with a great scraping shriek, and they both sat down on the rug, and a folded square of paper slid out of the very back of it and landed on Rusty's tail.

Rusty picked it up.

It was old, soft and yellowy and furry at the edges, and folded small. He unfolded it carefully on the rug.

It was a map.

A hand-drawn map, in faded brown ink, with wobbly lines and careful little pictures. There was a creek, curving down one side. There were trees. There were little drawings: a frog, some wavy lines with a dot in the middle, a tree with a face, three pointed hills in a row. And a dotted line, winding its way from the bottom of the map, past all the little pictures, up and up, to the very top, where there was a large X, gone over and over so many times that the ink had nearly worn through the paper.

Beside the X, in round childish writing, it said: THE BEST THING IN THE WHOLE HOLLOW.

Rusty stared at it. Then he looked up at Beau. Beau's ears were standing straight up.

"Treasure," whispered Beau.

"Grandpappy," said Rusty, holding it up. "What's this?"

Buck took the map, and put on his spectacles, and looked at it.

"Well, I never," he said softly. "I drew that. When I was about your age. I'd forgotten all about it."

"What's the best thing in the whole Hollow?" said Beau, bouncing. "Is it gold? Is it a cave? Is it a secret?"

Buck frowned at the map. He turned it upside down, and then the right way up again.

"Do you know," he said slowly, "I can't remember."

"You can't remember?"

"It was a very long time ago," said Buck. "I remember drawing it. I remember being very proud of it. I remember that I used to go up there, every spring." He tapped the X. "But what it was, and where, exactly..." He shook his head. "It's gone. Like a dream when you wake up."

Rusty and Beau looked at each other. And they both had exactly the same thought at exactly the same time.

"We could find it," said Rusty.

"We could follow the map," said Beau.

"Please, Grandpappy," they said together. "Please can we?"

Buck looked down at the two eager faces on his rug, and at the old map in his paws, and something in his eyes went a long way off.

"On one condition," he said. "I'm coming with you."


Chapter Two — Reading the Map

They set off on Saturday morning, at the beginning of May, from Buck's front porch.

It had taken the rest of the week to get ready, because a map that nobody can remember turns out to be quite hard to read.

For one thing, there was no north on it. Benny pointed that out, the first evening, peering over Rusty's shoulder at the kitchen table at Buck's. Every proper map had a north, he said, and this one did not. So nobody knew which way up it went.

It was Rusty who saw the answer. In the top corner of the map, above the three pointed hills, the young Buck had drawn a little round sun, with lines coming out of it.

"The sun comes up over Sunrise Ridge," said Rusty. "That's east. So the three hills are on Sunrise Ridge. So the top of the map is east."

Benny looked at him with great respect, and lent him his little brass compass, and showed him how to turn the map round until the little sun lay the same way as east on the compass, so the map and the land matched. Then he made Rusty promise to bring the compass back.

For another thing, nothing was the right size. The creek on the map was as wide as a road, and the trees were as tall as the creek was long, and the frog was nearly as big as a hill.

"I drew the things I liked big," said Buck, a little sheepishly, "and the things I didn't like small. I was only a youngster."

So they studied it.

The dotted line started at the bottom, by a little drawing of a house with a chimney, which Buck said must be the lodge. That was easy. Then it went along the creek, the wiggly line down the side. Then it turned away from the creek, by the frog. Then past the wavy lines with the dot in the middle. Then past the tree with a face. Then up, steeply, between the three pointed hills, and on and up to the X.

"Frog," said Rusty, tapping it. "That's got to be a pond."

"The beaver ponds?" said Beau.

"Too close," said Rusty. "The dotted line goes much further than that first."

"Wavy lines with a dot," said Beau. "Water? A puddle? A very small lake?"

Reba, who had come round with a tin of oat biscuits for the journey, leaned over and looked.

"That's boiling," she said at once. "In my mother's recipe book. Wavy lines with a dot in the middle means boil it. Water that bubbles."

"Water that bubbles," said Rusty, "in the middle of a wood?"

"I'm only telling you what it means in recipes," said Reba, and went back to packing the biscuits.

"The tree with a face," said Rusty. "That's a tree with a face."

"Obviously," said Beau.

They looked at the three pointed hills at the top, and could not decide whether they were hills or mountains or very large tents.

Sadie Belle came to the porch on Saturday morning to see them off, and gave Beau a quiet piece of advice.

"Look at the ground," she said. "Slowly. Like I showed you, in the snow. Old paths leave old marks, even when they're grown over."

"Slowly," said Beau. "Right."

Maverick, who was perched on the porch rail, said he would keep an eye on them from above, for as long as the day lasted. "Nobody in the Hollow sees further than I do," he said. "Ask anybody. Ask me."

And Buck said that the miller and Beau's father would come up and meet them at sunset, with lanterns, at whatever place Maverick told them, so that nobody would be walking down the mountain in the dark.

And so, with the picnic in Beau's backpack, and Benny's compass in Rusty's paw, and the map in the bib pocket of Buck's dungarees, and Buck's walking-stick going tap, tap, tap on the boards, they set off down the porch steps, and along the creek path, upstream, into a green and singing spring morning.

Rusty was out in front, with the compass. He went fast.

He always went fast. That was Rusty. He was quick and clever and full of plans, and when there was something at the end of a trail, he wanted to be at it.

"Come on!" he called back over his shoulder. "This way! We'll be there by lunchtime!"

Behind him, Great-Grandpappy Buck went tap, tap, tap, at exactly the same speed as before.

"Will we, now," he said, to nobody in particular.


Chapter Three — The Frog

The trouble started at the frog.

They had followed the creek upstream for most of an hour. Rusty had run ahead, and run back, and run ahead again, and had done the whole walk about three times over, while Beau went at a sort of bounce in between, and Buck went tap, tap, tap at the back.

At last, the creek bent away to the left, and Rusty stopped, and looked at the compass, and looked at the map.

"Here," he said. "The dotted line turns away from the creek here. By the frog." He looked round. "Where's the frog?"

There was no pond. There was no frog. There was only a wide, wet, marshy meadow, full of tussocks and reeds and little shining pools, stretching away from the creek towards the trees.

"Wrong place," said Rusty, frowning. "Must be further up."

But Beau had stopped. He was standing quite still, with his ears turned towards the marsh.

"Listen," he said.

They listened.

At first there was nothing but the creek, and a red-winged blackbird calling from a reed. Then, from somewhere in the marsh, very small and very clear, came a sound like somebody whistling a single note through a tiny silver pipe.

Peep.

Then, a little way off, another. Peep. And another.

"What is that?" whispered Beau.

Great-Grandpappy Buck was smiling, a slow wide smile, the kind that started at his whiskers and went all the way up.

"Spring peepers," he said. "Little frogs. Each one no bigger than the end of your thumb. Only a few sing in the daytime. But at dusk, the whole marsh sings, hundreds of them, so loud you can't hear yourself think." He shook his head. "I'd forgotten. I'd forgotten all about the peepers. This is it. This is the frog." He laughed. "I drew it big because I loved it."

They stood at the edge of the marsh and listened to the few peepers sing. Beau crept forward along a tussock, one paw at a time, as Sadie Belle had taught him, and peered into the reeds, and at last he saw one: a tiny tan-coloured frog, with a dark cross on its back, clinging to a reed stem with its throat puffed out into a shining bubble bigger than its own head, singing with all its might.

"There's one!" breathed Beau. "Rusty! Come and look! It's got a balloon!"

But Rusty was looking at the map.

"That's nice," he said. "But it's not the X. Come on. We've got miles to go."

And he set off, quickly, along the edge of the marsh, towards the trees, where the dotted line went.

Beau looked at the tiny frog for one more moment. Then he sighed, and followed.

Buck went tap, tap, tap at the back, and looked back at the marsh over his shoulder as they left it behind.


Chapter Four — The Shortcut

Past the marsh, the ground began to climb, and the trouble got worse.

The dotted line on the map went the long way round. It went away to the right, along the bottom of a slope, in a great slow curve, past the wavy lines with the dot in the middle. Then it came back again to the left, past the tree with a face, before it started climbing up between the three pointed hills.

Beau looked at the curve. He looked at the hill in front of them. He looked over Rusty's shoulder at the compass, and at the map turned the right way round, with its little sun to the east.

"It goes all the way round," he said. "Look. Miles round. But the three hills are that way." He pointed straight up the slope. "If we go straight up, it's half the distance."

Rusty looked at the slope. It was steep, and thick with laurel bushes, which in that part of the mountains grow so close together that you can hardly see between them.

"That's laurel," said Rusty. "I know laurel. Nobody gets through laurel."

"I could," said Beau. "Bobcats are made for squeezing."

Buck looked at the slope too. He pointed with his stick at a big grey rock, a little way up, at the edge of the bushes.

"As far as that rock," he said, "and not one step further. And if it's thick, back you come."

So Beau went in, and Rusty, who was not going to let his friend go into laurel on his own, went in after him.

It was like trying to walk through a hedge. Sideways. Uphill. The laurel branches were tough and twisty and grew every which way, and they caught on Beau's fur, and on his vest, and on the backpack, and on each other. Beau pushed through one bush, and found another. He pushed through that, and found a third, even thicker. He tried to go under, and got stuck. He tried to go over, and got more stuck. He got his neckerchief caught on a branch, and when Rusty pulled it free for him, the branch sprang back and flicked them both on the nose.

After a long, scratchy, puffing while, they reached the grey rock, and stopped, and looked round.

They had gone about as far as a stone's throw. And at the bottom of the slope behind them, where they had started, Great-Grandpappy Buck was sitting on a fallen log, quite comfortably, eating one of Reba's oat biscuits.

"Any luck?" he called up.

Beau looked at the wall of laurel above the rock, which went on and on up the slope, as far as he could see. He looked at Rusty, who had a laurel leaf stuck behind one ear and a scratch on his nose.

"Told you," said Rusty, but kindly.

So they came back down, which took almost as long as going up, and Buck stood up from his log, and brushed the crumbs off his dungarees, and they set off along the long slow curve of the dotted line at the bottom of the slope, tap, tap, tap.

Beau did not say anything for quite a while. His ears were down.

"The thing about the youngster who drew that map," said Buck after a while, to the air in general, "is that he'd been up that slope himself, the first year. Straight through the laurel."

Beau's ears came up a little.

"Did he get through?"

"He did not," said Buck. "He got stuck in it for an hour and came home without a hat. That's why the line goes round."


Chapter Five — The Wavy Lines

The dotted line led them along the bottom of the slope, through a wood of tall tulip trees just coming into leaf, so that the light came down green and gold. There were flowers everywhere underfoot: white trilliums, three petals each, nodding on their stems, and little blue violets, and something pink and frilly that Beau did not know the name of.

Beau kept stopping to look at them. Rusty kept stopping to wait for Beau, and checking the compass, and looking up the hill. And Buck went tap, tap, tap, at exactly the same speed, and somehow, without ever seeming to hurry, never fell behind.

And then, round a mossy boulder, in a little dip under the tulip trees, they found the wavy lines.

It was a spring. A real spring, coming up out of the ground. There was a small round pool, no bigger than a washtub, with a floor of pale golden sand, and the water was so clear that it was hard to tell where the air stopped and the water started. And in the middle of the sand floor, there was a little spot where the sand was moving. Bubbling and dancing and swirling round and round, all on its own, as if something underneath it were breathing.

"Boiling!" said Beau. "Reba was right! Water that bubbles! That's the dot! That's where the water's coming up!"

They knelt at the edge, all three of them, and watched the sand dance.

"Water from deep inside the mountain," said Buck. "Pushing up through the sand. It's been coming up here, just like that, since long before I was born." He dipped his paw in, and lifted it out, dripping. "Feel that. Go on. That's the coldest water in the whole of Hickory Hollow."

They dipped their paws in. It was so cold it made Beau gasp, and so clear that his paw under the water looked closer than it really was.

"That's two," said Rusty, ticking them off on the map. "The frog. The wavy lines. Tree with a face next." He stood up. "Come on, then."

But Beau did not stand up. He was still lying on his tummy at the edge of the spring, with his chin on his paws, watching the sand dance.

"In a minute," he said dreamily.

Rusty looked at him. Then he looked at the map. Then, for the first time all morning, he looked at the spring, properly, the bright golden sand turning over and over and over, never stopping, never still.

He sat down again.

Not for long. But he sat down.


Chapter Six — The Tree With a Face

They ate their picnic by the spring, because Buck said it was the best place for a picnic that he had ever known, and they were inclined to believe him. Reba's tin held oat biscuits, and cheese, and apple slices, and three small honey cakes, and a note that said: PLEASE TELL ME WHAT THE TREASURE IS, I WILL NOT EAT YOUR SHARE IF IT IS FOOD.

Maverick came gliding down through the tulip trees while they were eating, and landed on the mossy boulder.

"You're doing well," he said. "I've been watching from up top. Saw everything. Laurel, eh? I could have told you that from a mile up." He fluffed his feathers. "You're nearly at the three hills."

"The three hills?" said Rusty eagerly. "You can see them? Where?"

"Can't see them from in here," said Maverick. "But from up there, there's three rocky knobs on the shoulder of Sunrise Ridge, all in a row. Like the peaks on a crown. Your dotted line goes right between them, I'd say." He shook out his feathers. "But I couldn't see a tree with a face. Plenty of trees. None of them were looking at me."

After lunch, the dotted line began to climb again, gently, along an old path. Except that the old path was hardly there at all. It was so grown over with ferns and brambles and young trees that it kept vanishing altogether, and Rusty kept losing it, and having to come back.

"It's gone again," he said, for the third time, standing in a sea of ferns with the map in his paw. "It just stops. There's no path."

Beau remembered what Sadie Belle had said.

Look at the ground. Slowly. Old paths leave old marks.

He did not rush forward. He stood still, and looked. Slowly. At the ground, and at the ferns, and then, because the ferns did not tell him anything, at the trees.

And there, on the trunk of a big old oak, about as high as Buck's shoulder, was a mark. A long, smooth, grey scar in the bark, the shape of a long upside-down drop of water, old and healed and grown over at the edges.

"Rusty," said Beau. "Look. There's a mark."

He went to the next big tree uphill, and looked. There was another one, the same shape, at the same height. And on the next tree, another.

"Blazes," said Buck, very softly, behind them. "Trail blazes. You cut a little bit of bark off, so you can find your way back. Somebody marked this path, a long, long time ago." He put his paw on the old scar, and stood there for a moment. "That was my father, with his knife, and me beside him, holding the map. I was so proud of them."

They followed the blazes, tree to tree, with Beau looking.

And at the very last blaze, the path came out from under the oaks onto a sunny shoulder of hillside, and there in front of them was the biggest, oldest oak tree that either of them had ever seen. Its trunk was so wide that all three of them together could not have reached round it. And halfway up the trunk, where an old branch had come off long ago, the bark had grown into a great round lumpy swelling, with two dark knotholes for eyes, and a long crack below them for a mouth, curving up at the ends.

It was smiling at them.

"The tree with a face," whispered Beau.

They stood and looked up at it. And it looked down at them, with its knothole eyes and its long, old, crooked smile, as it had looked down at a young beaver and his father, with a map, a very long time ago.


Chapter Seven — Between the Three Hills

From the tree with a face, they could see the three hills.

They were not really hills. Maverick was right. They were three rocky knobs, grey and lichened, standing up out of the green shoulder of Sunrise Ridge in a row, like the peaks on a crown. And the dotted line went right up between the middle one and the one on the left, and over the top, to the X.

"Nearly there," said Rusty.

The last part was the steepest. The path went up in a zigzag, back and forth across the slope, which was slow, but which, Buck pointed out, was a great deal easier than going straight up, especially for somebody with a walking-stick and a great many birthdays behind him.

Halfway up, the zigzag came out onto a great flat shelf of grey stone that jutted out over the valley, and Buck sat down on it, leaning on his walking-stick, puffing.

"Sorry, young'uns," he said. "Old legs."

Rusty stared at the flat grey shelf.

"This is Lookout Rock," he said. "It's my favourite place in the whole Hollow. I come up here all the time. But I always come the other way, by the top path." He looked down at the zigzag they had climbed. "I never knew you could get here from the bottom."

They sat down on either side of Buck. And while they were sitting there, getting their breath back, they turned round.

They had been climbing all afternoon with their faces to the hill. They had not once looked back.

Below them, the whole of Hickory Hollow lay spread out in the late afternoon sun, all green and gold. They could see the creek, winding silver down the valley, all the way to Grandpappy's Bridge. They could see the marsh, a bright green patch beside it, where the peepers were. They could see the tulip trees, where the spring was hidden, and the dark line of the laurel slope, and the big oak with the face. They could see the Old Mill, tiny as a toy, with its wheel turning. They could see the smoke from Rose Arch Cottage, and the roof of Buck's lodge, and the beaver ponds shining like dropped coins.

They could see the whole of the day. Every bit of it. All laid out below them, in one piece, like a map.

"Oh," said Rusty.

Buck did not say anything. He just sat on his rock between them, and got his breath back, and looked.

Then they got up, and went on, up the last zigzag, between the middle knob and the one on the left, side by side, with Buck's stick going tap, tap, tap. And came out at the top.


Chapter Eight — The X

There was nothing there.

That was the first thing they saw. Nothing at all. The top of the shoulder was a wide, flat, grassy place, open to the sky, with a few big grey rocks, and the wind blowing gently across it, and the sun going low in the west over Weather Ridge.

And in the middle of the flat grassy place, there was a stump.

It was the biggest stump Rusty had ever seen. It was so wide that you could have laid a table on it for ten. But it was old and grey and soft, crumbling at the edges, covered in moss and lichen, with a soft rotten hollow in the middle.

Rusty stood beside it with the map in his paw. He looked at the X. He looked at the stump. He looked at the X again.

"This is it," he said slowly. "This is where the X is. But there's nothing here. It's just a stump."

He turned round.

"Grandpappy," he said, and his voice had gone small. "There's nothing here."

But Great-Grandpappy Buck was not listening. He had walked quietly across the grass to the stump, and put out his paw, and laid it flat on the old, crumbling wood. And he stood there, with his paw on the stump and his eyes closed, until Beau began to worry about him.

"Grandpappy?" said Beau.

Buck opened his eyes.

"I remember now," he said quietly. "It was a chestnut tree."

Rusty and Beau looked at each other.

"The biggest tree in the whole of the mountains," said Buck. "An American chestnut. They used to grow all up and down these ridges, in the old days. Tall as a church. And this one was the biggest of them all. It stood right here, all on its own, on top of the world."

He stopped, and breathed, and went on more quietly.

"Every summer it was covered in long white flowers, so many that the whole tree looked like it had snow on it in July. And every autumn it dropped chestnuts, sweet ones, so many you could fill a basket in a morning. I used to come up every spring, with my father, and my map, to see it wake up. To say hello."

"What happened to it?" said Beau.

"A sickness came," said Buck. "Long ago, when I was a young beaver. A blight, they called it. It came through all the mountains, and it got into nearly every wild chestnut there was, and one by one, they died. This one too. The chestnuts we roast at Harvest Home come from the little orchard trees down at the farms, a different kind, from far away, that the sickness doesn't touch." He patted the stump. "I suppose I stopped coming up after that. And then I forgot the way. And then I forgot I'd ever drawn a map at all."

Rusty looked down at the map in his paw, with its frog, and its wavy lines, and its tree with a face, and its three hills, and its big X, gone over so many times the ink had nearly worn through.

THE BEST THING IN THE WHOLE HOLLOW.

"So the map led nowhere," he said. "All this way. All day. And it was nowhere."

He sat down in the grass with his back against the stump, and put his chin on his knees.


Chapter Nine — Green

It was Beau who saw it.

He had not sat down. He had been walking round the edge of the great stump, looking at the ground, as Sadie Belle had taught him, because looking slowly had found the blazes, and he thought it might find something else.

And on the far side of the stump, where the roots went down into the earth, it did.

"Rusty," he said. "Grandpappy. Come here. Come and look at this."

They came round.

Growing up out of the ground, right at the foot of the old stump, out of the old roots, there were shoots. Young shoots. A whole cluster of them, thin and straight and reddish-brown, as tall as Beau's shoulder. And on every one of them, just unfolding in the spring sun, there were leaves. Long, bright green leaves, with big hooked teeth all the way down both edges, like the edge of a saw.

Buck stared at them.

Then he bent down, slowly, with a creak of old knees, and took one of the leaves gently between his claws, and turned it over, and looked at it.

"Chestnut," he said. His voice had gone rough. "That's chestnut. Those are chestnut leaves."

"But you said it died," said Rusty.

"The tree died," said Buck. "The trunk. Everything above the ground. But the roots..." He touched the reddish shoot. "The roots must still be alive. Down there in the dark. All these years. Sending up new shoots, every spring."

He straightened up, and looked at the little cluster of green shoots in the low gold light, with the wide old stump behind them.

"I never knew," he said. "In all these years. I never came back to see."

Rusty looked at the shoots. He looked at the stump. Then he turned round, and looked back the way they had come, down the long green shoulder of Sunrise Ridge, past the three rocky knobs and Lookout Rock, to where the whole of Hickory Hollow lay spread out below in the evening light. The creek. The marsh. The tulip trees. The big oak with its crooked smile. All the way back to Grandpappy's Bridge.

He looked at it for a while.

Then he folded up the old map, very carefully, along its old soft creases, and held it out to Buck, and Buck took it, and put it back in the bib pocket of his dungarees, and patted it.

Then he leaned on his walking-stick, and looked out over the Hollow, and said, in his slow, warm, mountain voice, "The creek gets where it's going by never being in a hurry."


Chapter Ten — Here

Maverick came gliding down out of the gold evening sky just as the sun touched Weather Ridge, and landed on the stump with a thump.

"Time," he said. "The miller and Beau's father are on their way up the zigzag with the lanterns, just as Buck arranged. They'll meet you at the tree with a face. I'm off to my roost before it's dark. Eagles do not do dark. Not even magnificent ones." He shook out his wings, and lifted off, and was gone.

So they went down, slowly, to the tree with a face, and sat in a row with their backs against the great old oak, and waited, while the sky went from gold to rose to a soft deep blue.

While there was still light enough to see, Rusty asked Buck if he could borrow the old map one more time, and the stub of pencil from Buck's pocket.

Buck gave him both.

Rusty unfolded the map on his knee. He did not change anything. He did not cross anything out. He only went along the dotted line, from the little house at the bottom to the X at the top, and beside every one of the young Buck's careful pictures, the frog, and the wavy lines with the dot, and the tree with a face, and the three hills, he wrote one small word, very neatly, in pencil.

HERE.

Then, beside the big X at the top, gone over and over until the ink had nearly worn through, he drew a tiny green shoot, with saw-toothed leaves, and wrote it once more.

HERE.

He folded the map up again, and gave it back to Buck, with the pencil.

Buck unfolded it, and held it up to the last of the light, and looked at it for a while. He did not say anything. He folded it up again, very carefully, along its old soft creases, and put it back in the bib pocket of his dungarees, over his heart, and buttoned the pocket down.

And then, from far below them in the gathering dark, down where the creek bent away towards the trees, the marsh began to sing.

It started with one. Peep. And then another. And then, all at once, as if somebody had given a signal, the whole marsh rang with it. Hundreds of tiny silver whistles, high and bright and everywhere, rising up the dark side of Sunrise Ridge to where they sat. Even from so far away, it was so loud and so lovely that Beau put his paws over his mouth so as not to laugh out loud.

"Every spring," said Buck softly. "Every spring, all my life, they've been doing that. And I'd forgotten."

Below them, round the bend of the zigzag, a single warm light appeared, and then a second, and the miller's voice called up the hill to ask whether there was an old beaver up there who wanted walking home.

Rusty stood up, and helped Buck to his feet, and Beau picked up the backpack, and they went down together to meet the lanterns.

They went home past the marsh. It was dark by then, and cool, and smelling of wet earth and new leaves, and the peepers were singing so loudly as they went by that nobody could hear anybody else say a single word. So nobody tried. They just walked, all five of them, along the edge of the singing marsh in the warm lantern light, with the little silver whistles ringing on every side, and Benny's compass safe in Rusty's pocket, to go back to its owner in the morning.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
