Chapter One — A Feather on the Path

It was Rusty who found the first one.

He was trotting along the old trail at the edge of the Hickory Woods early one autumn morning, nose down, tail up, on his way to Fern Meadow. Rusty was a young red fox, quick and lively, with a white-tipped tail and sharp amber eyes, and he knew every trail in the Hollow, and most of the ones that were not trails at all.

The woods were beautiful that morning. The hickories had turned to gold and the maples to flame, and the low sun came slanting between the trunks in long bright bars, so that the whole trail looked striped like a cat. The leaves were thick underfoot and crackled when you stepped on them.

And there, lying on top of the leaves, right in the middle of a bar of sunlight, was a feather.

Rusty stopped so suddenly that his back legs nearly overtook his front ones.

It was not an ordinary feather. It was about as long as his paw, and the top side of it was a soft brownish grey, like any woodland feather. But the shaft running down its middle was bright yellow, as yellow as a buttercup, and when Rusty picked it up and turned it over, the whole underside glowed a warm, rich gold, as though somebody had dipped it in sunlight and left it to dry.

"Well, I never," said Rusty softly.

He was a scout, and a scout notices things. So he slowed right down and noticed.

The second feather was lying at the foot of the fallen oak, half under a curl of bark. The third was caught on a bramble, twirling in the breeze like a little golden flag. The fourth was beside the old dead pine snag at the edge of the clearing, a tall grey trunk with no branches left, full of holes, where nothing much ever happened.

Rusty sat down on the trail and laid all four feathers on a flat stone in a row.

They were all the same. Brown on top. Gold underneath. Not one of them was broken or bent. They looked as if they had simply fallen, softly, from somewhere, and been left behind.

He looked up into the trees. He saw golden leaves, a patch of blue sky, and a squirrel, who looked back at him and said nothing helpful.

"Somebody," said Rusty, "is going about these woods dropping gold."

He gathered the feathers up and held them very gently in his mouth, which is not easy for a fox and made him look as if he had grown a golden moustache. Then he set off for Fern Meadow at a run, because there was only one creature in the whole Hollow who would know about feathers.


Chapter Two — Maverick Has a Theory

Maverick was in the middle of Fern Meadow, practising his landing.

He did this most mornings. He would fly up so high that he was only a speck against the blue, and then come swooping down in a long, beautiful curve, with his great brown wings spread wide and his white head gleaming, and land on the old fence post with a perfect flourish.

At least, that was the idea.

This morning he came swooping down, curved beautifully, spread his wings, reached for the fence post with his yellow talons, missed it entirely, and landed in a clump of thistles.

"Meant to do that," he said, climbing out. "Thistle landing. Very advanced."

Maverick was a young bald eagle and the most confident creature in the Hollow. He wore a tan explorer's vest covered in mountain patches and a red neckerchief, and he had a way of standing with his chest out that made smaller creatures want to stand like that too. He was brave and loyal and kind, and he could fly higher than anybody in the valley. He was also, now and then, a little too sure of himself.

"Maverick!" called Rusty, laying the feathers carefully on top of the fence post. "Look at these!"

Maverick bent down until his beak was almost touching them. His blue eyes went wide.

"Where did you find these?"

"On the old trail. Between the fallen oak and the pine snag. Just lying there."

Maverick picked one up in his beak and turned it in the sunlight. The gold underside glowed.

"Rusty," he said, in a hushed and important voice, "there is only one bird in the whole wide world that could have golden feathers like this."

"Who?"

Maverick drew himself up to his full height, which was considerable.

"A golden eagle," he said.

Rusty's mouth fell open.

"Here? In the Hollow?"

"Golden feathers. Golden eagle. It's obvious." His chest swelled. "A magnificent golden cousin of mine, come all the way over the mountains. And I'll tell you why."

"Why?"

"To meet me," said Maverick. "Word must have got about that there's a very impressive young eagle in Hickory Hollow."

Rusty looked at the feather. Something at the back of his mind gave a small prickle, like a burr in his fur. It was not a doubt, exactly. It was more like a question that had not finished growing.

"That's one idea," he said slowly. "But I've got another."

"Better than a golden eagle?"

"Different," said Rusty. He laid the four feathers in a line along the top of the post, the way he had found them, one after another. "Look. They were all along the same trail, one after the other, like this. What if somebody's leaving them on purpose? Like a trail of clues? A treasure trail?" His amber eyes lit up. "And if we follow it to the end, we'll find the treasure."

Maverick considered this. A golden eagle was a very fine idea. But a treasure trail was also a very fine idea.

"We could do both," he said. "We could follow the treasure trail, and at the end of it, there might be a golden eagle."

"Sitting on the treasure," said Rusty.

"Exactly," said Maverick.

They looked at each other and grinned.


Chapter Three — The Trail That Went in a Circle

They set off at once, back to the old trail, to follow the clues.

Rusty went in front, nose to the ground. Maverick flew just above the treetops, peering down with his sharp eagle eyes. Every time Rusty found a golden feather, he barked, and every time he barked, Maverick swooped down to look, and made a mark on a scrap of birch bark with a stub of charcoal held in his beak, which was very difficult and came out mostly as smudges.

They found a feather by the fallen oak. They found another a little way past the brambles. Then two more, close together, near the pine snag.

Then another, on the far side of the pine snag.

Then another, a little further round, back towards the brambles.

Then one by the fallen oak again.

Rusty stopped. He looked at the fallen oak. He looked back along the trail. He sat down with a thump.

"Maverick," he said. "Come down here."

Maverick landed on the oak trunk. He looked at Rusty's face.

"What?"

"We've gone round in a circle," said Rusty. "They're all round the pine snag. Not going anywhere. If it was a treasure trail, it would lead somewhere. This just goes round and round that old dead tree."

Maverick looked at his birch bark, which was mostly smudge, and tried to make it say something else. It would not.

"Maybe the treasure is in the tree," he suggested.

Rusty walked all the way round the pine snag twice, sniffing. It was grey and dead and full of round holes, big and small, like a cheese. There was no treasure. There were only a few more golden feathers at its foot, and the smell of old wood.

"No treasure," said Rusty flatly. "It isn't a treasure trail. I was wrong."

He did not enjoy saying it. But it was true. He had followed the trail, and the trail had told him so.

Maverick patted him on the back with a wing, which nearly knocked him over.

"Never mind," he said kindly. "That just leaves the golden eagle."

"Does it?" said Rusty.

"Of course. If it isn't a treasure trail, it must be my cousin. Who else could it be?"

Rusty opened his mouth to argue, and shut it again, because he could not think of anybody else either.

That evening, before anybody could stop him, Maverick flew to the very top of the tallest hickory and made an announcement loud enough for the whole valley to hear.

"Friends! Neighbours! A golden eagle has come to Hickory Hollow! The day after tomorrow, at noon, in Fern Meadow, there will be a Grand Golden Welcome! Bring flowers! Bring cake! Bring your best manners!"

Down below, the whole Hollow cheered.

Rusty did not cheer. He told his mother where he was going, and went back to the old trail before supper, while it was still light, and did something a scout knows how to do. With a broom of pine twigs, he swept every last leaf and feather off the stretch of trail around the pine snag, until it was bare brown earth. Then he went home.

"If there are new feathers tomorrow," he said to himself, "then whoever drops them comes back. And then I'll know one more true thing."


Chapter Four — For Certain Sure

There were new feathers the next morning. Three of them, lying on the bare swept earth at the foot of the pine snag, gold side up.

By then the whole Hollow had gone golden-eagle mad.

Charlie sat on top of the meadow fence all morning, shading her eyes with her paw and scanning the sky. Barney had heard it was as big as a cabin. One of the little rabbits had heard it could fly to the moon. Reba had heard that it was very partial to honey cakes, though she could not remember who had told her, and suspected it might have been herself.

And Maverick flew from one end of the valley to the other, telling everybody. With every telling, the golden eagle grew a little bigger and a little grander, until by lunchtime it had a crown of golden feathers and had once flown over the highest mountain in the world without stopping for breath.

Rusty went to see Great-Grandpappy Buck.

He found him on his porch in the afternoon sun, rocking slowly with Bramble's quilt across his knees and his spectacles on his nose. Rusty told him everything: the feathers, the golden eagle, the treasure trail that went in a circle, the swept earth and the three new feathers.

Buck listened without a word. Then he held out his paw for a feather, and turned it over in the light for a long time.

"Before you tell me what you think," he said at last, "tell me what you know. Not what Maverick thinks. Not what you'd like. What do you know about these feathers, Rusty, for certain sure?"

Rusty opened his mouth to say golden eagle, and stopped, because he did not know that. Not for certain sure.

He thought hard.

"I know they're as long as my paw," he said slowly. "I know they're brown on top and gold underneath. I know they're all round that one dead pine, not going anywhere. And I know there were new ones this morning, because I swept the trail last night and three more came." He paused. "And I know they're not broken. They look like they just fell out."

"That's five things," said Buck. "Five good, true things." He nodded slowly. "Do any of your five things say eagle?"

Rusty looked at the feather for a long moment.

"No," he said. "Not one."

Buck said nothing. He only rocked, and waited.

Rusty frowned at the feather. New ones overnight. All round the same tree. Fallen, not broken. And then the pieces came together in his head, the way a path through the woods comes together when you have walked it often enough.

"Whoever it is," he said slowly, "came back to that pine snag since last night. And they'd been and gone before I got there, because the feathers are already down by breakfast." He sat up straight. "So if I want to see who it is, I have to be there before breakfast. Before dawn. Hidden. Still. And watch."

Buck's whiskers twitched.

"That sounds like a scout's plan to me," he said. "And I'll come and sit by the meadow gate on my stool, with a flask of something hot. Near enough to call if you need me. Far enough that I won't frighten anybody off." He handed the feather back. "And I think you'd better take a friend."

"Maverick won't want to come," said Rusty. "He already knows."

"Ask him anyway," said Buck.


Chapter Five — The Dawn Watch

Maverick did come. He grumbled about it all the way along the trail in the grey half-dark.

"I don't see why we need to watch," he said. "I already know what we're going to see. A golden eagle. Probably wearing a crown."

"Then you can be the first to say hello," said Rusty.

That cheered Maverick up a great deal.

It was cold, the sharp, clean cold of an autumn dawn, and the grass was grey with dew. Every breath came out as a little cloud. Behind them, at the meadow gate, they could just see the shape of Great-Grandpappy Buck on his folding stool with a blanket round his shoulders, and the small, steady glow of his lantern, and every so often, the glint of his spectacles as he turned his head to check where they were.

Rusty chose the spot: a hollow among the ferns at the foot of a great hickory, with a clear view of the pine snag and the swept earth around it.

"Now we lie still," he whispered. "No talking."

"Eagles don't lie in ferns," whispered Maverick.

"This one does," whispered Rusty. "Pretend you're a log. A log doesn't fidget. A log doesn't wonder what's for breakfast. A log just lies there and listens."

Maverick tried very hard to be a log. He lasted almost a minute. Then a beetle walked up his beak and sat down between his eyes, and he went cross-eyed watching it, and Rusty had to bury his nose in the moss to keep from laughing out loud.

The sky grew paler. The mist lifted off the meadow in long white curls. A wren began to sing, and then a cardinal, and then the whole wood woke up at once.

Rusty knew every song. He lay with his ears pricked, sorting them one by one: wren, cardinal, chickadee, blue jay, the soft coo of a dove.

And then a call he did not know.

It was loud and clear and ringing. Kee-yer! Then a quick, bubbling, laughing run of notes: wick-a-wick-a-wick!

Rusty's ears swivelled. He could hear it. But in the dim light among all those trunks, with the sound bouncing about, he could not tell where it came from.

"Where?" he breathed. "I can't see anything. It's too dark under the trees."

Maverick was not cross-eyed any more. His head had come up out of the ferns. His blue eyes were wide and sharp and still, the way only an eagle's eyes can be, and he was staring at a spot high on the side of the pine snag that, to Rusty, was nothing but grey shadow.

"There," whispered Maverick. "Second hole down from the top. On the left. Something's looking out."

Rusty stared. He saw a hole. He saw a shadow. That was all.

"Are you sure?"

"I'm an eagle," whispered Maverick. "I can see a beetle on a leaf from the other side of the valley. There's a bird in that hole. A brown one. With a red mark on its head."

And then, from inside the hole, it came out.


Chapter Six — The Golden Secret

It was a bird, but not an eagle. Not by a long way.

It was about the size of a pigeon, with a soft brown back barred all over with black, and a pale chest covered in round black spots, as if somebody had flicked a paintbrush at it. It had a black crescent across the top of its chest like a little bib, a bright red mark on the back of its grey head, and a black stripe on each cheek like a moustache.

It clung to the side of the pine snag with its feet, propped on its stiff tail, and looked about. Then it called, kee-yer!, and dropped down to the ground.

Two more came after it, from two more holes. Then a fourth, from the trees.

They hopped about on the swept earth, poking their long beaks among the moss. Then one of them hopped back onto the snag and drummed on the dead wood with its beak. Rat-a-tat-tat-tat-tat! The sound rang across the clearing like somebody knocking on a hollow door.

"Woodpeckers," breathed Rusty. "That's why the snag's full of holes. They live in it."

Maverick let out a small, disappointed sigh.

"Brown," he whispered. "Spotty. Not golden at all. Come on. The golden eagle must be somewhere else."

But Rusty did not move. He was watching the nearest bird the way Buck had told him: watching, and waiting, and seeing.

The bird stopped poking at the ground. It fluffed up all its feathers until it looked twice its size, and gave itself a long, thorough shake from beak to tail. Then it began to preen, reaching under its wing and drawing each feather through its beak, one by one.

Something drifted down from under its wing and settled on the earth.

Then the bird took off.

And as it spread its wings, the whole underside of them flashed gold. Bright, rich, buttery gold, every feather, and the underneath of its tail too, and a big white patch just above its tail that shone like a lamp as it swooped away through the trees. Kee-yer! Wick-a-wick-a-wick!

Then the others were up too, all four, flashing gold, and away.

Rusty and Maverick stared after them.

Then Rusty crept forward and picked up the thing that had fallen. Brown on top. Gold underneath. A shaft as yellow as a buttercup.

"They're not from a golden eagle," he said quietly. "They're from these. They're only gold underneath, where you see it when they fly. And they're losing feathers."

"Moulting," said Maverick, in a small voice. "Old feathers out, new feathers in. I do it every summer." He looked at the feather. "I just didn't think of it."

They could not simply ask the birds about it, either. The flickers were wild, wary things that kept to themselves, and they had already gone off about their own business in a flash of gold, without so much as a backward glance.

Maverick looked towards Fern Meadow, where the sun was coming up gold behind the fence post.

"Rusty," he said. "The Grand Golden Welcome. It's today. At noon. I told everybody. The whole Hollow."

"I know," said Rusty.

"They're bringing flowers. And cake. Reba's baking honey cakes for a golden eagle that isn't real. That I made up."

He put his head down on his chest and did not say anything else.


Chapter Seven — Two Ways to Go

They walked back towards the meadow very slowly. At the gate, Great-Grandpappy Buck lifted a paw from his stool to show he had seen them safely back. But they had things to talk over on their own first, so they sat down on the grass a little way off, still in sight of him.

"I could just say it flew away," said Maverick at last.

Rusty looked at him.

"I could say it came in the night, and I saw it, and it was magnificent, and it had to fly home over the mountains. Nobody would know."

Rusty scuffed at the grass. It would be easy. He was a fox who knew every shortcut in the Hollow, and he knew which ones led somewhere good and which ones led you into the brambles.

"That'd be a shortcut," he said.

"Exactly," said Maverick, brightening.

"The kind that looks quick," said Rusty, "but then you're stuck in the briars going round and round, and every autumn somebody asks about the golden eagle, and you have to make up a bit more, and Charlie sits on that fence every year looking at the sky."

Maverick's wings drooped.

"I didn't mean to make it up," he said. "I saw the feather and thought gold, eagle, me, and it all went together so nicely. It felt right. I didn't think I needed to check."

"I didn't check either, at first," said Rusty. "I thought it was a treasure trail. I wanted that to be true too."

They sat in silence for a while.

"What would you do?" said Maverick.

"I'd go to the meadow at noon," said Rusty, "and tell everybody what we really found. And I'd show them the birds."

Maverick stood up. He shook out his feathers and retied his red neckerchief with a big, firm knot.

"Eagles," he said, "do not take shortcuts."

"Since when?"

"Since about one minute ago." Then his feathers drooped again, just a little. "But what if everybody laughs? What if they think I'm a silly show-off?"

"Some might laugh a bit," said Rusty honestly. "Barney, probably. Barney laughs at everything. But I'll be standing right next to you. I was in it too."

Maverick looked at him.

"In front of everybody?"

"Course. I found the first feather. Half of it's my fault."

"Most of it is my fault," said Maverick.

"Then I'll stand a bit to the side," said Rusty, and Maverick laughed, for the first time that morning.

"And afterwards," said Rusty, "when it's all over, you can show me how you land on that fence post. Properly. Without the thistles."

"I promise," said Maverick. "This evening. You'll see."


Chapter Eight — The Grand Golden Welcome

By noon, Fern Meadow was full.

The whole Hollow had come. There were garlands of autumn leaves strung between the fence posts. Maggie Mae had brought a lace cloth for the table, and Reba had brought three plates of honey cakes, of which only two and a half remained. The rabbit family had practised a special song of welcome, with a high part for the little ones and a low part for their father, and they were bursting to sing it.

Charlie was sitting on top of the fence post again, shading her eyes.

"Is it coming? Is it coming yet?"

Maverick landed in the middle of the meadow. He did it perfectly, for once, without a wobble, but he did not look as if he had noticed. Rusty came trotting up and stood beside him. Then, after a moment, with a sideways wink at Maverick, he shuffled a little to the side.

The meadow went quiet.

"Friends," said Maverick. His voice was not as loud as it had been on top of the hickory. "There isn't a golden eagle. There never was. I guessed. I guessed wrong, and then I told everybody my guess as if it were true. Rusty kept asking questions, and went and looked, and found out. I'm sorry."

There was a long silence.

Charlie's face went very still. The littlest rabbit looked down at the grass.

Maverick hopped across to the fence post and bent his white head down until his eyes were level with Charlie's.

"Charlie," he said. "I'm especially sorry to you. You were looking at the sky all yesterday, for something that was never going to come."

Charlie's lip wobbled. Then she straightened her bow.

"It's all right," she said bravely. "I liked waiting. I just wish it had been real."

"Well, I think it was a brilliant guess," said a cheerful voice from the crowd.

It was Barney, the younger beaver brother, standing on a bench to see over Brody. "I'd have guessed golden eagle too," he said. "Golden feathers! What else would you think? Or a golden goose. Or a chicken that fell in the honey pot."

The meadow burst out laughing. It was a warm laugh, not an unkind one, and Maverick's shoulders came down from round his ears.

"Does this mean," asked Brody, in a worried voice, "that we can't have the cake?"

"Of course we can have the cake," said Reba at once, holding her plates a little closer. "Cake isn't only for golden eagles. Cake is for anybody who walked all the way to the meadow. That's the rule."

"Is that a rule?" said Benny.

"It is now," said Reba.

Sadie Belle stepped forward, very gently, and touched Maverick's wing with her nose.

"It was brave," she said softly, "to stand up and say it in front of everybody. I don't think I could have."

Maverick did not know what to say to that. So for once in his life, he did not say anything at all. He only fluffed up his feathers a little, the way eagles do when they are pleased, and looked at his feet.

"So what are these spotty brown birds," called Great-Grandpappy Buck from the edge of the meadow, "with gold under their wings? I'd very much like to see one."

"We can show you," said Rusty. "They're at the old pine snag. If everybody goes very slowly, and very quietly, and doesn't sneeze."

"I don't sneeze," said Maverick. "Much."

So the whole Hollow went to see.


Chapter Nine — Kee-Yer

It took a long time to get the whole of Hickory Hollow along the old trail quietly, but they managed it.

They crept up to the edge of the clearing in a long line of ears and noses and whiskers, peering round tree trunks and through the ferns. Brody had to lie down, because he was so big. Reba held a honey cake in her mouth so that she would not talk. Charlie sat on Maggie Mae's shoulder and held her breath until her cheeks puffed out.

For a while, nothing happened.

Then Maverick, lying beside Rusty in the ferns, went very still. "Second hole from the top," he breathed. "Watch."

From the high hole in the pine snag, a spotty brown head with a red mark on it looked out. Kee-yer!

Down came the birds, three, then four, then five, swooping in to land on the swept earth. They hopped about, poking with their long beaks, bobbing their spotty heads.

"Oh," breathed Charlie. "Oh, they're lovely."

"Are they woodpeckers?" whispered Benny, who liked to know exactly what things were.

"Look at the way that one holds on to the trunk, with its tail pressed flat for a prop," whispered Buck. "That's a woodpecker's trick. But it's a woodpecker that likes the ground better than most, I'd say."

One of the birds hopped onto the snag and drummed. Rat-a-tat-tat-tat-tat!

"That's how they talk across the woods," whispered Rusty. "The hollower the tree, the louder it goes."

Then, all at once, the birds took off together. They burst up into the sunlight, and every one of them flashed gold underneath, golden wings and golden tails and that bright white patch above, shining as they wheeled away through the trees.

There was a gasp from the whole line of watchers. Even Maverick gasped, though he had seen it before.

And floating down through the sunlight, turning slowly over and over, came a single golden feather.

It landed right in front of Charlie.

She picked it up and held it, wonderstruck.

"Grandpappy Buck," she whispered. "What are they called?"

Buck smiled. "I believe," he said, "the old folk used to call them flickers. Because of the way the gold flickers when they fly."

"Flickers," said Charlie, trying the word out. "That's better than golden eagles. That's much, much better. Because they're real."

Then the rabbits, who had come all that way and practised so hard, stood up very quietly in a row at the edge of the clearing and sang their song of welcome to the empty pine snag, the high part and the low part, very softly, so as not to disturb anybody. And somewhere off in the trees, as if it had heard, a flicker called back.

Kee-yer.


Chapter Ten — An Answer Guessed

As the sun went down that evening, Rusty and Maverick went back to Fern Meadow, to the old fence post, because there was something they had promised to do.

Great-Grandpappy Buck came too, slowly, with his walking-stick and his folding stool, and set the stool down by the gate, and settled onto it with a sigh.

"Go on, then," he said. "Let's see it."

The meadow was empty and gold and quiet. The garlands of leaves still hung between the posts, stirring in the evening breeze. A golden feather was tucked into the top pocket of Maverick's explorer's vest, among the mountain patches.

"Evidence," he had said, patting it. "So I remember."

He flew up. Not very high this time. Just high enough. Then he came gliding down in a long, slow, careful curve, with his wings spread wide and his blue eyes fixed on the fence post, and he did not think about how splendid he looked. He only thought about the post.

His talons closed on the top of it.

He wobbled. He flapped. He stayed on.

"I did it," said Maverick, astonished. "I landed it. First time all autumn."

"You were watching the post," said Rusty, "instead of watching yourself."

Maverick thought about that.

"That's quite clever," he said. "Did you just make that up?"

"Mostly," said Rusty.

Great-Grandpappy Buck chuckled from the gate. He looked out at the meadow, at the shadows of the fence posts stretching long across the grass, and at the first star coming out above Sunrise Ridge.

"I've been wondering all day what I'd say to the pair of you," he said. "And I've decided it's only this." He leaned on his walking-stick. "A question asked is never as foolish as an answer guessed."

Neither of them said anything. Rusty looked at the ground and smiled. Maverick looked at the sky and smiled.

The light went from gold to rose to soft grey-blue. The air grew cool. Over in the Hickory Woods, beyond the dark line of the trees, a flicker called once, sleepily, from its hole in the old pine snag, and was quiet.

"Time for home," said Buck, and he stood up and folded his stool.

They walked back across the meadow together in the dusk, the old beaver in the middle with his walking-stick, the fox trotting on one side, and the eagle hopping along the grass on the other, because it seemed a shame to fly when your friends were walking.

At the bridge they said goodnight. Rusty went home the proper way, along the path, with no shortcuts at all. Maverick flew up to his tall hickory, settled on his branch, and looked down over the whole sleeping valley with his sharp eagle eyes, at every dark roof and every glowing window and every quiet path, until the very last light went out.

Then he tucked the golden feather a little deeper into his pocket, and closed his eyes.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
