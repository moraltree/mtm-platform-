Chapter One — A True Gentleman

It started with something Great-Grandpappy Buck said, on the evening after the Blossom Gathering.

The folk from over Weather Ridge had gone home along the low pass that morning, with their kettles clanking, and the old badger had stopped at the edge of Fern Meadow to say goodbye. He had taken off his hat to the miller's wife. He had bowed to Maggie Mae. He had shaken every paw in the Hollow, and thanked everybody for everything, twice, and walked backwards for the first few steps so as not to turn his back on anybody.

"Now there," said Great-Grandpappy Buck, watching him go, "goes a true gentleman."

Everybody on the porch nodded.

And Beau, who was sitting on the top step with his chin on his paws, sat up very straight.

A true gentleman.

He thought about it all evening. He thought about it all night, too, lying in bed, staring at the ceiling. Everybody in the Hollow liked Beau. He knew that. They liked him because he was quick, and brave, and funny, and could jump higher than anybody, and always said yes to an adventure. But nobody had ever, not once, looked at him going past and said: there goes a true gentleman.

By morning, he had decided.

He was going to become the most refined, most well-mannered, most gentlemanly creature in the whole of Hickory Hollow. By Saturday. And then everybody would look at him going past, and say it.

The only question was how.

He thought about who in the Hollow had the very best manners. And the answer came at once.

"Maggie Mae," he said, knocking on the door of Rose Arch Cottage before breakfast, "I need lessons."

Maggie Mae opened the door in her apron and looked at him.

"Lessons in what?"

"Manners," said Beau. "Bowing. And saying things. And napkins. Everything. I want to be a gentleman. A true one. By Saturday."

Maggie Mae looked at him for a while. She looked at his tufted ears, and his golden neckerchief, which was on crooked, and his eager blue eyes, and his tail, which was twitching with excitement.

"Beau," she said slowly. "Are you sure?"

"Everybody likes me," said Beau. "I know that. But they don't say gentleman."

Maggie Mae sighed. Then she stood back and opened the door wider.

"Come in," she said. "And wipe your feet."

Beau wiped his feet. He wiped them so thoroughly, and for so long, that Maggie Mae had to come back and tell him to stop.

"Lesson one," she said, "is knowing when to stop."

Beau did not hear her. He was already practising his bow.


Chapter Two — The Lessons

The lessons took three days.

Maggie Mae taught him to say "please" and "thank you", which he knew already, but she taught him to say them clearly, so people could hear. She taught him to stand up when somebody came into the room, and to offer them his seat. She taught him to say "after you" at a door, and to hold it open. She taught him to put his napkin on his knee at the table, and not to talk with his mouth full, and to pass the bread before taking any himself. She taught him a small, neat bow, just a nod of the head and a dip of the shoulders, the sort of bow you might give the miller's wife if you met her on the bridge.

Beau learned everything. And then he did all of it at once, all the time, as hard as he possibly could.

He stood up whenever anybody came into the room. He stood up when Maggie Mae came into the room. He stood up when Charlie came into the room. He stood up when a bee came in through the window, and offered it his seat.

He said "after you" at every door so many times that nobody could get through any of them, because everybody else, being polite, said "no, after you", and then they all stood there.

He put his napkin on his knee, and then on his other knee, to be safe, and then tucked one in at his collar as well, and then, when Maggie Mae served him a slice of honey cake, he said "Thank you so very much, I am most exceedingly grateful," and bowed over his plate, and got honey on his chin, and on the end of his nose, and on the napkin at his collar, and on Charlie.

"Smaller bow," said Maggie Mae.

Beau bowed again, smaller, and got honey on Charlie's other ear.

His bow was the worst of all. It started as a neat little nod of the head, just as Maggie Mae had shown him. But Beau did not do anything by halves. By the second day, it had become a deep, sweeping bow, from the waist, with one paw out to the side. By the third day, it was so deep that his nose touched the floor, and his tail went straight up in the air behind him, and he had to put both front paws down to stop himself tipping over.

"Beau," said Maggie Mae, on the third afternoon, when he had bowed to the teapot. "Remember lesson one."

"What was lesson one?" said Beau, from the floor.

Maggie Mae closed her eyes for a moment.

"Never mind," she said. "Just try not to bow to anything with a spout."

Benny, who had come round to borrow a cup of sugar, stood in the doorway and watched the bow for a while with his head on one side. Then he took out his notebook.

"On Monday," he said, "your bow went down about as far as a teacup. On Tuesday, as far as a chair seat. Today, it's all the way to the floor." He did a sum. "By Saturday, if it keeps on like this, you'll be bowing underground."

"Thank you, Benny," said Beau, from the floor, with dignity. "Most kind."

At the end of the last lesson, on the Wednesday afternoon, Beau announced that on Saturday afternoon he would hold a Gentleman's Tea Party on Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, for everybody, with cold mint tea and lemonade, because the weather was much too warm for hot. Maggie Mae said she would lend him her cups if he promised not to bow at them.

Then Beau walked home from Rose Arch Cottage feeling more refined than he had ever felt in his life. He said "Good afternoon" to three trees, and bowed to a gatepost.

And as he passed the edge of the Hickory Woods, Sadie Belle, who was coming the other way with her mother and a basket of greens, stopped on the path and looked at him.

"Good afternoon, Sadie Belle," said Beau, and bowed so low that his ear tufts brushed the path. "How very delightful to see you."

Sadie Belle went pink from her ears to her hooves, and could not think of a single thing to say, and dropped her basket.

Beau picked up every leaf for her, with another bow for each one, and handed her the basket, and went on his way, feeling extremely gentlemanly, and never once noticed that she was still standing exactly where he had left her, holding the basket, looking after him down the path.


Chapter Three — After You

On Saturday morning, Beau set out to be a gentleman to the whole of Hickory Hollow.

He had brushed his fur until it shone. He had straightened his neckerchief three times. He had borrowed an old hat from his father's cupboard, so that he would have something to take off to people, and it was a little bit too big, and came down over one ear.

The trouble started at the footbridge.

It was the brothers' bridge over Pebble Run, the one Benny and Barney had built in the autumn, a single stout plank on a stone pier, and it was only wide enough for one creature at a time. Beau got to one end of it at exactly the same moment as Rusty got to the other.

"After you," said Beau, and bowed, and took off his hat.

"Thanks," said Rusty, and stepped onto the bridge.

"No, no," said Beau, holding up his paw. "I insist. After you."

"That's what I'm doing," said Rusty, puzzled. "I'm going after you said after you."

"Ah," said Beau, "but a true gentleman always goes second. So really, it should be after you, after me, after you."

Rusty stopped in the middle of the bridge, and frowned.

"So I go first?"

"Yes."

"But you said..."

"After you," said Beau, and bowed again.

Rusty took one step forward. Beau said "after you" again, out of habit. Rusty took one step back. Beau bowed.

By the time Brody arrived at Rusty's end of the bridge, with a sack of oats on his shoulder for the miller, the two of them were standing at opposite ends, both saying "after you", neither of them moving. Brody waited politely behind Rusty. Then the littlest rabbit and her mother arrived behind Brody. Then the miller came down the path with a wheelbarrow, and stopped behind the rabbits. Then Benny arrived at Beau's end, with his notebook, and stopped behind Beau. Then Barney behind Benny. Then Maverick landed on the rail in the middle, looked at both queues, and said, "What's going on?"

"After you," said Beau.

"After YOU," said Rusty, who had begun to enjoy himself.

"Excuse me," said a very small voice.

It was Charlie. She had come down the path, looked at the two long queues at either end of the plank, and then simply walked a little way up the bank, to where Pebble Run was so narrow and shallow that it was hardly more than a wet line in the grass, and stepped over it, and walked back down the other side.

Everybody looked at her.

"There," said Charlie. "Now you can all go."

And everybody did, all at once, both ways, in a great muddle of paws and oats and wheelbarrow and rabbits, and somehow, in the middle of it, Beau's hat fell off and went rolling away down the bank into the mud, and Barney fetched it back for him.

Beau put the muddy hat back on. It slid over one eye.

"That," said Rusty, as they all finally got off the bridge, "was the longest it's ever taken to cross that bridge, and it's four steps long."

"But it was very polite," said Beau.

Rusty looked at him.

"Was it?" he said.


Chapter Four — Delighted, I'm Sure

The day did not get better.

Beau went to the Old Mill, where the miller's wife was sitting in her big chair by the door, with her bad back, shelling peas. Beau took off his muddy hat, and bowed so low that his nose went into her basket of peas.

"Good morning, madam," he said, muffled. "Delighted, I'm sure."

"Good morning, Beau," said the miller's wife, a little startled, picking a pea out of his ear. "Are you feeling all right?"

"Very much so, thank you most kindly," said Beau, standing up. "May I hold the door for you?"

"I'm not going anywhere, dear," said the miller's wife. "I'm shelling peas."

"Nevertheless," said Beau, and held the Mill door wide open, with a sweeping gesture of his paw, and stood there holding it, waiting for somebody to go through.

Nobody went through. The miller was inside, working. The miller's wife was outside, shelling. A great many flies went through, and the miller came out to see why the Mill was suddenly full of flies, and found Beau holding the door open for nobody, with a muddy hat on.

"Morning, Beau," said the miller. "Would you mind shutting that?"

"After you," said Beau.

On the way back across Fern Meadow, he met Maverick, who was sitting on a fence post, sunning his wings.

Beau stopped, took off his muddy hat, and bowed.

Maverick looked at him. Then, because he was an eagle, and no eagle in the history of the world has ever let anybody out-bow him, he stood up very tall on his post, spread both his great brown wings out wide, and bowed back. Deeper.

Beau bowed again. Deeper still, with his nose in the grass.

Maverick bowed again, so low that his white head went right down below the top of the post, and his tail feathers went straight up in the air, and he had to grip the post very hard with both feet.

Beau bowed so low that he did a complete somersault, and landed on his back in the clover with his legs in the air.

There was a long, wobbly pause on the fence post. Then Maverick, still bowing, very slowly toppled forwards off it, and landed in the clover next to Beau with a great soft thump of feathers.

They lay side by side, looking up at the sky.

"I won," said Maverick.

"You fell off," said Beau.

"Gracefully," said Maverick, and got up, and shook out his feathers, and flew away before anybody could argue.

Beau went to Rose Arch Cottage. Maggie Mae was out. He went to Reba's den, and offered to carry Reba's basket for her, and when she said she was not carrying a basket, he offered to carry Reba instead, and she laughed so much she had to sit down.

He went to Fern Meadow, where the littlest rabbit was playing on her own with a ball. He bowed to her, very deeply, with his hat off.

"Good morrow, little madam," he said, in his very grandest voice. "Pray, might I be of any assistance this fine morning? Might I perchance retrieve your ball?"

The littlest rabbit looked up at him with her eyes very wide. She did not know what good morrow meant. She did not know what pray meant, or perchance, or retrieve. All she knew was that Beau, who usually played chase with her and let her win, and made silly faces, and called her Rabbity, was standing over her in a strange muddy hat, bowing, and talking in a voice she had never heard before, as if he were somebody else entirely.

Her lip wobbled.

"I want the old Beau," she said, and picked up her ball, and ran home to her mother.

Beau stood alone in the middle of Fern Meadow, with his hat in his paw.

He did not understand it at all. He had done everything right. Every bow. Every after you. Every please and thank you, loudly and clearly.

He put the hat back on, and set his jaw.

"The tea party," he said to himself. "This afternoon. That'll show them. Then they'll see."


Chapter Five — The Tea Party

Beau had planned the tea party all week, as the crowning glory of his gentleman day.

He held it on Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, because it was the best porch in the Hollow, and he had asked Buck most politely, three times, with three bows. He had invited everybody. He had borrowed cups from Maggie Mae and cakes from Reba's mother and a tablecloth from his own, and he had laid the long table on the porch with the cups in a perfectly straight row, and a napkin folded beside each one into the shape of a swan. Or nearly a swan. Most of them looked more like a duck that had sat down suddenly.

At four o'clock exactly, everybody arrived.

Beau stood at the top of the porch steps, in his hat, and bowed to each one of them as they came up.

"Welcome, welcome, most delighted. Pray be seated. After you. No, after you. Do take a swan."

It was the stiffest, most uncomfortable tea party that anybody in the Hollow had ever been to. Maggie Mae came, and sat at the end, watching her cups. Sadie Belle came, and sat beside her, holding her napkin swan very carefully, and was the only one who said it was a lovely tea party, which made Beau stand up and bow to her twice, and knock over the lemonade.

Beau would not let anybody pour their own tea. He poured it for them, one at a time, standing very straight, with his little finger sticking out, and his little finger sticking out made his paw wobble, so that the cold mint tea went into the saucers, and on the tablecloth, and once, a little bit, on Great-Grandpappy Buck.

"So very sorry," said Beau, and bowed, and his hat fell into the sugar.

He would not let anybody talk with their mouth full, so every time Barney took a bite of cake, Beau said, "Ahem," and Barney had to stop and chew, and then had forgotten what he was going to say. He kept standing up every time anybody moved, so that he was hardly ever sitting down at all, and every time he stood up, he bowed, and the table wobbled. Brody had been given a very small chair, because it was the politest one, and he was sitting on it with his knees up round his ears, holding a teacup between two claws, not daring to move in case something broke.

Nobody laughed. Nobody told stories. Nobody even talked very much, because whenever anybody started, Beau leapt up and offered them more tea.

At last Rusty put down his cup.

"Beau," he said. "Can I say something? Not politely?"

Beau looked at him.

"You're no fun any more," said Rusty. "None of this is any fun. You're so busy bowing at us, you haven't looked at any of us properly all day." He looked round the table, at Brody with his knees round his ears, and Barney trying to chew quietly, and Charlie with tea in her lap. "It's like having tea with a stranger in a hat."

Charlie put her cup down, carefully, in its saucer.

Beau looked round the table. At all his friends, sitting stiffly, holding their cups, not looking at him. At Great-Grandpappy Buck, with tea on his dungarees. At the swans, which were mostly ducks.

He took off his hat. He put it down on the porch rail, very slowly.

Then he turned round and walked down the porch steps, and away along the creek path, without a single bow.


Chapter Six — The Lane

Beau walked a long way along the creek path, kicking stones.

He did not know where he was going. He just walked. Past the Old Mill, where the door was shut now, and the flies had gone. Past the bridge. Past Rose Arch Cottage. Out along the lane towards the far end of the creek path, where it was quieter, and nobody was about.

He felt more miserable than he could remember. Even worse than the Winter Games, when he had landed on his nose in front of everybody. At least then, everybody had laughed with him. Today, nobody had laughed with him at all.

He had tried so hard. He had done everything Maggie Mae had taught him. He had been polite to everybody, all day, without stopping. And it had all gone wrong. The bridge, and the flies, and the littlest rabbit crying, and Rusty saying he was a stranger in a hat.

He came round a bend in the lane, and stopped.

Ahead of him, sitting at the side of the path in the dust, was Granny Opal.

She was sitting very still, with her long grey face turned down, and her knitted shawl round her shoulders, and all round her on the path there was flour. A great white spill of it, across the stones, and a torn paper sack lying on its side, and a few late-afternoon sparrows already hopping in to peck at it.

Granny Opal was looking at the flour, and not moving.

Beau forgot all about bowing.

He forgot about hats, and napkins, and after you, and delighted I'm sure. He just ran.

"Granny Opal!" he said, skidding to a stop beside her in the dust. "Are you all right? Did you fall?"

Granny Opal looked up at him, startled, with her bright shy black eyes. Her paws were trembling.

"No, dear, no," she said. "I didn't fall. The sack split. On my way home from the Mill. I ran short this week, so I went for a little extra on my own, for once, and look. It just went, all at once, and everything came out." She looked at the flour on the path. "That was my flour for the whole week."

"Oh, no," said Beau.

"And I heard somebody coming up the lane behind me," said Granny Opal, very quietly, "and I didn't want them to see me in a muddle. So I just sat down." She shook her head. "Silly old thing. Sitting in the road."

Beau looked at the flour, and the torn sack, and Granny Opal sitting in the dust in her shawl.

Then he sat down in the dust beside her.


Chapter Seven — Flour

"Right," said Beau, in his ordinary voice. "What we need is a bag."

He did not have a bag. He looked about. Then he took off his golden neckerchief, the one he wore every day of his life, and spread it out flat on the path, and began to scoop the flour into it with both paws.

Most of the flour was clean. It had fallen on the flat dry stones of the lane, in a soft white heap. Beau scooped up the top of the heap, carefully, leaving the dusty bits at the bottom, and pushed the sparrows away gently with his tail, and talked the whole time.

"Do you know what happened to me at the footbridge this morning?" he said. "I said after you to Rusty, and he said after you to me, and we stood there for so long that Brody came, and the rabbits, and the miller with a wheelbarrow, and Benny and Barney, and Maverick, and nobody could get across at all until Charlie went underneath."

Granny Opal looked at him.

"Underneath?" she said.

"Along the beam," said Beau. "Like a squirrel. And then my hat fell in the stream." He scooped up more flour. "It was my dad's hat. Barney fished it out with a stick."

Granny Opal's mouth twitched.

"And then I held the Mill door open," said Beau, "for absolutely nobody, for ages, and all the flies in the Hollow went in, and the miller came out and asked me to shut it, and I said after you."

Granny Opal made a small creaky sound, which was the beginning of a laugh.

"And then," said Beau, warming up, "I poured tea on Grandpappy Buck."

Granny Opal laughed. Her small, creaky, rusty laugh, that she did not use very often. She laughed so much that she had to wipe her eyes with the corner of her shawl, and her paws stopped trembling, and she leaned forward and began to help him scoop the flour.

By the time they had finished, Beau's golden neckerchief was full of flour, tied up at the corners into a fat white bundle. It was not quite a whole week's worth. But it was nearly.

"Your lovely neckerchief," said Granny Opal. "It'll be all flour."

"It'll wash," said Beau. He picked up the bundle, carefully, so as not to spill any. "I'll carry it home for you. If you don't mind."

Granny Opal looked at him for a moment, with her bright black eyes.

"Thank you, Beau," she said.

So they walked to the end of the creek path together, slowly, Granny Opal's paw on his arm, Beau carrying the bundle of flour, and talking all the way, about bridges and hats and flies, and the littlest rabbit, and how he had made her cry, which he still felt very bad about. And Granny Opal told him, in return, about the time she had tried to be very grand at a wedding in the valley where she grew up, and had curtseyed so low that she could not get up again, and had to be lifted by two badgers. And Beau laughed so much he nearly dropped the flour.

At her hollow under the elderberry, he set the bundle down carefully on her table.

"I'll bring the neckerchief back tomorrow," said Granny Opal. "Washed."

"There's no hurry," said Beau.

And he went home, along the creek path, in the evening light, without his neckerchief, with flour on his paws and on his nose and on his ears, and did not bow to a single thing.

He did not know that Sadie Belle and her mother, coming home along the lane, had come round the bend behind them just as Beau sat down in the dust. Sadie Belle's mother had seen Granny Opal's face, and put a gentle hoof on Sadie Belle's shoulder, and the two of them had stepped back out of sight behind the elderberries, so that Granny Opal would not have any more faces to be in a muddle in front of. And they had waited there, quietly, until it was all done.


Chapter Eight — What the Hollow Saw

By the next morning, everybody in Hickory Hollow had heard.

Sadie Belle had told the miller's wife, very quietly, at the Mill, first thing. The miller's wife had told the miller. The miller had told the rabbit mother. And the rabbit mother, who had been very cross with Beau for making the littlest rabbit cry, had stopped being cross with him at once, and told everybody else.

"Sat right down in the dust next to her," the miller's wife was saying, when Beau came into the Mill that morning for his family's flour, "and took off his own neckerchief, the one he never takes off, and scooped it all up for her with his bare paws. And made her laugh till she cried. And walked her all the way home."

Everybody in the Mill turned round and looked at Beau.

Beau stopped in the doorway. He had no hat. He had no neckerchief. His fur was not brushed. He still had a little flour behind one ear, because he had not noticed it. He did not bow.

"Morning," he said, a little uncertainly.

The miller's wife looked at him from her big chair by the door, and her whole face crinkled up.

"Now there," she said, "is a true gentleman."

Beau stared at her.

"But I didn't do anything," he said. "Not properly. I didn't bow, or say delighted, or after you. I didn't even have my hat. I just..." He stopped. "I just helped."

Beau went out of the Mill again, very puzzled, with his flour.

On Fern Meadow, the littlest rabbit was playing with her ball. When she saw him coming, she stopped, and looked at him carefully, as you look at something you are not sure about.

Beau did not bow. He did not say good morrow. He just crouched down in the grass, low, with his ears forward and his tail twitching, and wiggled his bottom, as he always did when he was about to pounce on her ball.

The littlest rabbit shrieked with joy, and grabbed her ball, and ran, and Beau chased her all the way across Fern Meadow and let her win, and she shouted "The old Beau's back!" so loudly that the swifts flew up off the Mill roof.

Then Beau went to find Rusty.

Rusty was sitting on the footbridge, the four-step footbridge, swinging his legs over the stream.

Beau sat down next to him.

"Sorry," he said. "About the tea party. And the bridge. And being a stranger in a hat."

Rusty looked at him sideways.

"You've got flour on your ear," he said.

"I know," said Beau.

"It suits you," said Rusty.

And they sat on the bridge together, in the morning sun, swinging their legs, and when Barney came along and wanted to cross, they both shuffled over to the side to let him past, at exactly the same moment, and then looked at each other, and laughed.


Chapter Nine — The Porch

That evening, Beau went back to Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch.

He went to say sorry about the tea on Buck's dungarees. But when he came up the steps, he found that everybody was already there, just as they always were on a fine evening, sitting about on the boards and the steps and the rail, talking and laughing and eating Reba's mother's shortbread. Nobody was sitting up straight. Nobody had a napkin. Brody was lying flat on his back on the boards with his feet up the wall. Barney was talking with his mouth full.

Beau stopped at the top of the steps.

"Beau!" said Charlie. And Sadie Belle, without a word, moved up to make room for him on the bench, and went a little pink, and Beau sat down beside her and did not notice either thing.

Beau sat down. Nobody stood up. Nobody bowed. Somebody passed him the shortbread, and he took a piece, and said "Thanks," with his mouth full, and nobody said "Ahem."

Great-Grandpappy Buck looked at him from his rocking chair.

"I'm sorry about the tea, Grandpappy," said Beau. "On your dungarees."

"It came out," said Buck comfortably. "Tea always does."

Then Granny Opal came slowly up the porch steps, in her knitted shawl, holding something folded in her paws. She did not usually come to the porch, because of all the faces. But she came this evening. She came up the steps, and across the boards, and held out the folded thing to Beau.

It was his golden neckerchief. Washed and pressed, and smelling faintly of elderberry.

"Thank you, dear," she said. "For yesterday."

Beau stood up. He did not bow, or say delighted. He only stood up, because she was standing, and took the neckerchief, and tied it on, a bit crooked, as he always did. Then he went and fetched the best chair on the porch, the one with the cushion, and set it down for her right by the rail where the evening breeze came in, without saying a word about it.

Granny Opal sat down in it, slowly, and looked round at all the faces, and did not seem to mind them at all.

Great-Grandpappy Buck watched the whole thing from his rocking chair, with his paws folded and his eyes crinkling behind his spectacles.

Then he said, in his slow, warm voice, "Be the same creature on the trail as you are on the porch."


Chapter Ten — The Hat

The sun went down behind Weather Ridge, and the evening came on soft and warm, a real June evening, with the smell of honeysuckle drifting up from the creek.

Nobody wanted to go home. So nobody did, for a while.

Maggie Mae came late, from Rose Arch Cottage, and sat down on the step below Beau, and looked up at him with one eyebrow raised.

"I heard about your tea party," she said.

Beau groaned and put his paws over his face.

"My cups survived," said Maggie Mae. "Nearly all of them." And she leaned her head back against his knee, and looked out at the dusk.

The old hat was still on the porch rail, where Beau had left it at the tea party, still with a little sugar in its brim. As the light went, Barney picked it up, and put it on, and it came right down over his eyes, and he walked into the rocking chair, and said, "After you," to it, very politely, and the whole porch laughed, Beau loudest of all.

Then the littlest rabbit took the hat from Barney, and put it on, and it came right down to her shoulders, so that she was only a hat with two feet underneath, walking about the porch bumping into everybody, very pleased with herself.

Then Brody tried it on, and it sat on top of his great head like an acorn cup on a pumpkin.

Then Maverick, who was just leaving to go to his roost before dark, tried it on, and it slid straight off his smooth white head, and he said, "Eagles are not built for hats," with great dignity, and flew home.

And so the hat went round the whole porch, from head to head, in the soft dusk, and everybody looked ridiculous in it, and nobody minded at all.

When at last it came round to Granny Opal, she looked at it for a moment, sitting in her best chair by the rail. Then she put it on. It came down over her long grey face as far as her pink nose, so that only her whiskers stuck out underneath. And from under the brim, in front of all the faces on the porch, came her small, creaky, rusty laugh, out loud, for everybody to hear.

Beau laughed so hard he had to hold on to the rail.

Then, slowly, the porch grew quiet. The honeysuckle smell came up from the creek. The lamp by the door was lit, and somebody's mother came along the path with a lantern, and then somebody else's, and the youngsters began to gather up their things. Beau took his father's hat back, carefully, to carry home. His golden neckerchief still smelled faintly of elderberry, from Granny Opal's washing, and he sniffed it, and smiled.

Great-Grandpappy Buck rocked slowly in his chair, and watched them all go, two by two, down the porch steps and along the creek path into the warm June dark.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
