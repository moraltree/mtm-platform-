Chapter One — The Mending Branch

Beside Grandpappy's Bridge, in the deep of winter, there is a small bare dogwood tree that the youngsters of Hickory Hollow call the Mitten Tree.

When the hard cold comes, the folk of the Hollow bring the mittens they have grown out of, or have spare, and hang them on its low branches in pairs. Anybody who needs a pair may come and take one. No asking. No thanking. That is the first part of the custom.

The second part is the Mending Branch.

It is the highest branch on the little tree, and it is for the mittens with holes in. Worn thumbs, thin palms, unravelling cuffs. Anybody who is handy with a needle may take a pair from the Mending Branch, and mend it at home by the fire, and bring it back whole, and hang it on the low branches for somebody who needs it. That way, the old saying goes, no mitten in the Hollow is ever wasted.

This winter, as every winter, Maggie Mae had hung the mittens.

She was a young skunk, glossy black and white, with emerald-green eyes, a pink polka-dot bow and a woolly yellow scarf wound twice round her neck, and she could not look at a heap of odd mittens without wanting to sort them. She had a notebook, with a list in it. She always had a list.

"Fourteen whole pairs on the low branches," she said, ticking. "Small ones at the bottom, where the little ones can reach. And ten pairs with holes on the Mending Branch."

"These ones are terrible," said Reba, holding up a pair of striped mittens with both thumbs worn right through. "You could put your whole paw through the end."

Reba was a young raccoon, soft grey-brown, with a black mask, a ringed tail, a pink bow and a red-and-pink flowered neckerchief. She was the most curious creature in the Hollow, and she had come to help mostly because she wanted to look at every single mitten. She sniffed the striped pair.

"Somebody's oven," she announced. "Cinnamon. And these ones smell of creek water and somebody's soup."

"Brody's, and the miller's," said Maggie Mae, consulting her list. She took the striped pair gently and hung it up on the Mending Branch with the others. "Somebody will mend them. Somebody always does."

They stood back and looked. The little tree glowed with colour in the cold blue morning, reds and blues and stripes and spots, swaying gently in the breeze off the creek.

Then they went home.

The next morning, five whole pairs had gone from the low branches.

"Good," said Maggie Mae, ticking. "That's what they're for."

And every single pair had gone from the Mending Branch.

"Ten," said Maggie Mae slowly. "All ten. In one night."

"Somebody's going to be very busy mending," said Reba.

Maggie Mae wrote it in her notebook. She did not think anything more of it.

Not that day.


Chapter Two — Nothing Comes Back

By the third morning, Maggie Mae was thinking a great deal of it.

Because nothing had come back.

Usually, by the third day, the first mended pairs began to appear on the low branches: a darned thumb here, a patched palm there, neat little stitches in a different-coloured wool. That was how you knew the Mending Branch was working.

This year, not one mended mitten had come back. And every time Maggie Mae hung new holey pairs on the Mending Branch, they vanished the very next night.

Reba's mother had sent three pairs with worn cuffs. Gone by morning. The miller's wife had sent two pairs with holes in the palms. Gone by morning.

"That's fifteen pairs from the Mending Branch," said Maggie Mae, standing under the tree with her pencil, "and not one of them back."

She was not the only one who had noticed.

"Somebody's keeping them," said a voice at the bridge. It was one of the families from along the creek, stopping on their way to the mill. "Taking the holey ones and not bringing them back. Keeping them for themselves."

"Fifteen pairs," said somebody else. "Who needs fifteen pairs of mittens with holes in?"

"Somebody greedy," said the first voice. "That's who."

Maggie Mae's whiskers prickled. She did not like that word.

"Maybe they're just slow at mending," she said.

"Nobody's that slow," said the voice, and went on to the mill.

Benny and Barney, who had been sliding on the frosty boards of the bridge, came over at once.

"A mitten-keeper," said Barney, delighted. "We should catch them. We could build a trap. A big box, propped up on a stick, with a mitten underneath for bait."

"And a string," said Benny, already measuring the bridge rail with his ruler, "running from the stick to behind that bush, where we'd hide. I'd need about forty feet of string. And a box. And possibly some biscuits, for the waiting."

"Nobody is catching anybody in a box," said Maggie Mae firmly. "Whoever it is might just be a neighbour who hasn't finished. You don't put a neighbour in a box."

Barney looked disappointed. "Not even a small box?"

"Not even a small box," said Maggie Mae.

So Benny wrote down forty feet of string in his own notebook anyway, in case it ever came in useful for something else, and the twins went back to sliding on the bridge.

That afternoon, Reba came running across the bridge with her eyes very wide behind her mask.

"Maggie Mae!" she said. "It's a mystery! A real one! Somebody's taking the mending mittens, and nobody knows who! Can we find out? Can I be the detective?"

Maggie Mae looked at the bare Mending Branch. Then she looked at her notebook.

"We'll both be detectives," she said. "You do the sniffing. I'll do the list."

Reba was so pleased that she sniffed the entire tree at once, from top to bottom, with her nose so close to the bark that she went cross-eyed and walked into the bridge rail.

"Ow," she said. "Wool. Snow. And something sweet. Like fruit gone soft. Like the old persimmons by the creek."

"Persimmons," said Maggie Mae, writing it down.

"And this," said Reba, holding up a tiny grey thread caught on a twig. "It's not wool. It's fur. Grey fur. Very soft."

Maggie Mae looked at the thread for a long moment. Then she put it carefully between two pages of her notebook, as if it were a pressed flower.

"There's something else," she said, standing on her tiptoes to look at the Mending Branch. "Up there. In the fork."

Tucked into the place where the Mending Branch met the trunk was a little twist of dried twigs, tied with a thread of grass. Maggie Mae reached up and unhooked it. It was a sprig of elderberry, the kind that grows in thickets along the creek, with a few shrivelled berries still clinging to it.

"Somebody put that there," said Reba. "Elderberry doesn't grow on a dogwood."

"There were two more yesterday," said Maggie Mae slowly. "I thought they'd blown in off the creek. I threw them away." She looked at the little sprig in her paw. "I don't think they blew in."

She tucked it back in the fork exactly where she had found it, and wrote it down.


Chapter Three — Seen at Dusk

The next day, Beau came bounding round the corner of the creek path, with Rusty trotting behind him.

"Are you finding the mitten-keeper?" called Beau. "Everybody's talking about it. I know something!"

"What?" said Maggie Mae, pencil ready.

"I saw somebody at the Mitten Tree. Last night. At dusk. Carrying an armful of mittens!" Beau skidded to a halt in the snow, and then his face changed as he looked at Reba. "Oh. Actually. It was you, Reba. I just remembered."

"It was me," said Reba slowly, "bringing my mother's three pairs. To put on the Mending Branch."

"Oh, yes," said Beau. "That makes sense."

But Rusty was looking at the snow, not at Reba, and his ears had gone flat.

"Somebody at the mill said something this morning," he said uncomfortably. "That Reba's always poking about in other folks' cupboards. And that maybe she'd been collecting. I didn't say it. I just heard it. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have repeated it."

Reba went very still.

It was true that she poked about in cupboards. She could not help it. She was curious. But she had never in her life taken anything that was not hers, and the idea that somebody at the mill was saying she had, about the Mending Branch, made her feel hot and cold all over.

"I put mittens on," she said. Her voice wobbled. "I didn't take them."

"Everybody who knows you knows that," said Maggie Mae at once, and put her paw on Reba's shoulder.

"Not everybody knows me," said Reba quietly.

Maggie Mae looked at her for a moment. Then she closed her notebook with a snap.

"Then they'd better," she said. "Come on."

She walked straight across the bridge and down the path to the Old Mill, with Reba hurrying behind her, and went in at the big door. The miller was there, and two families from along the creek, warming their paws by the stove.

"Good morning," said Maggie Mae politely. "I believe somebody here has been saying that Reba took the mittens from the Mending Branch."

The room went quiet.

"Reba hung three pairs on that branch," said Maggie Mae. "From her mother. I saw her do it. I wrote it in my list." She held up the notebook. "She didn't take a single one. And I'd be grateful if anybody who heard otherwise would hear this instead."

The miller looked at Reba. Then he took off his floury cap.

"I'm sorry, young Reba," he said. "That was me, I'm ashamed to say. I said it without thinking. I'll put it right."

Reba nodded. She could not quite speak. But walking back up the path afterwards, beside Maggie Mae, she felt as if a heavy, wet coat had been lifted off her shoulders.

"Thank you," she said.

"Friends don't let things like that sit," said Maggie Mae.

"Now we have to find who it really is," said Reba. "Properly."


Chapter Four — Little Hands

That evening, Charlie came running onto the bridge with news of her own.

"Maggie Mae! Reba! The mitten fairy came again!"

"The what?" said Reba.

"The mitten fairy," said Charlie, bouncing. "That's what the rabbit family calls it. Every morning this week, there's been new mittens on somebody's doorstep. Not holey ones. Brand-new ones. Knitted. The rabbit mother found eight tiny pairs on her step on Tuesday, one for each of the little ones! And Brody found a great big pair on his yesterday, exactly his size! And nobody knows who's leaving them!"

Maggie Mae's pencil stopped moving.

She looked at the bare Mending Branch. She looked at the thread of grey fur pressed in her notebook. She looked at Charlie.

"Charlie," she said slowly. "Did anybody look at the snow round the rabbits' doorstep? For footprints?"

Charlie shook her head. Then her eyes went very round.

"I can look!" she said. "I'm good at small things! I'm the best at small things!"

So the three of them went, before the light was gone, to the rabbit family's burrow at the bottom of Fern Meadow, and Charlie crept along the edge of the doorstep with her nose almost touching the snow.

"Here!" she whispered. "Tiny ones. Look!"

In the old snow, half filled in by the wind, there was a line of small prints. They were strange prints. They did not look like a fox's, or a rabbit's, or a raccoon's. Each one looked like a tiny hand, with five spread fingers. And on the back feet, one finger stuck out sideways, all on its own, like a thumb.

Maggie Mae drew one very carefully in her notebook.

Charlie put her own paw down beside the clearest print, very lightly, so as not to spoil it.

"It's bigger than my paw," she reported, "and smaller than Reba's. And look, here, it's put its back foot almost exactly where its front foot went. And here. And here. All the way along." She crept along the line, counting under her breath. "It walks slowly. The prints are close together. It isn't running away from anywhere. It's just walking home."

"How do you know it's walking home?" said Reba.

Charlie pointed. Beside the prints, all the way along, ran a thin wavering line in the snow, as if something long and thin had been dragging behind.

"Because it's carrying something," said Charlie. "And you only carry things that slowly when you're taking them home."

"I've never seen a print like that," said Reba.

But Rusty, who had followed them down to see, sat back on his haunches and whistled softly.

"I have," he said. "Not often. But I have. Little hands, and a thumb on the back foot that sticks out sideways." He looked at the prints, and then along them, where they went off across the meadow towards the creek, and then along the creek towards the far, wild end of the path where nobody much went. "That's an opossum."

"An opossum?" said Charlie. "I've never even seen an opossum."

"Nobody has, much," said Rusty. "They only come out at night."

Maggie Mae opened her notebook to the page with her map of the Hollow. She drew the line of little prints on it, from the rabbits' door to the creek, and along the creek path, past the last cabin, all the way to the far end.

"Somebody," she said quietly, "lives at the end of the creek path."


Chapter Five — The Watch from the Porch

Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge stands right beside Grandpappy's Bridge, and from his porch you can see the Mitten Tree as clearly as if you were standing under it. So the next evening, Reba and Maggie Mae asked if they might sit on his porch at dusk and watch.

"Watch for what?" said Buck, from his rocking chair.

"For whoever takes the mending mittens," said Reba.

Buck looked at the Mitten Tree over his spectacles.

"You may sit and watch as long as you like," he said. "And I'll sit and watch with you."

So the three of them sat on the porch as the light went, wrapped in blankets, with mugs of hot cordial. Maggie Mae had hung four fresh holey pairs on the Mending Branch that afternoon, and counted them twice. Reba sat at the very edge of the porch with her nose pointed at the bridge, so still that a chickadee landed on her ear and she did not notice.

The sky went gold, and pink, and a soft winter blue.

And then, along the creek path from the far end, came a shape.

It was low and grey and slow, a little bigger than Reba, with a long pale pointed face, small round black ears, and a long, bare, pinkish tail. It came right up to the Mitten Tree, and looked round once, carefully, with bright black eyes. Then it climbed up the little tree, slowly, holding on with its small pink hands, and with its tail curled round a branch behind it to keep steady.

It reached the Mending Branch. It took down the holey pairs, one by one: all four. And then it did something astonishing. It wrapped the end of its long tail round the bundle of mittens, curled it up tight like a rope round a parcel, and climbed back down with the mittens held in its tail.

Then it went away along the creek path in the dusk, slow and grey, with four pairs of holey mittens bobbing along behind it.

Reba's mouth had fallen open.

"An opossum," she whispered. "Carrying them in its tail."

"Only the Mending Branch ones," said Maggie Mae slowly. "Not one whole pair. Did you see? It didn't touch the low branches at all."

But Reba's face had gone set and certain.

"It's taken every one," she said. "Every single pair from the Mending Branch, every night, all week. And never brought one back. That's what everybody's been saying. Somebody greedy." Her mask crinkled. "It's the opossum. Keeping them all for itself, at the end of the creek path, where nobody goes."

Behind them, Great-Grandpappy Buck rocked slowly and said nothing at all.


Chapter Six — Halfway Through a Sentence

The next morning, Reba was at the Old Mill before breakfast.

She had not meant to go there to tell anybody. She had gone to fetch flour for her mother. But the miller was there, and two families from along the creek were warming their paws by the stove again, and they were talking about the Mending Branch, and the miller looked up and said kindly, "Any news, young Reba? On the mitten mystery?"

And Reba opened her mouth and said, "Yes! It's an opossum! At the end of the creek path! It comes at dusk and takes all the mending mittens in its tail and keeps them all for itself, and it never..."

She stopped.

Everybody was looking at her. The two families by the stove had turned round. One of them was already nodding, and turning to the other, and beginning to say something.

Reba knew exactly what they were going to say. She had heard it before. About herself. Two days ago. In this same room.

Somebody greedy.

She thought about how it had felt, standing in the snow by the bridge, hearing that somebody at the mill had decided she was a mitten-thief, without asking her, without knowing her, without ever having spoken to her at all.

And she thought about the opossum, alone at the far end of the creek path, who did not know yet that anybody was talking about it.

"It never," said Reba slowly, "brings them back. That's all I know. That's all I actually saw." Her ears had gone hot. "I don't know why. I didn't ask. I shouldn't have said keeping them. I don't know that. I'm sorry. Please don't tell anybody I said it."

The miller looked at her for a long moment over the top of his floury spectacles.

Then he nodded, slowly, and turned to the two families by the stove.

"You heard the young lady," he said. "She doesn't know why. And neither do any of us. So let's none of us go saying we do."

The families looked at each other, a little sheepishly, and turned back to the stove.

Reba walked out of the mill with her mother's flour and stood on the step in the cold, with her heart thumping.

Maggie Mae was coming down the path.

"Reba," she said. "What's the matter? You look as if you've seen a ghost."

"I nearly did the same thing," said Reba, in a very small voice. "To the opossum. What they did to me. I was halfway through saying it. And then I remembered."

Maggie Mae looked at her for a moment. Then she took the flour basket from her, and held it, and put her other paw through Reba's arm.

"But you stopped," she said.

"Only just," said Reba.

"Just is enough," said Maggie Mae. "Now. I think it's time somebody went and asked."


Chapter Seven — The Door at the End of the Path

It was Sadie Belle who said they should not go empty-pawed.

"If somebody came to my door to ask me something hard," she said, "I'd want them to come as a neighbour first. Not as a detective."

So Reba made a pan of cornbread, golden and warm. Maggie Mae picked a little pot of dried flowers from her windowsill, because she always took flowers to anybody new. And Great-Grandpappy Buck, when they told him where they were going, picked up his walking-stick and his lantern and said he had been meaning to go and call on the new neighbour at the end of the path himself, for weeks, and had kept putting it off, and that it was high time.

"New neighbour?" said Reba.

"She came at the end of the autumn," said Buck. "Over the ridge from somewhere further south. Took the empty hollow under the old elderberry thicket at the very end of the path. Keeps to herself." He looked a little ashamed. "I meant to go and say good evening. I never did."

It was a long way, past the last cabin, where the path grew wild and the snow lay deep and untrodden. At the very end, under a great old tangle of elderberry heaped with snow, there was a hollow at the foot of a hickory, with an old wooden board leaned across the opening for a door, and a thin thread of smoke coming out from somewhere among the roots.

Maverick came gliding down out of the white sky as they passed the last cabin, and landed on a fence post with a thump that knocked a shower of snow onto Reba's head.

"Morning, young ones! Morning, Buck! Off to see the opossum? Rusty told me." He shook out his great brown wings. "Now. Something you ought to know about opossums. Very nervous folk. If you give one a real fright, do you know what it does? Falls over. Flat on its side, stiff as a stick, eyes shut, tongue out, as if it's fainted dead away. Can't help it. Just happens to them. Then, when the fright's gone, up they get again, right as rain."

"Truly?" said Charlie, horrified and delighted.

"Truly," said Maverick. "So knock gently. And don't all shout at once."

"We weren't going to shout," said Reba.

"Benny and Barney might," said Maverick, which was fair, though Benny and Barney were not there. He lifted off the post, circled once over their heads, and went back up into the snowy sky.

They walked on more quietly after that, following Maggie Mae's map, and Rusty's little-hand prints, and there it was.

Reba knocked, very softly, on the board.

For a long while, nothing happened.

Then the board moved aside, just a crack, and a long pale grey face looked out. It was an old opossum, with bright, shy black eyes and small round ears and a pink nose, and she looked at them all standing in the snow, and her eyes went very wide.

Then she opened her mouth and hissed, a long nervous hiss, and showed a great many small white teeth.

Everybody took a step back.

Then, very slowly, the opossum shut her mouth again, and looked rather embarrassed.

"I'm sorry," she said, in a small creaky voice. "I always do that when I'm surprised. I don't mean anything by it. Folk always step back." She looked at the ground. "That's why I don't go out much."

"Good evening," said Great-Grandpappy Buck gently, and took off his cap. "We're your neighbours. We've come to say welcome. I'm sorry it's taken us so long."

Reba held out the warm cornbread.

The old opossum looked at it. Then at them. Then, slowly, she moved the board all the way aside.

"You'd better come in," she said. "It's cold. My name's Opal."


Chapter Eight — Granny Opal's Hollow

Granny Opal's hollow was small and round and warm, with roots for a ceiling, a little fire in a ring of stones, and it was full of wool.

There were baskets of it everywhere, sorted into colours. Reds and blues and yellows and greens. And in one big basket by the fire were the mittens from the Mending Branch, all nineteen pairs of them, the striped ones with holes in the thumbs and all the rest.

Reba's heart sank.

But then she looked closer.

The mittens in the basket were not being kept. They were being taken apart. Somebody had found the end of the wool on each one and was patiently unravelling it, row by row, into long crinkly strands, and knotting the short pieces together, and winding them into balls.

And on a shelf above the fire, in a neat row, sat new mittens. Thick, soft, whole, warm ones, in bright stripes and checks, every one a different size.

Granny Opal sat down slowly in a low chair, and picked up two long wooden knitting needles and a half-finished mitten, and began to knit. Click, click, click.

"The ones on that high branch were too far gone to mend," she said, not looking up. "Holes as big as a paw. You can't darn a hole that size. So I take them home and unpick them, and knit the wool up again into new ones that'll keep a paw properly warm." Click, click. "Then I leave them on the doorsteps of them that need them. At night. When nobody's about. I saw the little rabbits' paws were cold, going past to the creek. And the big bear cub's paws were poking out the ends of his."

"And the little elderberry sprigs on the branch?" said Maggie Mae. "Were those yours?"

Granny Opal nodded, and her needles slowed a little.

"Where I came from," she said, "you tuck a sprig of elderberry in the fork when you take from the mending branch. So folk know the mittens have gone for mending, not gone for good. Everybody back home knew it." She looked at the fire. "I suppose nobody here does."

"I threw two of them away," said Maggie Mae, very quietly. "I thought they'd blown in."

"You couldn't have known," said Granny Opal.

"You've been doing it every night?" said Maggie Mae.

"These last few nights," said Granny Opal. "Since the tree went up. It's the first winter I've been here. Where I came from, we had a mending tree too. That's what we did with the worn-out ones. I thought it would be the same here." Her needles slowed. "I should have said. I should have hung a little note on the branch, telling where they'd gone. I meant to. But I didn't know anybody. And folk step back from me."

There was a quiet moment, with only the click of the needles and the crackle of the fire.

Then Reba put the cornbread down on the table.

"Granny Opal," she said. "There's something I have to tell you. Folk at the mill were saying somebody greedy was keeping the mending mittens. And when I saw you take them last night, I thought it was you. And this morning, I very nearly said so to everybody. I said some of it." She took a breath. "And it's especially bad, because two days ago somebody said the same thing about me, and it was horrible. So I knew. And I nearly did it anyway. I'm sorry."

Granny Opal's needles stopped.

She looked at Reba for a long while, with her bright, shy black eyes.

"But you came and asked," she said at last. "With cornbread."

"Yes," said Reba.

"Then I don't think there's any harm done," said Granny Opal. "Not that won't mend." And very slowly, for the first time, she smiled. It showed a great many small white teeth. But it was not frightening at all.


Chapter Nine — The Mending Evening

It was Maggie Mae's idea, and it was the very best kind of Maggie Mae idea.

"A Mending Evening," she said, on the walk home along the creek path, with Buck's lantern swinging ahead of them. "At Rose Arch Cottage. Tomorrow night. I'll do the tea and the cakes and the cushions, and the good lamp, and Granny Opal can sit in my best chair and show us all how to unpick and knit. And everybody who comes can bring a worn-out mitten, and some spare wool."

"Will she come?" said Reba doubtfully. "She doesn't go out."

"She'll come," said Maggie Mae, "if somebody walks along the path with her."

So the next evening, at dusk, Reba and Maggie Mae and Maggie Mae's mother walked all the way to the end of the creek path, and knocked on the board across the hollow, and Granny Opal came out in a knitted shawl, very nervously, and walked back with them, slowly, between them, with her long tail curled round a bundle of knitting needles.

Rose Arch Cottage had never looked lovelier. Maggie Mae had lit the good lamp in the window, and set out her best cups, and baked a honey cake, and put the softest cushion on the best chair by the fire. She met Granny Opal at the door and took her shawl and settled her in the chair and put a cup of hot cordial in her paws before she could even say good evening, and Granny Opal looked round the warm, pretty little room with her bright black eyes and did not hiss once.

The friends came, one by one, each with a worn mitten and some spare wool.

Charlie wound wool. She was very good at it, and very fast, and got tangled only twice. Sadie Belle asked to learn to knit, and Granny Opal showed her, needle by needle, and her first row went uphill like a mountain path, and nobody minded at all. Brody came wearing his new mittens, exactly his size for the first time in his life, and said thank you so many times that Granny Opal had to ask him to stop. Benny measured everybody's paws for future mittens with his ruler. Barney tried to knit and somehow knitted his own tail into it, and Granny Opal had to unpick him, row by row, while he stood very still and tried not to laugh.

And Reba sat at Granny Opal's feet and learned to unpick.

Her clever, curious paws, which had poked into every cupboard in the Hollow, turned out to be perfect for it. She could find the hidden end of any mitten and draw it out, and unravel it row by row without a single tangle.

"You've a gift for it," said Granny Opal.

"I like finding out what's inside things," said Reba. "Even mittens."

Granny Opal laughed, a small creaky laugh, as if it had not been used for a long time.

Great-Grandpappy Buck came late, slowly, with his walking-stick, and sat in the chair by the window with a cup of tea, and watched them all for a long time.

"I'm ashamed I never knocked on your door before, Opal," he said quietly.

"You knocked on it now," said Granny Opal. "That's what counts, where I come from."

Buck looked round the lamplit room, at the young ones bent over their wool, and the old opossum in the best chair with her needles clicking, and the row of half-finished mittens growing on Maggie Mae's table. Then he said, softly, "The creek that runs deepest makes the least noise."


Chapter Ten — A Note on the Branch

The next morning, early, before most of the Hollow was awake, Reba went down to the Mitten Tree by Grandpappy's Bridge.

The sun was just coming up over Sunrise Ridge, turning the snow pink. The creek steamed in the cold. The little dogwood stood by the bridge with its low branches full of mittens, and the Mending Branch at the top was bare.

Reba had a small piece of birch bark in her paw. On it, in her best and neatest writing, which was not very neat, but which she had done four times to get right, it said:

Mending Branch mittens go to Granny Opal, at the end of the creek path. She knits them into new ones for anybody whose paws are cold. Bring her spare wool. And if you are passing, knock and say good evening.

Granny Opal had read it first, the night before, and said that it would do very well, and that she would like that. Very much.

Reba tied the note to the Mending Branch with a piece of pink wool, and stood back to look.

Then she climbed up the little tree, carefully, and hung something else on the low branches, right at the bottom, where the smallest paws could reach. It was a pair of mittens. They were rather lumpy. One was slightly bigger than the other. The thumb of the left one pointed in a direction that thumbs do not usually point. They had taken her all of the Mending Evening, and most of the night after, by lamplight in her mother's kitchen, with her mother sitting beside her.

They were the first mittens Reba had ever made.

She looked at them hanging there for a while. Then she went home to breakfast.

That evening, as the light went blue and the stars began to show over the Hollow, a family from along the creek stopped at the Mitten Tree on their way home from the mill. They read Reba's note by the light of their lantern. They looked at each other.

Then, instead of going home, they turned and walked all the way along the creek path, past the last cabin, to the elderberry thicket at the very end, and knocked on the board across the hollow.

"Good evening," they said, when the long grey face looked out. "We've brought you some wool."

And Granny Opal did not hiss at all.

Back at the bridge, the Mitten Tree swayed gently in the cold night air. On the very lowest branch, the lumpy mittens with the strange thumb were gone. Somebody small had needed them, and taken them home, and was wearing them right now, somewhere in the Hollow, with warm paws.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
