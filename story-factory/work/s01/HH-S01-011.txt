Chapter One — The Mitten Tree

In the deep of winter, beside Grandpappy's Bridge, there is a small bare dogwood tree that nobody in Hickory Hollow would ever dream of cutting down, because it is the Mitten Tree.

Nobody can remember who started it. It may have been the very first settlers. But every year, when the snow lies deep and the cold bites at paws and noses, the folk of the Hollow bring the mittens they have grown out of, or have spares of, or simply do not need, and hang them on the bare branches of the little dogwood by the bridge. And anybody who needs a pair, for any reason at all, may come and take one. No asking. No thanking. That is the custom.

On the first morning of the hard cold, the Mitten Tree looked like a tree that had blossomed in winter. There were red mittens and blue mittens, striped mittens and spotted ones, big brown bear-sized mittens and tiny pink ones no bigger than a thimble, all hanging from the twigs in pairs, swaying gently in the breeze off the creek.

Maggie Mae had hung most of them.

She was a young skunk, glossy black and white, with emerald-green eyes, a pink polka-dot bow and a warm woolly yellow scarf wound twice round her neck. She organised the Mitten Tree every winter, because somebody had to, and because Maggie Mae could not look at a heap of odd mittens without wanting to sort them into pairs and sizes and colours. She had a little notebook with a list in it. She always had a list.

"Small ones at the bottom, where the little ones can reach," she said, stepping back to look. "Big ones at the top. Every pair pinned together, so nobody ends up with one red and one green."

"I'd wear one red and one green," said Reba. "I think it would look jolly."

Reba was a young raccoon with soft grey-brown fur, a black mask, a ringed tail, a pink bow and a red-and-pink flowered neckerchief. She had come to help, mostly because helping Maggie Mae usually ended in something to eat, and partly because she was the most curious creature in the Hollow and wanted to see every single mitten.

She held one up to her nose and sniffed it.

"This one's been near a bakery," she said. "Cinnamon."

"That's Brody's old one," said Maggie Mae, consulting her list. "He's grown out of it."

"And this one smells of creek water and somebody's dinner."

"The miller's."

Reba held up a pair of tiny striped ones and turned them over in her clever paws.

"These have got holes in the thumbs," she said. "Both of them. You could put your whole thumb through."

Maggie Mae sighed and wrote something in her notebook.

"Lots of them have holes," she said. "Folk give the ones they don't want. And the ones they don't want are usually the ones with holes in." She smoothed a crooked mitten straight. "Still. A mitten with a hole's better than no mitten at all."

They hung the last pair, and stood back, and looked at the Mitten Tree, all colours, swaying by the bridge in the cold blue morning.

"Twenty-two pairs," said Maggie Mae, ticking her list. "That should keep everybody warm till spring."

It did not keep everybody warm until spring.

It did not even last until the next morning.


Chapter Two — Half Gone

When Maggie Mae came down to the bridge the next day with her notebook, half the mittens had gone.

Not just a few. Half. Eleven pairs. The top branches were bare. The big brown mittens had gone, and the striped ones with the holes, and most of the blue ones.

"Well," she said, after a moment. "That's what it's for. Somebody needed them."

"Eleven pairs, though," said Reba, looking up at the bare twigs. "In one night. That's a lot of needing."

By the middle of the morning, the first of the little rabbits had come down to the bridge to look for a pair, because her old ones had worn right through at the paws. She stood at the bottom of the Mitten Tree and looked up at the bare top branches for a long while.

"There aren't any my size," she said in a small voice.

Maggie Mae looked. She was right. All the little ones were gone too.

That afternoon, the rabbit mother came and looked, and then the miller's wife, and then a family from along the creek. And by evening, there was muttering by the bridge.

"Somebody's taking more than their share," said someone.

"Eleven pairs in a night. That's not needing. That's greed."

"It's not the custom. Take what you need. Not the whole tree."

Maggie Mae stood by the Mitten Tree with her notebook and felt her whiskers prickle. She did not like muttering. Muttering was untidy, like a list with crossings-out all over it.

"Maybe somebody had a big family," she said. "Maybe they needed eleven pairs."

But nobody in the Hollow had a family that big except the rabbits, and the rabbits had got none.

That night, Maggie Mae hung up six more pairs that she had found at the bottom of her own cupboard. Reba brought three that her mother had been keeping for "one day".

In the morning, all nine of them had gone too.

"Right," said Maggie Mae, taking out her pencil. "That is quite enough. We are going to find out where these mittens are going."

Reba's eyes went bright behind her mask. There was nothing in the world she loved quite so much as finding out.

"A mystery," she said. "A real one. Oh, Maggie Mae, can I be the detective?"

"We'll both be detectives," said Maggie Mae. "You do the sniffing. I'll do the list."


Chapter Three — Seen at Dusk

The detecting began very well.

Reba sniffed every branch of the Mitten Tree, slowly, one by one, with her nose so close to the bark that she went cross-eyed. "Wool," she announced. "And snow. And something else. Something like... leaves. Old leaves. And a bit of pine."

Maggie Mae wrote that down. Then she drew a map of the bridge and the tree and the paths leading away from it, and marked where the snow was trodden, and where it was not.

"The paths are no good," said Reba. "Everybody comes this way. It's the bridge."

Then, round the corner of the creek path, came Beau the bobcat, bounding through the snow in great leaps, with Rusty trotting behind him. Beau was tawny and spotted, with black ear tufts and bright blue eyes, and a golden-yellow neckerchief, and he was very nearly always in a hurry.

"Are you looking for the mitten taker?" he called. "Everybody's talking about it. I know something!"

"What?" said Maggie Mae, pencil ready.

"I saw somebody at the Mitten Tree. Last night. At dusk. Carrying an armful of mittens." Beau skidded to a stop, and then his face changed, as he looked at Reba. "Oh. Well. It was you, actually, Reba. I just remembered. It was you."

There was a pause.

"It was me," said Reba slowly, "bringing my mother's three pairs. To put on the tree."

"Oh," said Beau. "Oh, yes. That makes much more sense."

But Rusty was looking at the snow and not at Reba.

"Somebody said," he said uncomfortably, "down at the mill this morning. That you're always poking about in other folks' cupboards, Reba. And that maybe you'd been, well. Collecting." His ears went flat. "I didn't say it. I just heard it. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have repeated it."

Reba went very still.

It was true that she poked about in cupboards. She could not help it. She was curious. She loved to see what was inside things. But she had never, ever taken anything that was not hers. And the idea that somebody down at the mill was saying she had, about the Mitten Tree, about mittens for little rabbits who had none, made her feel hot and cold all at once.

"I didn't," she said. Her voice wobbled. "I wouldn't. I put mittens on. I didn't take them."

"I know you didn't," said Maggie Mae at once, very firmly, and put her paw on Reba's shoulder. "Anybody who knows you knows you didn't."

"Not everybody knows me," said Reba quietly.

Beau looked dreadful.

"I'm sorry, Reba," he said. "I didn't mean it like that. I only meant I saw you. I didn't think about what it would sound like."

Reba nodded. She did not trust her voice.

When the boys had gone, she sat down on the bridge with her chin on the rail and looked at the bare top branches of the Mitten Tree.

"We have to find who it really is," she said. "We have to. So everybody stops saying it's me."


Chapter Four — The Watch from the Porch

Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge stands right beside Grandpappy's Bridge, and from his porch you can see the Mitten Tree as clearly as if you were standing under it. So that evening, Reba and Maggie Mae asked if they might sit on his porch at dusk and watch.

"Watch for what?" said Buck, from his rocking chair.

"For whoever's taking all the mittens," said Reba.

Buck looked at the Mitten Tree for a while over his spectacles.

"Well now," he said. "You may sit and watch from my porch as long as you like. It's a fine porch for watching." He tucked Bramble's quilt round his knees. "I'll just sit here and watch with you, if you don't mind."

So the three of them sat on the porch as the light went, wrapped in blankets, with mugs of hot cordial. Maggie Mae had hung four fresh pairs on the tree that afternoon, so that there would be something for the taker to take. Reba sat at the very edge of the porch with her nose pointed at the bridge, so still that a chickadee landed on her ear and she did not even notice.

The sky turned gold, and then pink, and then a soft winter blue. The creek went dark and silver. The first lamps came on in the cabins along the bank.

Then there was a crunch of small feet on the snowy bridge.

Reba's ears shot up. Her whole body went tight, like a spring. She half rose from the porch boards, ready to pounce.

It was the littlest rabbit.

She tiptoed up to the Mitten Tree, looked over her shoulder once, and stood on her very tippiest toes, and reached up, and took down one small pair of red mittens from the lowest branch. She put them on, there and then, one paw and then the other, and held both paws up in front of her face and looked at them for a long moment, glowing. Then she hopped away home across the bridge, with her paws held out in front of her like a pair of lanterns.

Reba sank slowly back down onto the porch.

"That's what it's for," she whispered. "That's exactly what it's for."

"Just so," said Great-Grandpappy Buck, very quietly, and rocked.

And then, along the creek path from the far end, came a shape.

It was low and round and slow, and it moved in a strange rustling way, as if it were wearing a coat made of a thousand knitting needles. It came right up to the Mitten Tree, and stood up on its hind legs, and reached up into the branches with small dark paws. One pair came down. Then another. Then all four.

Then the shape turned, with the mittens held carefully in its arms, and went rustling slowly away along the creek path in the dusk, back the way it had come.

Reba's mouth had fallen open.

"That's old Mrs Quill," she whispered. "The porcupine. From the end of the creek path. Under the elder hedge."

Maggie Mae had seen her too. Everybody in the Hollow knew of Mrs Quill, though hardly anybody knew her. She lived alone at the very end of the creek path, past the last cabin, in a burrow under a tangled old elder hedge. She never came to the Harvest Home, or the music evenings, or the Longest Night. She hardly ever spoke. She was covered all over in long, sharp, pale-tipped quills, and when she walked past, folk tended to step back off the path a little, the way you would from a holly bush.

"She took all four," said Reba. Her voice had gone sharp and certain. "All of them. In one go. For herself."

"Maybe," said Maggie Mae slowly. "We only saw her take them."

"She's taken every one," said Reba. "Every night. Forty-one pairs, Maggie Mae. For one porcupine. She's got four paws, not forty." Her mask crinkled with indignation. "And she never comes to anything. She never shares anything. She doesn't even say good morning. And now we know why."

Behind them, Great-Grandpappy Buck rocked slowly in his chair and did not say anything at all.


Chapter Five — What Reba Said

Reba was down at the bridge before breakfast the next morning, and by the time Maggie Mae arrived with her notebook, Reba had told Barney, and Barney had told Benny, and Benny had told Brody, and the rabbit mother had overheard, and was telling the miller's wife.

"Old Mrs Quill," the muttering went. "Taking the lot. Every night. Fancy. Well, she always did keep to herself. You never know with the quiet ones."

"Reba," said Maggie Mae. "What have you done?"

"I told them," said Reba. Her eyes were very bright. "I told them it wasn't me. That it was Mrs Quill. Now they'll stop saying it's me."

Maggie Mae looked at her for a moment. Then she looked at the little group by the bridge, all muttering together, shaking their heads, glancing along the creek path towards the elder hedge at the far end.

"Reba," she said quietly. "Do you remember yesterday? When Beau said he saw you at the tree, with an armful of mittens?"

"Yes," said Reba. "But that was different. I was putting them on."

"And everybody at the mill decided you were taking them," said Maggie Mae. "Because you like cupboards. Because they saw you with mittens at dusk. And they didn't ask you. They just decided."

Reba opened her mouth.

"We saw Mrs Quill with mittens at dusk," said Maggie Mae. "That's all we saw. We didn't see where she took them. We didn't ask her why. We just decided. Because she's quiet, and prickly, and keeps to herself." She looked down at her notebook. "I wrote it down last night, Reba. Everything we actually know. It's two lines long."

She held out the page. It said, in her neat curly writing:

Mrs Quill took four pairs at dusk. She went back along the creek path.

That was all.

Reba looked at the two lines for a long time.

The hot, prickly feeling from yesterday came back, the feeling of everybody looking at her and deciding something about her without asking. Only this time, it was the other way round. This time, she was the one who had decided. And it was old Mrs Quill, alone at the end of the creek path, who did not even know yet that the whole Hollow was muttering about her.

"Oh," said Reba in a very small voice. "Oh, Maggie Mae. I did the same thing."

"You did," said Maggie Mae, but gently.

"I didn't mean to. I was just so cross about them saying it was me. I wanted it to be somebody else, so badly, that when we saw her, I just..." She stopped. "Everybody's saying it now. Because of me."

Down by the bridge, the muttering went on.

Reba stood up.

"Then I have to find out the truth," she said. "The real truth. All of it. Not two lines."

"How?" said Maggie Mae.

Reba looked along the creek path towards the elder hedge.

"We could ask her," she said.


Chapter Six — Visiting

It was Sadie Belle who said they should not go empty-pawed.

"If somebody came to my door to ask me something difficult," she said, "I'd want them to come as a neighbour first. Not as a detective."

So Reba made a pan of cornbread, golden and warm, and wrapped it in a cloth. Maggie Mae put her notebook away in her pocket and did not take it out again. And Great-Grandpappy Buck, when they told him where they were going, picked up his walking-stick and his lantern and said he fancied a walk to the end of the creek path himself, and that he would wait for them by the gate.

It was a long way, past the last cabin, where the path grew narrow and wild and the snow lay deep and untrodden.

Reba walked in front, holding the cornbread, and found that the further they went, the slower her feet wanted to go.

"What if she's cross?" she whispered to Maggie Mae. "What if she's heard what I said? What if she won't open the door?"

"Then we'll leave the cornbread on the step," said Maggie Mae, "and come back tomorrow, and try again."

Reba thought about that, and walked a little faster. At the very end, under a great old tangle of elder hedge heaped with snow, there was a small round wooden door, with a thin thread of smoke rising from a chimney poking up through the hedge.

Buck stopped at the little gate and leaned on his stick.

"I'll be right here," he said.

Reba walked up to the door with the warm cornbread in her arms. Her heart was going very fast. She knocked, very softly.

For a long while, nothing happened.

Then the door opened, just a crack, and an old, round, prickly face looked out. Mrs Quill was smaller than Reba had expected, close to, with small, dark, shy eyes and a soft greying nose, and her long quills lay smooth and flat along her back like the bristles of a well-brushed brush.

"Yes?" she said. Her voice was quiet and creaky, like a door that is not opened often.

"Good afternoon, Mrs Quill," said Reba. "I'm Reba. And this is Maggie Mae. We've brought you some cornbread." She held it out. "And we wanted to ask you something. About the Mitten Tree. If that's all right."

Mrs Quill looked at the cornbread. Then she looked at them. Then, past them, she saw Great-Grandpappy Buck at the gate, and he lifted his cap to her.

Her quills, which had begun to lift very slightly when the door opened, settled down flat again.

"You'd better come in," she said. "It's cold."


Chapter Seven — The Burrow Under the Elder Hedge

Mrs Quill's burrow was small and round and very warm, with a little fire in a stone hearth and a low ceiling held up by roots.

And it was full of wool.

There was wool everywhere. There were baskets of it in every corner, sorted into colours: reds and blues and yellows and greens. There were balls of it on the table and skeins of it hanging from the roots of the ceiling. And there, in a big basket by the fire, were mittens. The mittens from the Mitten Tree. Dozens of them. The striped ones with holes in the thumbs, and the big brown ones, and the blue ones, all in a heap.

Reba's heart sank right down into her toes.

But then she looked closer.

The mittens in the basket were not being kept. They were being taken apart. Somebody had found the end of the wool on each one and was patiently unravelling it, row after row, back into long crinkly strands, and winding the strands into balls.

And on a little shelf above the fire, in a neat row, sat new mittens. Not holey ones. Thick, soft, warm, whole ones, beautifully knitted, in bright stripes and checks, every one a different size. There were big ones that might fit a bear cub with paws a size too big. There were middle-sized ones. And at the end of the row, nine pairs of tiny ones, all different colours, no bigger than thimbles.

Maggie Mae counted them without meaning to.

"Nine," she said softly. "Nine tiny pairs."

Mrs Quill sat down slowly in a low chair by the fire, and picked up two long wooden knitting needles and a half-finished mitten, and began, very slowly and steadily, to knit.

"The ones on that tree are mostly worn through," she said, not looking up. "Holes in the thumbs. Holes in the palms. A mitten with a hole lets the cold in, and a cold paw's a miserable thing. So I take them home and unpick them, and knit the wool up again into new ones that'll keep a paw properly warm." Click, click, went the needles. "And then I leave them on the doorsteps of them that need them. At night. When nobody's about."

"The rabbit kits," said Reba slowly. "Their mittens wore out."

"Nine of them," said Mrs Quill. "I could see their little paws were cold, going past my hedge to the creek. I've been at those all week." Click, click. "And Brody's grown out of his. I noticed, the day of the first snow. His paws were poking out of the ends. And the miller's wife's back is bad, and her paws get cold sitting still at that window."

"But why don't you just give them to people?" asked Maggie Mae. "In the daytime? So they know it's you?"

Mrs Quill's needles stopped for a moment.

"Folk step back from me," she said quietly. "On the path. They think I'll throw my quills at them." She almost smiled. "I can't, you know. Nobody can. A porcupine's quills only come out if you push against them. But folk step back all the same. So I keep to myself. And I leave the mittens at night." The needles started again. "It's easier."

Reba stood in the middle of the little warm burrow, with the cornbread going cold in her arms, and felt worse than she had ever felt in her whole life.


Chapter Eight — The Truth, All of It

Reba put the cornbread down on the table, very carefully.

"Mrs Quill," she said. "I have to tell you something. And it's not nice."

The needles stopped.

"Everybody in the Hollow thinks you've been stealing the mittens," said Reba. Her voice shook, but she kept going. "Taking all of them, for yourself. They're saying it down at the bridge right now. And it's my fault. I saw you take four pairs at dusk last night, and I decided you were greedy, without asking. I told everybody this morning. Because yesterday, somebody decided the same thing about me, without asking. And I was so cross about it, that when I saw you, I did exactly the same thing to you." She took a breath. "I'm so sorry. It was the worst thing I've ever done."

Mrs Quill looked at her for a long moment. Then she set her knitting down in her lap.

"Well," she said. "I'm not surprised folk thought it. I'd have thought it myself, I expect."

"But you're doing something kind," said Reba. "The kindest thing anybody's done all winter. And everybody thinks it's the opposite."

"Then we'll have to put that right, won't we?" said Maggie Mae.

Mrs Quill looked at her.

"There's no need," she said. "I don't mind what folk think."

"I think you do," said Maggie Mae gently. "A little. Or you wouldn't leave the mittens at night."

Mrs Quill did not answer that. She looked into the fire.

So they set about putting it right, and they did not do it with a speech.

The very next morning, the Mitten Tree by the bridge had something new hanging on it. Not mittens. A card, in Maggie Mae's best curly writing, tied with a pink ribbon. It said:

The worn mittens from this tree are being mended into warm new ones by Mrs Quill, at the end of the creek path. If your mittens have holes, bring them to her. If you have spare wool, bring that too. She would be glad of help. And Reba was wrong about her, and is very sorry, and says so.

And underneath that, smaller, in large wobbly letters that went slightly uphill, Charlie had added: SHE CANT THROW HER QUILLS. NOBODY CAN. I ASKED.

By the middle of the morning, there was a crowd at the bridge, reading it.

By the afternoon, there was a crowd at the elder hedge.


Chapter Nine — The Knitting Afternoon

Mrs Quill opened her little round door that afternoon to find most of Hickory Hollow standing in the snow outside her gate.

Her quills went up a little. Then she saw Reba at the front, and Maggie Mae, and Great-Grandpappy Buck, and they settled down again.

The rabbit mother was there, with all nine kits, and every one of them was wearing a pair of tiny new mittens that they had found on their doorstep that very morning. They had come to say thank you. The littlest one could not say it, so she just held up both paws and waved them, so that Mrs Quill could see her own knitting, warm and whole, on the smallest paws in the Hollow.

The miller's wife had sent a basket of spare wool, all the odd ends she had been saving for years.

Brody came, wearing a pair of enormous new brown mittens with a red stripe round the cuffs, which had been on his doorstep when he got up, and which fitted his big paws perfectly for the first time in his life.

"I couldn't think who'd know my size," he said, holding them up in wonder. "Nobody ever gets my size right."

"I watched you go by on the path," said Mrs Quill shyly. "Your paws were poking out."

And then, because the burrow was far too small for everybody, they brought the knitting outside.

Benny and Barney cleared the snow from the space in front of the elder hedge and carried out Mrs Quill's low chair, and every other chair anybody could find. Great-Grandpappy Buck built a small fire in a ring of stones, well away from the hedge, and kept it himself. Maggie Mae sorted all the donated wool into colours, with a list. Charlie wound the unpicked wool into balls, which she was very good at, and which she did so fast that she got tangled up twice and had to be unwound. Rusty and Beau went up and down the creek path collecting holey mittens from every house. Sadie Belle sat beside Mrs Quill and asked her to teach her to knit, and Mrs Quill did, slowly, needle by needle, and Sadie Belle's first row went uphill like Charlie's writing, and nobody minded at all.

And Reba sat at Mrs Quill's feet and learned to unpick.

It turned out that her clever, curious paws, which had poked into every cupboard in the Hollow, were perfect for it. She could find the loose end of any mitten, however well it was hidden, and pull it free, and unravel it row by row into a long crinkly strand without a single tangle.

"You've a gift for it," said Mrs Quill, watching her.

"I like finding out what's inside things," said Reba. "Even mittens."

Mrs Quill laughed. It was a small, creaky laugh, as if it had not been used for a long time. But it was a real one.


Chapter Ten — The Quiet One

As the afternoon went blue and the first stars began to show over the bare elder hedge, the fire burned down low, and the knitting slowed, and the folk of the Hollow began to drift home along the creek path with their lanterns, and their new mittens, and their holey ones left in Mrs Quill's basket to be made new.

Nobody stepped back off the path as they passed her. Several of them, without quite knowing why, stopped and said goodnight.

At last only a few were left round the embers: Mrs Quill in her low chair with her needles, and Great-Grandpappy Buck on an upturned log beside her, and Reba and Maggie Mae on a blanket on the snow, and Charlie asleep between them, wound in a long strand of red wool like a parcel.

"Mrs Quill," said Reba quietly. "Will you come to the Longest Night? On Grandpappy Buck's porch? Everybody would like it if you did."

Mrs Quill's needles went click, click, click.

"I might," she said. "If somebody walks along the path with me."

"I will," said Reba at once.

"So will I," said Maggie Mae.

Mrs Quill nodded, and went on knitting, and did not say anything more. But her quills lay flat and smooth and soft along her back, like the bristles of a brush that has been put away happy.

Great-Grandpappy Buck looked into the glowing embers for a long time.

"I've known Mrs Quill a good many years," he said at last, slowly. "Longer than any of you have been alive. And I couldn't have told you, till today, what she did of an evening, all alone at the end of the path." He shook his head. "I never asked. I'm ashamed to say it, but I never once asked."

He looked at Reba, and then at the row of tiny mittens drying by the fire.

"The creek that runs deepest makes the least noise," said Buck.

Mrs Quill's needles paused for a moment. Then they went on.

Buck stood up slowly, with his walking-stick, and put out the last of the embers carefully with snow, until there was nothing left but a soft hiss and a curl of steam, and lifted his lantern.

"Time for home," he said. "Mind the path. It's icy past the last cabin."

Maggie Mae picked up Charlie, wool and all, without waking her.

Reba stood up last. At the gate, she turned round.

Mrs Quill was standing in her little round doorway, under the snowy elder hedge, with the firelight from inside behind her.

"Goodnight, Mrs Quill," said Reba.

"Goodnight, Reba," said Mrs Quill. And then, after a moment, quietly: "Thank you for coming to ask."

The door closed softly.

They walked home along the creek path together in the starlight, the old beaver with his lantern in front, and Maggie Mae carrying Charlie in her wool, and Reba at the back, holding Maggie Mae's free paw. All along the bank, in the windows of the cabins, the lamps were going out, one after another.

And on the doorstep of every house where a paw had been cold, a pair of warm new mittens was waiting, and nobody had to wonder any longer who had put them there.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
