Chapter One — Awake in Winter

Chipmunks are not very good at winter.

That is not a rude thing to say. It is simply true. When the cold comes, most chipmunk families in Hickory Hollow curl up snug in their burrows with their winter store of nuts and seeds piled up beside them, and sleep, and sleep, and sleep. Every few days they wake up, have a little something to eat from the store, look round sleepily, and go straight back to sleep again.

Since the deep cold had come at the turn of the year, Charlie's family had been exactly like that.

Charlie was not.

She woke up on a grey morning in the middle of winter, in the round snug burrow under the big beech, and looked round. Her mother was asleep. Her father was asleep. Her aunt and her two small cousins were asleep in a heap in the corner, breathing slow and soft, with their tails over their noses.

Charlie had a nut from the store. Then she had another. Then she lay down and closed her eyes and tried very hard to go back to sleep.

She could not. Her eyes kept popping open. Her tail kept twitching. Her paws wanted to run. Her whole small striped body was fizzing with wide-awakeness, the way a kettle rumbles just before it sings.

She wriggled out from under her blanket, straightened her purple gingham bow, and decided that if she could not sleep, she would at least look.

She crept to the door, and pushed it open a crack, and looked out.

The sky was low and heavy and yellowish-grey, the colour of old porridge. Everything was very still. Not a branch moved. Not a bird sang.

And there, coming along the path with a basket on her arm and a woolly scarf round her neck, was Maggie Mae.

"Maggie Mae!" called Charlie, in her best whisper, which was not very quiet.

Maggie Mae stopped. She was a young skunk, black and white and elegant, with emerald eyes and a pink polka-dot bow, and she was Charlie's best friend and something like a big sister.

"Why, Charlie," she said. "You're awake."

"I'm SO awake," said Charlie. "Everybody else is asleep and I'm awake and I can't stop being awake. Where are you going?"

"To Grandpappy Buck's," said Maggie Mae. "Some of us are going for a story this morning, before the weather turns. He says there's snow coming. A great deal of it." She looked down at Charlie's fizzing face, and then at the sleeping burrow behind her, and smiled. "Would you like to come?"

Charlie went back in, very quietly, and stroked her mother's ear until her mother opened one eye, and then both.

"Mama," she said softly. "Maggie Mae's going to Grandpappy Buck's for a story. Can I go with her?"

Her mother sat up a little, properly awake now, and looked past Charlie at Maggie Mae in the doorway, and at the heavy yellow sky.

"You may," she said. "Stay with Maggie Mae. And if it snows, you stay at Buck's till I come for you. Promise?"

"Promise," said Charlie.

Her mother nodded to Maggie Mae, and Maggie Mae nodded back. Then she curled up again with her tail over her nose.

Charlie was out of the door before Maggie Mae had finished turning round.


Chapter Two — Snowed In

By the time they reached Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge by Grandpappy's Bridge, the first flakes were falling.

By the time Buck had finished his story, the first flakes had become a great many flakes, and the great many flakes had become a thick, whirling, white wall, so that from the window you could not see the bridge, or the creek, or even the end of the porch.

"Well now," said Great-Grandpappy Buck, looking out of the window over his spectacles. "That's settled, then. Nobody's going anywhere till that's blown itself out. Not in that."

The friends looked at each other. There were quite a lot of them. Brody had come, and Sadie Belle, the young deer with the pink floral headband, who had gone straight to the window to watch the snow, and Benny and Barney, and Reba, and Rusty, and Beau, and Maverick, who had flown in just before the snow and was sitting on the mantelpiece looking very pleased to be indoors. And Maggie Mae and Charlie.

"Snowed in!" said Barney delightedly. "At Grandpappy Buck's! All day!"

"Possibly all night," said Buck. "We'll see. There's soup, and there's bread, and there's plenty of firewood. Your families know you're here. We'll be quite all right."

He put another log on the fire himself, and settled back into his big armchair with a sigh.

Charlie was thrilled. For about ten minutes.

Then she began to fidget.

She sat on the rug. She got up. She looked out of the window at the snow. She sat on the rug again. She got up again. She ran round the room once, quite fast, and jumped onto the arm of the sofa, and off again.

"Charlie," said Maggie Mae gently. "Why don't you sit with me?"

Charlie sat with Maggie Mae. For nearly a minute.

Then she was up again, because there was a moth on the window, and then because Maverick had shuffled on the mantelpiece, and then because she had remembered that she had not seen the inside of Buck's cupboard under the stairs for a long time.

Everybody else seemed to know exactly what to do with a snowed-in day.

Benny had found an old ruler of Buck's, with numbers carved on it counting up from the bottom, and every hour, with Buck's say-so, he stepped out onto the sheltered porch to measure how deep the snow had got on the porch rail, and came back in and wrote it down on a scrap of bark. Reba was helping Buck in the kitchen with the soup, which meant mostly tasting it. Brody was playing a very quiet tune on his little banjo, so quiet you had to lean close to hear it. Rusty was lying on the rug with his chin on his paws and his ears pricked, listening to something nobody else could hear. Beau had curled up by the fire and gone to sleep. Sadie Belle was sitting very still by the window, watching the snow.

Charlie did not know how to do any of that. She did not know how to be still. She had never had to. There had always been something to run to.

"I'm bored," she said, to nobody in particular.

"Bored?" said Barney. "We're snowed in at Grandpappy Buck's!"

"I know," said Charlie. "But nothing's happening."


Chapter Three — Too Much Charlie

As the morning went on, Charlie tried to make something happen.

She tried a game of chase with Barney round the sofa. It was a very good game for about three laps. On the fourth lap, Charlie shot between Benny's legs just as he was stepping back in from the porch with his ruler, and the ruler flew out of his paw, skittered across the porch boards, slid off the top step, and vanished into the snow.

"My measuring!" said Benny. "That was my eleven o'clock measuring!"

"Sorry," said Charlie.

She tried a game of hide-and-seek. She hid in the cupboard under the stairs, which was a brilliant hiding place, so brilliant that nobody came to look, and after a while she came out by herself, covered in cobwebs, and found that nobody had noticed she was gone.

She tried to help in the kitchen, where Buck was stirring the soup on the stove and Reba was being allowed to do the tasting, from a spoon, sitting at the table. Charlie climbed up on the table beside Reba to see better, and danced from one foot to the other, and knocked over the salt pot, and the bread board, and very nearly Reba, and Buck had to come over from the stove and lift her down, and say, quite firmly, that the stove was for grown-ups, and that anybody under his roof stayed well back from it.

She tried to make Maverick tell a story, and he said he would in a minute, he was just resting his eyes, and then he fell asleep on the mantelpiece.

She tried to make Brody play a faster tune, and he did, and it woke Beau up, and Beau was cross for a moment, which Beau almost never was.

And at last, a little after lunch, running from the window to the fire and back for the twentieth time, Charlie tripped over Rusty's tail, and fell into Sadie Belle, and Sadie Belle's cup of warm milk went all over the rug.

Everybody looked at Charlie.

Not one of them was cross. Maggie Mae fetched a cloth. Sadie Belle said it did not matter in the least. Rusty said his tail was fine, really. Buck said the rug had seen worse.

But Charlie stood in the middle of the room with milk on her paws and cobwebs on her bow and felt as small as she had ever felt.

"I'm too much," she said, in a very little voice. "Aren't I. I'm too much Charlie for a snowed-in day."

Maggie Mae put down the cloth and knelt in front of her, milk and all.

"You are not too much," she said. "You are exactly enough Charlie. You're just a fast Charlie on a slow day." She looked over at the window, where Sadie Belle had turned round, and something passed between the two of them, a look and a small nod. "And I think Sadie Belle knows a game for slow days."

Sadie Belle stood up.

"Charlie," she said. "Would you like to come and sit on the porch with me for a bit? It's sheltered there, under the roof. And it's quiet."

"Quiet," said Charlie miserably. "That's the worst thing I'm good at."

Sadie Belle smiled.

"I'll show you a game," she said. "It's a quiet game. But it's a good one."


Chapter Four — The Quiet Game

The porch was sheltered from the wind, with the roof overhanging it, and the snow was falling more gently now, in big slow flakes, straight down. Buck came and put a thick blanket on the porch bench, and tucked it round them both, and said he would be just inside the window if they wanted anything, and that he'd be out to check their noses were still on in a little while. Charlie could see him through the glass, settling back in his armchair, and Maggie Mae beside him.

It was very cold. It was very white. And it was very, very quiet.

"Here's the game," said Sadie Belle softly. "It's called Smallest Wonder. You have to sit still. Really still. And watch. And listen. And whoever finds the smallest, most wonderful thing wins."

"What sort of thing?" said Charlie, quietly.

"Any sort," said Sadie Belle. "But you can only find it if you're still. That's the rule. If you move about, it hides."

Charlie looked out at the snow. It was white. It was falling. That was all.

"There isn't anything," she said.

"Not yet," said Sadie Belle.

So they sat.

Charlie sat for the count of twenty, and then fidgeted, and then sat again. She sat for the count of thirty, and her tail twitched, and she sat on it to keep it still. She sat for the count of forty, and her nose itched, and she rubbed it, and Sadie Belle did not say anything, and Charlie sat still again.

"I can't," she said. "My legs want to run. They always want to run."

"I know," said Sadie Belle. "Mine used to as well, when I was littler. Long legs are very bossy." She shifted a little under the blanket. "Here's a secret. Don't try to make them stop. Just let them want to run, and don't let them. It gets easier."

Charlie tried it.

Her legs wanted to run. She let them want to. She did not let them.

And bit by bit, they stopped wanting quite so much.

"Listen," whispered Sadie Belle.

Charlie listened.

At first there was nothing. Then she began to hear it. It was not quite a sound. It was more like the edge of a sound. A soft, endless, whispering hush, all around them, as if the whole world were breathing out very slowly.

"What's that?" said Charlie, hardly out loud.

"That's the snow," said Sadie Belle. "Landing. On everything. All at once."

Charlie listened to the snow landing on everything, all at once. She had never heard it before. She had never been quiet enough.

It was the most enormous small sound she had ever heard.


Chapter Five — Little Visitors

They sat on, listening to the snow.

Then something moved at the end of the porch.

Charlie's head turned sharply, and her paws twitched, and the something vanished in a flicker.

"Oh," she whispered. "It went."

"If you move about, it hides," Sadie Belle reminded her gently. "Let's wait."

So they waited. Charlie kept her head still this time, and only moved her eyes.

After a while, the something came back.

It was a small grey bird with a black cap and a black bib and white cheeks, round as a ball of wool, no bigger than Charlie herself. It hopped along the porch rail, flicking its tail, and looked at them with one bright black eye, and said, very clearly, "Chick-a-dee-dee-dee."

Then another came. And another.

"Chickadees," said Sadie Belle, under her breath. "They come to Buck's porch every winter. He leaves them seeds."

The chickadees hopped along the rail to a little wooden tray at the far end that Charlie had never noticed before. It had a few sunflower seeds in it. Each bird took one seed, and flew off to a branch of the snowy hickory tree beside the porch, and held the seed under its foot, and hammered it open with its tiny beak, tap-tap-tap, and ate what was inside, and came back for another.

They were not frightened of Charlie and Sadie Belle at all. Not as long as they kept still.

Behind the window, inside, Reba had noticed. She watched for a moment with her nose against the glass, and then went quietly to the kitchen, and came back, and opened the porch door just a crack, and slipped a small pile of sunflower seeds from Buck's tin into Sadie Belle's hoof, without a word, and closed the door again.

Sadie Belle tipped half the seeds very slowly into Charlie's paw.

"Hold it out," she whispered. "Flat. Like a little table. And don't move at all. Not even a whisker."

Charlie held out her paw, flat, with the seeds on it. She held it so still that her arm began to ache. She held it until she thought nothing would ever happen, and then she held it some more.

And then a chickadee landed on it.

It weighed almost nothing at all. Its tiny feet were cold and gripped her fingers lightly, like a whisper. It looked up at her with its bright black eye. It picked out one sunflower seed, very carefully, choosing the best one. And then it was gone, back to the hickory branch, tap-tap-tap.

Charlie did not move. She could not move. She sat with her paw out and her mouth open and her whole body full of a feeling she did not have a name for.

"Sadie Belle," she said, when she could breathe again. "A bird stood on me."

"I saw," said Sadie Belle, very softly, so as not to break it.

"A bird stood on me," said Charlie again, wonderingly. "On my actual paw."

"On your actual paw," said Sadie Belle.

The porch door opened a crack. Beau slipped out, yawning, with his ear tufts flattened from sleep, and saw the chickadees on the rail, and stopped.

Then, instead of bounding over, he did something Charlie had never seen him do. He put one paw down. Then he waited. Then he put the next paw down. Then he waited. One step at a time, as if every board were a footprint he had to read before he trod on it, he came along the porch and sat down on the far side of Sadie Belle, and folded his paws, and kept his tail quite still. It took him nearly a whole minute. Not one chickadee flew away.

"Sadie Belle's been teaching me," he told Charlie, under his breath, a little proudly. "Ever since the Winter Games. Slow tracking. One print at a time." He glanced at Sadie Belle. "I'm not very good yet."

"You're much better," said Sadie Belle.

Beau looked at Charlie's outstretched paw, and his ears went up.

"Look at you," he said. "Still as a stone. A little striped stone."

Charlie had never in her life been called a stone. She found that she liked it very much.


Chapter Six — Finding Wonders

After that, Charlie did not need to be told how to play Smallest Wonder. She was much too busy finding them.

She held out her paw again, and again, and three more chickadees came, one after another, and the third one stayed so long, sitting on her finger with its seed, that Charlie could see every tiny grey feather on its back.

Then, through the falling snow, out across the white blur where the creek ran, a flash of red.

"Oh," said Charlie, hardly moving her mouth. "What was that?"

The porch door opened a crack, and Maverick's white head came round it.

"Cardinal," he said, very quietly for Maverick. "On the far bank. In the holly. A boy one, red all over. I've been watching him from the window." He looked at Charlie's outstretched paw. "Is that a chickadee on you? Well, I never. Don't stop."

He stayed in the doorway after that, very still for an eagle, and did not say another single word for a quarter of an hour, which for Maverick was a kind of wonder all by itself.

Once, Buck came out, as he had promised, and asked whether their paws were warm and their noses were still on, and felt Charlie's ears to make sure, and tucked the blanket in again, and went back to his armchair.

And Charlie found things of her own.

She found that the icicles hanging from the edge of the porch roof had tiny bubbles trapped inside them, like little silver beads, hundreds of them, and that if you looked through an icicle at the snowy bank, the whole world went wobbly and bent and upside down. She looked through one icicle, and then another, and then a third, until Sadie Belle had to put a hoof over her mouth so as not to laugh out loud at the faces she was making.

And then, best of all, the window.

The window of Buck's front room looked out onto the porch, and the room inside was warm, and the porch outside was cold, and as the afternoon went on and grew colder, something was happening on the glass.

At first it was only a mist. Then, in the corners, tiny white feathery lines began to grow. They grew so slowly that you could only see them moving if you kept very still and watched one spot. They branched, and branched again, into fronds and ferns and curling feathers, spreading across the glass like a forest growing in a dream.

"Frost ferns," said Sadie Belle. "When it's cold enough outside, and warm enough inside, they grow on the glass. My grandmother used to say Jack Frost drew them. But they're just ice. Growing."

Inside, Maggie Mae had seen too. She came and sat on the other side of the glass, very close, and pointed at one fern with her paw, and Charlie pointed at the same one from the outside, and the two of them sat on opposite sides of the window and watched it grow, and neither of them moved.


Chapter Seven — The Snow Fleas

The snow began to ease off in the late afternoon.

The great white wall thinned to a curtain, and then to a few drifting flakes, and then, all at once, it stopped, and the sky began to break up into long grey streaks with pale gold light between them. For an hour the air went soft and mild, as it sometimes does when a snowstorm has blown itself out, before the clear cold night comes down. And the whole Hollow lay under a thick, smooth, perfect blanket of new snow, so deep that the porch steps had disappeared, and the bridge rail was just a long soft white hump.

The others came out onto the porch then, all of them, carefully, onto the sheltered boards, to look.

"Two hands and a whisker deep," said Benny, measuring the snow on the end of the porch rail with a twig, since his ruler was still somewhere out in the drifts. "Since this morning. That's the most I've ever measured."

Rusty was leaning over the far end of the porch rail, where the old hickory grew close beside it, looking down at the foot of its trunk. The tree had kept the snow thin there, in a little ring, so that the brown leaves underneath showed through.

"Charlie," he said, very low. "Come and look at this. Slowly."

Charlie came. Slowly.

On the snow round the foot of the hickory, where the white met the brown leaves, there were specks. Tiny dark specks, like pepper sprinkled on sugar. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

And they were jumping.

Every few moments, one of the tiny specks would suddenly flick up into the air, no higher than a whisker, and land again a little way off. And then another. And another. The whole edge of the snow was alive with tiny hopping pepper.

"What are they?" said Charlie.

"Snow fleas," said Rusty. "That's what everybody calls them. They're not really fleas. They're some of the tiniest creatures there are. They live in the old leaves under the snow, and when it's mild, they come up to the top. They've got a little spring underneath, and when they want to go somewhere, they flick it, and pop."

Charlie stared at the hopping specks. Each one was smaller than a crumb. And every one of them was alive, and hopping, on the top of the snow, on a winter afternoon, right there at the foot of Great-Grandpappy Buck's hickory.

"That's the smallest wonder," she said. "That's the smallest one of all."

Rusty's whiskers twitched.

"I've been watching them for ages," he said. "I didn't want to say. You were busy with your chickadee."

"You win, then," said Charlie generously. "You found them first."

"No," said Rusty. "I'd never have shown anybody if you hadn't been sitting so still. I'd have thought you'd just shout and frighten them." He looked at her. "You didn't, though."

"No," said Charlie, surprised. "I didn't."


Chapter Eight — The Snow Measure

As the gold light faded, Charlie stood on the edge of the porch and looked out at the deep new snow, and something caught her eye.

Just at the bottom of the buried porch steps, a little to the left, there was a small straight shape sticking up out of the snow. Only the very end of it showed.

"Benny," she said quietly. "Is that your ruler?"

Benny peered. It was. It had skittered off the top step that morning and gone into the snow end-first, and stuck there, upright, and the snow had been piling up round it ever since.

"How did you even see that?" said Benny. "I've been looking for it all afternoon."

"She sees everything," said Maggie Mae. "She always has. Quickest eyes in the Hollow."

"I'll get it!" said Barney, and was halfway over the porch rail before Buck's voice said "Barney," from the doorway, and Barney climbed back again.

"Nobody's going off this porch into snow that deep," said Buck. "Not today."

But Charlie was looking at the ruler with her head on one side.

"Leave it," she said. "Look. It's measuring by itself."

Everybody looked.

The ruler stood upright in the snow, with its carved numbers counting up from the bottom. It had gone in at the morning's snow, and everything that had fallen since had piled up round it, and the new snow came up to a number. You could read it from the porch.

"It's been measuring all day," said Charlie. "While we were inside. Ever since I knocked it off the porch. It's been standing out there in the snow, all on its own, keeping still, and measuring."

Benny looked at the ruler for a while. Then he began to smile.

"You're right," he said. "It's been doing my job better than I was. I only measured on the porch rail, and the wind kept blowing it off." He wrote the number down carefully on his bark. "It's next to the steps, so the snow's drifted a bit there. But it's a better number than mine."

"So it was a good thing I knocked it out?" said Charlie hopefully.

"I wouldn't go that far," said Benny. But he was still smiling.

"New game!" said Barney at once. "Everybody guess what number it'll say tomorrow morning. Closest wins."

So everybody guessed, and Benny wrote all the guesses down on the back of his bark, and Charlie guessed the highest number of anybody, because she always did, and Barney guessed a number so enormous that Benny said it was taller than the lodge, and Barney said that was the point.

Then, from inside, came the smell of soup, warmed up again, and Buck's voice saying that anybody who wanted some had better come and get it before Reba finished it, and everybody laughed and went in.

Charlie went in last. She stopped in the doorway and looked back at the porch rail, where the chickadees had gone to roost for the night somewhere in the hickory, and the frost ferns covered the whole of the window now in silver, and the tiny snow fleas were still hopping round the foot of the hickory in the last of the light.

Then she went in, and sat down by the fire, and ate her soup.

Afterwards, Brody took out his little banjo again and played the quietest tune he knew, so quiet that the notes seemed to float in the warm air of the room like the snowflakes outside. Charlie sat beside him and listened, and found that she could hear things inside the tune that she had never noticed before: the little pause between the notes, and the way the last note of each line hung in the air for a moment before it went.

"I didn't know there were gaps in it," she said, very low.

"The gaps are the best bit," Brody said back. "That's where the tune breathes."

She sat still the whole time.


Chapter Nine — The Path to the Steps

Just as the sky turned from grey to deep blue, there came a scraping sound from outside, and a great deal of puffing.

It was the miller, with a wide wooden shovel, digging a path through the deep snow from the creek path all the way to Buck's buried steps, and then digging the steps themselves out again, one by one, taking great care to go round Benny's ruler and leave it standing. Behind him, along the narrow trodden way he had made, came the families of the Hollow, in ones and twos, to fetch their youngsters home.

Charlie's mother was among them, on snowshoes of woven willow so that she would not sink, with her tail wrapped round her neck like a scarf. She had woken in the afternoon, and seen the snow, and remembered her promise, and come.

"Charlie!" she called, from the bottom of the steps.

Charlie ran to the door. Then she stopped, because she remembered the chickadees, roosting somewhere in the hickory.

So she did not shout. She went out onto the porch, quick and quiet on her toes, and waved with both paws, as hard as she possibly could.

"Hello, Mama!" she said, in the biggest whisper in the world. "You'll never believe what happened. A bird stood on me."

Her mother's eyebrows went right up into her fur.

"Stood on you?"

"On my actual paw," said Charlie. "And I found the smallest wonder. And I found Benny's ruler. And I learned a quiet game, and I looked through an icicle, and I heard the snow land." She stopped for breath. "I can do still now, Mama. As well as everything else."

Her mother laughed, and held out her arms, and Charlie went down the miller's dug-out steps in one enormous bound and landed in them, which was exactly what everybody had expected her to do, and nobody minded at all.

The grown-ups came up onto the porch and stamped the snow off their feet and had a little soup, and there was a great deal of talking and laughing and wrapping-up, and finding of mittens, and Benny's mother admiring his snow measurements, and Reba's mother asking how much soup Reba had eaten, and Reba saying not very much, really, considering.

Charlie found Sadie Belle by the window, looking at the frost ferns.

"Sadie Belle," she said. "Thank you for showing me the quiet game."

"You found all the wonders yourself," said Sadie Belle. "I only showed you where to sit."

"Can we play it again?" said Charlie. "Next time it snows? Beau can come too. He's getting better."

"Every time it snows," said Sadie Belle, "if you like."

Charlie thought about it. Then she gave Sadie Belle the very gentlest hug she had ever given anybody, so gentle it would not have frightened a chickadee. And then, because she could not help it, a second one, much bigger and quicker, which nearly knocked Sadie Belle over, and made them both laugh.


Chapter Ten — The Crackle

Before anybody set off, they all went out onto the porch to say goodbye, grown-ups and youngsters together, in the deep blue snowy dusk.

The mild hour was over. The air had turned so cold again that it made your nose tingle, and it was so still that, far down under its blanket of snow, you could hear the creek, still running, talking quietly to itself.

Great-Grandpappy Buck came out last, with his walking-stick, and stood at the top of the newly dug steps with his paw on the rail. Charlie went and stood beside him.

For a little while, they both just listened.

"Grandpappy," said Charlie, very low. "Can you hear it?"

"Hear what, little one?"

"The creek, under the snow," said Charlie. "And the snow sliding off the branches. Flump. And there's a little crackle, in the trees. Over there." She pointed. "Listen. There it is again."

Buck tipped his head, and listened. His old eyes crinkled up behind his spectacles.

"Well now," he said softly. "I've stood on this porch every winter evening for more years than I can count. And I'd forgotten that crackle." He listened again. "That's the cold getting into the branches as the night comes down. My grandfather used to call it the trees putting on their nightshirts." He chuckled. "I haven't heard it in years. I suppose I stopped listening for it."

"I'll listen for it for you," said Charlie. "Whenever you forget."

So they stood there together, the old beaver and the small chipmunk, at the top of the steps, and listened to the trees putting on their nightshirts.

Then Buck said, "There's magic in an ordinary evening, little one, if you sit still long enough to feel it."

After that the porch filled up with goodbyes. Benny showed his mother the bark with the snow numbers on it, and all the guesses for the morning. Barney told everybody his guess again, in case they had forgotten it. Maggie Mae wound Charlie's scarf round twice, and then a third time, and tied it in a bow under her chin, and said, "Exactly enough Charlie," so quietly that only Charlie heard. Sadie Belle's family set off first, and Sadie Belle turned at the end of the miller's path, and waved.

Then Charlie and her mother set off for home, along the narrow trodden path through the deep snow, with the miller walking just ahead of them with his lantern and his shovel, because the big beech was on his way. Charlie walked on her own feet, in the flattened prints of her mother's snowshoes. Every now and then, where the path grew wide enough, she did a little skipping hop, just because she could. Her mother did not tell her to stop.

At the round green door under the big beech, the miller said goodnight, and her mother went in first to stir up the warm, holding the door open behind her, and Charlie stood for a moment on the doorstep, quite still, listening to the whole white Hollow settling down for the night.

Then she went inside, and curled up in her bed beside her sleeping cousins, and tucked her tail over her nose, and was asleep before her mother had even finished saying goodnight.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
