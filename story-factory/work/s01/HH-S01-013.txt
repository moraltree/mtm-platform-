Chapter One — Awake in Winter

Chipmunks are not very good at winter.

That is not a rude thing to say. It is simply true. When the cold comes, most chipmunk families in Hickory Hollow curl up snug in their burrows with their winter store of nuts and seeds piled up beside them, and sleep, and sleep, and sleep. Every few days they wake up, have a little something to eat from the store, look round sleepily, and go straight back to sleep again.

Charlie's family was exactly like that.

Charlie was not.

She woke up on a grey morning in the middle of winter, in the round snug burrow under the big beech, and looked round. Her mother was asleep. Her father was asleep. Her aunt and her two small cousins were asleep in a heap in the corner, breathing slow and soft, with their tails over their noses.

Charlie had a nut from the store. Then she had another. Then she lay down and closed her eyes and tried very hard to go back to sleep.

She could not. Her eyes kept popping open. Her tail kept twitching. Her paws wanted to run. Her whole small striped body was fizzing with wide-awakeness, the way a kettle fizzes just before it boils.

Charlotte the chipmunk, whom everybody called Charlie, was young and curious and enthusiastic and loving, with bright blue eyes and a purple gingham bow, and she had never in her life been able to sit still for longer than it took to say so.

She crept to the door, and pushed it open a crack, and looked out.

The sky was low and heavy and yellowish-grey, the colour of old porridge. Everything was very still. Not a branch moved. Not a bird sang.

And there, coming along the path with a basket on her arm and a woolly scarf round her neck, was Maggie Mae.

"Maggie Mae!" whispered Charlie, as loudly as she could whisper, which was not very quietly.

Maggie Mae stopped. She was a young skunk, black and white and elegant, with emerald eyes and a pink polka-dot bow, and she was Charlie's best friend and something like a big sister.

"Why, Charlie," she said. "You're awake."

"I'm SO awake," said Charlie. "Everybody else is asleep and I'm awake and I can't stop being awake. Where are you going?"

"To Grandpappy Buck's," said Maggie Mae. "Some of us are going for a story this morning, before the weather turns. He says there's snow coming. A great deal of it." She looked down at Charlie's fizzing face, and then at the sleeping burrow behind her, and smiled. "Would you like to come?"

Charlie went back in, very quietly, and put her nose to her mother's ear.

"Mama," she whispered. "Can I go to Grandpappy Buck's with Maggie Mae?"

"Mmm," said her mother, without opening her eyes. "Stay with Maggie Mae. Stay at Buck's if it snows. I'll come and fetch you when it stops." And she went straight back to sleep.

Charlie was out of the door before Maggie Mae had finished turning round.


Chapter Two — Snowed In

By the time they reached Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge by Grandpappy's Bridge, the first flakes were falling.

By the time Buck had finished his story, the first flakes had become a great many flakes, and the great many flakes had become a thick, whirling, white wall, so that from the window you could not see the bridge, or the creek, or even the end of the porch.

"Well now," said Great-Grandpappy Buck, looking out of the window over his spectacles. "That's settled, then. Nobody's going anywhere till that's blown itself out. Not in that."

The friends looked at each other. There were quite a lot of them. Brody had come, and Sadie Belle, and Benny and Barney, and Reba, and Rusty, and Beau, and Maverick, who had flown in just before the snow and was sitting on the mantelpiece looking very pleased to be indoors. And Maggie Mae and Charlie.

"Snowed in!" said Barney delightedly. "At Grandpappy Buck's! All day!"

"Possibly all night," said Buck. "We'll see. There's soup, and there's bread, and there's plenty of firewood. Your families know you're here. We'll be quite all right."

He put another log on the fire himself, and settled back into his armchair with Bramble's quilt over his knees.

Charlie was thrilled. For about ten minutes.

Then she began to fidget.

She sat on the rug. She got up. She looked out of the window at the snow. She sat on the rug again. She got up again. She ran round the room once, quite fast, and jumped onto the arm of the sofa, and off again.

"Charlie," said Maggie Mae gently. "Why don't you sit with me?"

Charlie sat with Maggie Mae. For nearly a minute.

Then she was up again, because there was a moth on the window, and then because Maverick had shuffled on the mantelpiece, and then because she had remembered that she had not seen the inside of Buck's cupboard under the stairs for a long time.

Everybody else seemed to know exactly what to do with a snowed-in day.

Benny had found a ruler, and was measuring how deep the snow was getting on the windowsill outside, every hour, and writing it down on a scrap of bark. Reba was helping Buck in the kitchen with the soup, which meant mostly tasting it. Brody was playing a very quiet tune on his little banjo, so quiet you had to lean close to hear it. Rusty was lying on the rug with his chin on his paws and his ears pricked, listening to something nobody else could hear. Beau had curled up by the fire and gone to sleep. Sadie Belle was sitting very still by the window, watching the snow.

Charlie did not know how to do any of that. She did not know how to be still. She had never had to. There had always been something to run to.

"I'm bored," she said, to nobody in particular.

"Bored?" said Barney. "We're snowed in at Grandpappy Buck's!"

"I know," said Charlie. "But nothing's happening."


Chapter Three — Too Much Charlie

As the morning went on, Charlie tried to make something happen.

She tried a game of chase with Barney round the sofa. It was a very good game for about three laps. On the fourth lap, Charlie ran under Benny's chair just as he was reaching out of the window with his ruler, and his elbow jerked, and the ruler flew out of his paw and out into the snow and vanished.

"My measuring!" said Benny. "That was my eleven o'clock measuring!"

"Sorry," said Charlie.

She tried a game of hide-and-seek. She hid in the cupboard under the stairs, which was a brilliant hiding place, so brilliant that nobody came to look, and after a while she came out by herself, covered in cobwebs, and found that nobody had noticed she was gone.

She tried to help Reba in the kitchen. She stirred the soup so enthusiastically that a great dollop of it slopped over the side of the pot and onto the stove with a hiss, and Great-Grandpappy Buck had to come and lift the pot off and say, quite firmly, that stoves were for grown-ups, and stirring was for afterwards, when it had cooled.

She tried to make Maverick tell a story, and he said he would in a minute, he was just resting his eyes, and then he fell asleep on the mantelpiece.

She tried to make Brody play a faster tune, and he did, and it woke Beau up, and Beau was cross for a moment, which Beau almost never was.

And at last, a little after lunch, running from the window to the fire and back for the twentieth time, Charlie tripped over Rusty's tail, and fell into Sadie Belle, and Sadie Belle's cup of warm milk went all over the rug.

Everybody looked at Charlie.

Nobody said anything unkind. Maggie Mae fetched a cloth. Sadie Belle said it did not matter in the least. Rusty said his tail was fine, really. Buck said the rug had seen worse.

But Charlie stood in the middle of the room with milk on her paws and cobwebs on her bow and felt as small as she had ever felt.

"I'm too much," she said, in a very little voice. "Aren't I. I'm too much Charlie for a snowed-in day."

Maggie Mae put down the cloth and opened her arms. But before she could say anything, Sadie Belle had stood up.

Sadie Belle was a young deer, gentle and kind, with green eyes and a pink floral headband and a soft, slow way of moving. She was the quietest of all of them. She did not seem to mind sitting still at all.

"Charlie," she said. "Would you like to come and sit on the porch with me for a bit? It's sheltered there, under the roof. And it's quiet."

"Quiet," said Charlie miserably. "That's the worst thing I'm good at."

Sadie Belle smiled.

"I'll show you a game," she said. "It's a quiet game. But it's a good one."


Chapter Four — The Quiet Game

The porch was sheltered from the wind, with the roof overhanging it, and the snow was falling more gently now, in big slow flakes, straight down. Buck came and put a thick blanket on the porch bench, and tucked it round them both, and said he would be just inside the window if they wanted anything. Charlie could see him through the glass, settling back in his armchair, and Maggie Mae beside him.

It was very cold. It was very white. And it was very, very quiet.

"Here's the game," said Sadie Belle softly. "It's called Smallest Wonder. You have to sit still. Really still. And watch. And listen. And whoever finds the smallest, most wonderful thing wins."

"What sort of thing?" whispered Charlie.

"Any sort," said Sadie Belle. "But you can only find it if you're still. That's the rule. If you move about, it hides."

Charlie looked out at the snow. It was white. It was falling. That was all.

"There isn't anything," she whispered.

"Not yet," said Sadie Belle.

So they sat.

Charlie sat for the count of twenty, and then fidgeted, and then sat again. She sat for the count of thirty, and her tail twitched, and she sat on it to keep it still. She sat for the count of forty, and her nose itched, and she rubbed it, and Sadie Belle did not say anything, and Charlie sat still again.

"I can't," she whispered. "My legs want to run. They always want to run."

"I know," said Sadie Belle. "Mine used to as well, when I was littler. Long legs are very bossy." She shifted a little under the blanket. "Here's a secret. Don't try to make them stop. Just let them want to run, and don't let them. It gets easier."

Charlie tried it.

Her legs wanted to run. She let them want to. She did not let them.

And slowly, very slowly, they stopped wanting quite so much.

"Listen," whispered Sadie Belle.

Charlie listened.

At first there was nothing. Then, slowly, she began to hear it. It was not quite a sound. It was more like the edge of a sound. A soft, endless, whispering hush, all around them, as if the whole world were breathing out very slowly.

"What's that?" breathed Charlie.

"That's the snow," whispered Sadie Belle. "Landing. On everything. All at once."

Charlie listened to the snow landing on everything, all at once. She had never heard it before. She had never been quiet enough.

It was the most enormous small sound she had ever heard.


Chapter Five — Little Visitors

They sat a long while, listening to the snow.

Then something moved at the end of the porch.

Charlie's head turned sharply, and her paws twitched, and the something vanished in a flicker.

"Oh," she whispered. "It went."

"If you move about, it hides," Sadie Belle reminded her gently. "Let's wait."

So they waited. Charlie kept her head still this time, and only moved her eyes.

After a while, the something came back.

It was a small grey bird with a black cap and a black bib and white cheeks, round as a ball of wool, no bigger than Charlie herself. It hopped along the porch rail, flicking its tail, and looked at them with one bright black eye, and said, very clearly, "Chick-a-dee-dee-dee."

Then another came. And another.

"Chickadees," breathed Sadie Belle. "They come to Buck's porch every winter. He leaves them seeds."

The chickadees hopped along the rail to a little wooden tray at the far end that Charlie had never noticed before. It had a few sunflower seeds in it. Each bird took one seed, and flew off to a branch of the snowy hickory tree beside the porch, and held the seed under its foot, and hammered it open with its tiny beak, tap-tap-tap, and ate what was inside, and came back for another.

They were not frightened of Charlie and Sadie Belle at all. Not as long as they kept still.

Behind the window, inside, Reba had noticed. She watched for a moment with her nose against the glass, and then went quietly to the kitchen, and came back, and opened the porch door just a crack, and slipped a small pile of sunflower seeds from Buck's tin into Sadie Belle's hoof, without a word, and closed the door again.

Sadie Belle tipped half the seeds very slowly into Charlie's paw.

"Hold it out," she whispered. "Flat. Like a little table. And don't move at all. Not even a whisker."

Charlie held out her paw, flat, with the seeds on it. She held it so still that her arm began to ache. She held it until she thought nothing would ever happen, and then she held it some more.

And then a chickadee landed on it.

It weighed nothing. It weighed less than a leaf. Its tiny feet were cold and gripped her fingers lightly, like a whisper. It looked up at her with its bright black eye. It picked out one sunflower seed, very carefully, choosing the best one. And then it was gone, back to the hickory branch, tap-tap-tap.

Charlie did not move. She could not move. She sat with her paw out and her mouth open and her whole body full of a feeling she did not have a name for.

"Sadie Belle," she breathed, when she could breathe again. "A bird stood on me."

"I saw," whispered Sadie Belle, and her green eyes were shining.

"A bird stood on me," said Charlie again, wonderingly. "Because I was still. It wouldn't have, if I'd been running about."

"No," said Sadie Belle. "It wouldn't."

The porch door creaked, very softly. Beau slipped out, yawning, with his ear tufts flattened from sleep, and stopped dead when he saw the chickadees on the rail.

"Ooh," he breathed, and his tail began to twitch with excitement, and the nearest chickadee flicked away at once.

"Tail," whispered Sadie Belle.

Beau looked round at his own tail as if it belonged to somebody else. Then he did something Charlie had never seen him do. He lowered himself down onto the porch boards, slowly, slowly, until his tummy touched the wood, and tucked his paws in, and let his tail lie flat and still behind him. And then he simply stopped. He did not twitch. He did not blink. He became, as far as Charlie could tell, a tawny spotted cushion with two blue eyes in it.

"How do you do that?" whispered Charlie, without moving her lips.

"Bobcat trick," whispered Beau, without moving his. "When I want to watch a butterfly, I pretend I'm a stone. Stones don't want anything. They just wait." His blue eyes flicked to her outstretched paw. "You're doing it too. Look at you. You're a little striped stone."

Charlie had never in her life been called a stone. She found that she liked it very much.


Chapter Six — Finding Wonders

After that, Charlie did not need to be told how to play Smallest Wonder. She was much too busy finding them.

She held out her paw again, and again, and three more chickadees came, one after another, and the third one stayed so long, sitting on her finger with its seed, that Charlie could see every tiny grey feather on its back.

Then, through the falling snow, out across the white blur where the creek ran, a flash of red.

"Oh," breathed Charlie. "What was that?"

The porch door opened a crack, and Maverick's white head came round it.

"Cardinal," he whispered, very quietly for Maverick. "On the far bank. In the holly. A boy one. I've been watching him from the window." He looked at Charlie's outstretched paw, and his blue eyes went wide. "Is that a chickadee on you?"

"Yes," whispered Charlie, without moving.

"I've never had a bird land on me," whispered Maverick, rather wistfully. "They think I'm going to fly off with them."

He stayed in the doorway after that, very still for an eagle, and pointed out things with his wingtip: the cardinal's red against the white holly, the place where a rabbit's tracks went under the porch, the dark round blob in the top of the hickory that was a squirrel's nest with the squirrel inside it, fast asleep.

And Charlie found things of her own.

She found that each snowflake that landed on the dark wood of the porch rail was a different size. Some were big soft clumps, like feathers. Some were tiny single specks. She found that if you breathed out very gently, your breath made a little cloud that hung in the air and slowly faded, and that Sadie Belle's breath-cloud was bigger than hers, and that if you breathed out at the same time, the clouds met in the middle.

She found that the icicles hanging from the porch roof had tiny bubbles trapped inside them, like little silver beads.

And then, best of all, the window.

The window of Buck's front room looked out onto the porch, and the room inside was warm, and the porch outside was cold, and as the afternoon went on and grew colder, something was happening on the glass.

At first it was only a mist. Then, in the corners, tiny white feathery lines began to grow. They grew slowly, slowly, so slowly that you could only see them moving if you kept very still and watched one spot. They branched, and branched again, into fronds and ferns and curling feathers, spreading across the glass like a forest growing in a dream.

"Frost ferns," whispered Sadie Belle. "When it's cold enough outside, and warm enough inside, they grow on the glass. My grandmother used to say Jack Frost drew them. But they're just ice. Growing."

Inside, Maggie Mae had seen too. She came and sat on the other side of the glass, very close, and pointed at one fern with her paw, and Charlie pointed at the same one from the outside, and the two of them sat on opposite sides of the window and watched it grow, and neither of them moved.


Chapter Seven — The Snow Fleas

The snow began to slow down in the late afternoon.

The great white wall thinned to a curtain, and then to a few drifting flakes, and then, all at once, it stopped, and the sky began to break up into long grey streaks with pale gold light between them. And the whole Hollow lay under a thick, smooth, perfect blanket of new snow, so deep that the porch steps had disappeared, and the bridge rail was just a long soft white hump.

The others came out onto the porch then, all of them, carefully, onto the sheltered boards, to look.

"Two hands and a whisker deep," said Benny, measuring the snow on the end of the porch rail with a twig, since his ruler was still somewhere out in the drifts. "Since this morning. That's the most I've ever measured."

Rusty was crouched at the very edge of the porch, with his nose almost touching the new snow on the top step, very still.

"Charlie," he whispered. "Come and look at this. Slowly."

Charlie came. Slowly.

On the white snow on the top step, right at the edge, where the snow met the dark wood, there were specks. Tiny dark specks, like pepper sprinkled on sugar. Dozens of them. Hundreds.

And they were jumping.

Every few moments, one of the tiny specks would suddenly flick up into the air, no higher than a whisker, and land again a little way off. And then another. And another. The whole edge of the snow was alive with tiny hopping pepper.

"What are they?" breathed Charlie.

"Snow fleas," said Rusty. "That's what my grandfather called them. They're not really fleas. They're the tiniest little creatures there are. They live in the leaves under the snow, and when it's mild, they come up to the top. They've got a little spring underneath, and when they want to go somewhere, they flick it, and pop."

Charlie stared at the hopping specks. Each one was smaller than a crumb. And every one of them was alive, and hopping, on the top of the snow, on a winter afternoon, right there on Great-Grandpappy Buck's step, and if she had been running about, she would never, ever have seen them.

"That's the smallest wonder," she whispered. "That's the smallest one of all."

Rusty grinned.

"I've been watching them for ages," he said. "I didn't want to say. You were busy with your chickadee."

"You win, then," said Charlie generously. "You found them first."

"No," said Rusty. "I'd never have shown anybody if you hadn't been sitting so still. I'd have thought you'd just shout and frighten them." He looked at her. "You didn't, though."

"No," said Charlie, surprised. "I didn't."


Chapter Eight — The Snow Measure

As the gold light faded, Charlie stood on the edge of the porch and looked out at the deep new snow, and something caught her eye.

Just at the bottom of the hidden porch steps, a little to the left, there was a small straight shape sticking up out of the snow. Only the very end of it showed.

"Benny," she said quietly. "Is that your ruler?"

Benny peered. It was. It had fallen out of his paw that morning and landed point-down in the snow, and stuck there, upright, and now the snow had piled up all round it.

"I can't reach it," said Benny. "It's too deep there."

"I can't either," said Brody.

"I could fly down for it," said Maverick.

But Charlie was looking at the ruler with her head on one side.

"Leave it," she said. "Look. It's measuring by itself."

Everybody looked.

The ruler stood upright in the snow, with the numbers Benny had carved into it running down from the top. And the snow came up to a number. You could read it from the porch.

"It's been measuring all day," said Charlie. "While we were inside. Ever since I knocked it out of the window. It's been standing out there in the snow, all on its own, keeping still, and measuring."

Benny looked at the ruler for a long time. Then, slowly, he began to smile.

"You're right," he said. "It's been doing my job better than I was. I only measured on the windowsill, and the wind blew half of it off." He wrote the number down carefully on his bark. "That's the proper number. The real one."

"So it was a good thing I knocked it out?" said Charlie hopefully.

"I wouldn't go that far," said Benny. But he was still smiling.

Then, from inside, came the smell of soup, warmed up again, and Buck's voice saying that anybody who wanted some had better come and get it before Reba finished it, and everybody laughed and went in.

Charlie went in last. She stopped in the doorway and looked back at the porch rail, where the chickadees had gone to roost for the night somewhere in the hickory, and the frost ferns covered the whole of the window now in silver, and the tiny snow fleas were still hopping on the top step in the last of the light.

Then she went in, and sat down by the fire, and ate her soup.

Afterwards, Brody took out his little banjo again and played the quietest tune he knew, so quiet that the notes seemed to float in the warm air of the room like the snowflakes outside. Charlie sat beside him and listened, and found that she could hear things inside the tune that she had never noticed before: the little pause between the notes, and the way the last note of each line hung in the air for a moment before it went.

"I didn't know there were gaps in it," she whispered.

"The gaps are the best bit," Brody whispered back. "That's where the tune breathes."

She sat still the whole time.


Chapter Nine — Lanterns in the Snow

Just as the sky turned from grey to deep blue, there were lights on the creek path.

They came slowly, bobbing, swinging, along the snowy bank. Lanterns. A long line of them. The families of the Hollow, wading through the deep new snow with their lanterns held high, coming to fetch their youngsters home.

Charlie's mother was among them, with her paws in big snowshoes made of woven willow, so that she would not sink, and her tail wrapped round her neck like a scarf. She had woken up, and seen the snow, and remembered, and come.

"Charlie!" she called, from the bottom of the buried steps.

Charlie ran to the door. Then she stopped.

She did not shout. She did not leap. She did not tumble down the steps in a whirl of fur and squeaks, the way she always had before.

She walked out onto the porch, quite slowly, and waved.

"Hello, Mama," she said. "You'll never believe what happened. A bird stood on me."

Her mother looked at her, standing still on the porch in the lamplight, with her paw held out to show where the chickadee had been, and her eyebrows went right up into her fur.

"Well," she said. "Well, I never. Who are you, and what have you done with my Charlie?"

Everybody on the porch laughed, and Charlie laughed too.

The grown-ups came up onto the porch and stamped the snow off their feet and had a little soup, and there was a great deal of talking and laughing and wrapping-up, and finding of mittens, and Benny's mother admiring his snow measurements, and Reba's mother asking how much soup Reba had eaten, and Reba saying not very much, really, considering.

Charlie found Sadie Belle by the window, looking at the frost ferns.

"Sadie Belle," she said. "Thank you for showing me the quiet game."

"You found all the wonders yourself," said Sadie Belle. "I only showed you where to sit."

"Can we play it again?" said Charlie. "Next time it snows?"

"Every time it snows," said Sadie Belle, "if you like."

Charlie thought about it. Then she gave Sadie Belle the very gentlest hug she had ever given anybody, so gentle it would not have frightened a chickadee.


Chapter Ten — An Ordinary Evening

They went out onto the porch to say goodbye, everybody together, in the deep blue snowy dusk.

The sky had cleared completely now. The air was so cold it made your nose tingle, and so still that you could hear the creek, far down under its blanket of snow, still running, talking quietly to itself. The first faint stars were coming out over the white valley.

Great-Grandpappy Buck came out last, slowly, with his walking-stick, and stood at the top of the buried steps with his paw on the rail, looking out.

Charlie stood beside him. She did not fidget. She just stood, and looked, and listened.

"Grandpappy," she whispered. "Can you hear it?"

"Hear what, little one?"

"The quiet," said Charlie. "It's got sounds in it. The creek under the snow. And a little crackle in the trees. And the snow settling. You can only hear them if you stop."

Buck looked down at her for a long moment. His old eyes crinkled up behind his spectacles.

"Well now," he said softly. "I've stood on this porch every winter evening for more years than I can count. And I'd forgotten that crackle." He listened. "There it is."

They stood together and listened to it.

"There's magic in an ordinary evening, little one," said Buck, "if you sit still long enough to feel it."

Charlie did not say anything. She did not need to.

Then it was time to go. The families set off along the snowy creek path with their lanterns held high, in a long, slow, bobbing line, the youngsters walking close beside them in the trodden snow. Maggie Mae walked beside Charlie's mother. Sadie Belle walked with her own family, a little way ahead, and turned round once to wave.

Charlie rode home on her mother's back, because the snow was far too deep for chipmunk legs, wrapped in her mother's tail like a scarf.

She did not chatter all the way home, the way she usually did. She looked. At the lanterns swinging ahead, making little golden circles on the snow. At the dark shapes of the trees, every branch outlined in white. At the stars, coming out one after another in the clear cold sky.

At the round green door under the big beech, her mother set her down, and went in to warm the burrow, and left her for a moment on the doorstep.

Charlie stood very still in the snow.

And out of the dark beech tree above her, sleepily, so soft she almost did not hear it, came one tiny voice.

"Chick-a-dee-dee."

Charlie smiled, and did not move, until it was quiet again.

Then she went inside, and curled up in her bed next to her sleeping cousins, and for the first time all winter, she fell asleep straight away.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
