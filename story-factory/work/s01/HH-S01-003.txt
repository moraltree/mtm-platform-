Chapter One — The List

There is a week in every autumn when Hickory Hollow is at its most beautiful, and everybody knows it.

The hickories have gone gold. The maples have gone red. The sassafras has gone a hundred shades of orange, and its leaves hang like little mittens on every branch. The air is cool and clear and smells of apples. And for that one week, before the wind comes and blows it all away, the whole valley looks as though it has been dressed up for a party.

Which was exactly why Maggie Mae had decided to give one.

She had a list. It was written in her best curly handwriting, on her best cream paper, with a pressed violet glued at the top for luck, and it was pinned to her kitchen wall beside the window, where the morning sun came in and lit it up. It said, at the top, in letters twice the size of all the others:

MAGGIE MAE'S MAGNIFICENT AUTUMN PARTY.

Underneath, there were forty-one things to do.

Maggie Mae stood in front of it with her paws clasped and her tail curled neatly behind her, and read every one twice. She was a young skunk with two white stripes that swept back over her shoulders like ribbons, emerald-green eyes that missed nothing, and a yellow dress printed with tiny white flowers. Her cottage stood at the edge of Fern Meadow, with a rose arch over the gate and a front step she swept every morning whether it needed it or not, and everybody in the Hollow called it Rose Arch Cottage, because of the roses, and the arch.

She had given little teas before. She had baked for other creatures' suppers and arranged the flowers for the Harvest Home. But she had never given a party for the whole of her circle of friends, all at once, in her own home.

And it was going to be perfect.

"Number one," she read aloud. "Write the invitations. Number two. Deliver the invitations. Number three. Bake the apple-spice cake..."

There was a knock at the door. It was a very small knock, very low down, followed at once by a much louder voice.

"Maggie Mae! Maggie Mae! It's me! Are you making a list? I can smell a list!"

Maggie Mae smiled and opened the door, and there on the step stood Charlie.

Charlotte the chipmunk was very young, very small, and very full of everything. Her purple gingham bow had come loose already, and the daisy on the pocket of her overalls was bouncing because she was bouncing.

"Lists don't smell," said Maggie Mae.

"Yours do," said Charlie. "They smell like something nice is going to happen."

She dashed past into the kitchen and stopped in front of the list with her mouth open.

"A PARTY?" she breathed. "A MAGNIFICENT one? Can I help? Please can I help? I'm the best helper in the whole valley. I'll do anything. I'll do everything!"

Maggie Mae looked at the list, with its forty-one neat, careful things. She looked at Charlie, bouncing so hard on her toes that her bow had slipped over one eye.

Something in her wanted to say, "Thank you, sugar, but I'd better do it myself." Something warmer remembered how it felt to be very small and want, more than anything, to be part of something.

"Of course you can help," said Maggie Mae. "You can be my assistant."

Charlie squeaked so loudly that a robin fell off the window box.


Chapter Two — Invitations

The invitations took Maggie Mae all morning.

She wrote each one on a little card, with a pressed autumn leaf in the corner and a thread of pink ribbon round it. Each one said:

You are warmly invited to Maggie Mae's Magnificent Autumn Party, at Rose Arch Cottage, tomorrow, at three o'clock. Tea, cake and dancing. Do come.

She wrote nine of them: one for each of their friends, and one for Great-Grandpappy Buck. When she had finished, they lay on the kitchen table in a neat fan, and they were the prettiest things Charlie had ever seen.

"Now," said Maggie Mae. "Number two. Deliver the invitations. Could you do that, Charlie? Carefully?"

"I can do it SO carefully," said Charlie.

She looked at the nine little cards. She had two paws, and she needed both of them for running. She thought hard.

Then she did what chipmunks have always done when they have something precious to carry.

She put them in her cheek-pouches.

All nine. One after another, folded in half, until her cheeks were round as apples and she could hardly see past them.

"Mmf," said Charlie proudly. "Mmf mmf mmf!"

Maggie Mae opened her mouth to say something. But Charlie was already gone, a little brown streak with enormous cheeks, out of the door and down the lane.

She delivered every one. She ran the whole length of the valley, from the beaver ponds to the Old Mill and back up to Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge, and at every door she took out a card and handed it over with a beaming smile.

There was only one small difficulty, which Charlie did not notice at the time.

A chipmunk's cheek-pouch is a warm, snug place for an acorn. It is not such a good place for ink. By the time each card came out, it was crumpled and a little damp, and the ink had begun to run.

At the beaver brothers' lodge, Benny smoothed his flat on the table and frowned.

"Party," he read. "At... three, I think. Or eight. It might be eight."

"Mine says free o'clock," said Barney happily. "Free o'clock! Is that a time? I like it."

At the foot of Lookout Rock, Beau turned his upside down, and then the right way up, and then upside down again.

"Tomorrow," he said at last. "Or possibly the day after. And there's definitely cake."

Back at Rose Arch Cottage, Charlie burst through the door with empty cheeks and fur standing on end from running.

"Done! Every single one! And nobody dropped any!"

"That's wonderful," said Maggie Mae, and she ticked number two off the list with a neat little stroke.

Charlie did not mention that her cheeks had felt a bit damp. It had not seemed important.


Chapter Three — The Cake That Grew

Number three was the apple-spice cake.

Maggie Mae's apple-spice cake was famous from one end of the Hollow to the other. It was light and golden and smelled of cinnamon and nutmeg, with thin slices of apple baked into the top in a pattern like the petals of a rose.

"Baking is all about being exact," she said, tying an apron round her assistant. It came down to Charlie's toes and had to be folded up twice. "A little of this. A little of that. No more, no less. Can you do exactly?"

"I can do exactly better than anybody," said Charlie.

So Maggie Mae measured, and Charlie poured.

One cup of flour. Charlie poured it in very carefully, and then, because the flour looked lonely, she added a little more.

Two spoons of honey. Charlie dripped them in, and then, because honey was the nicest thing in the world, she added a third.

One spoon of the rising powder that made the cake light and fluffy. Charlie looked at that tiny spoonful and thought it looked much too small to make a magnificent cake. When Maggie Mae turned round to fetch the apples, she quietly added three more.

"More is more," she whispered to herself. "And this is going to be MAGNIFICENT."

Maggie Mae laid the apple slices on top in their beautiful rose and slid the tin into the oven.

They washed the bowls. They swept the flour off the floor. The cottage filled with the warm smell of cinnamon.

And then, slowly, the oven door began to open by itself.

Maggie Mae and Charlie both stopped humming.

Out of the gap came the cake. It had risen out of its tin, and over the edge, and up to the top of the oven, and now it was pushing the door open like a large golden sponge trying to see what was going on outside. The rose of apple slices sat on top of it like a hat, very high up, looking surprised.

"Oh," said Maggie Mae faintly. "Oh, my stars."

She grabbed a cloth and pulled the tin out. The cake went on puffing on the table for a while before it sank back with a long, soft sigh, and leaned over sideways like a tired hen.

"Charlie," said Maggie Mae, very calmly. "How much rising powder did you put in?"

Charlie looked at her toes.

"A bit more," she said. "To make it magnificent."

Maggie Mae closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, she was smiling, though it was a slightly stretched sort of smile, like a ribbon pulled a little too tight.

"Well," she said. "It's certainly magnificent."

They tasted a corner. It was very chewy, and tasted a little odd, and Charlie said it was delicious, and Maggie Mae said it was certainly interesting. She cut it into squares anyway and put them in a tin, because Maggie Mae never threw away anything that somebody had tried hard to make.

"I'll bake another one tomorrow," she said. "On my own. Just to be on the safe side."

"I can help!"

"Why don't you help with the decorations instead?" said Maggie Mae gently.


Chapter Four — The Ladybird Invasion

Charlie thought decorations were the best job of all.

"Leaves!" she announced. "It's an autumn party, so we need autumn leaves. All the colours. I'll fill the whole cottage!"

"Just a few, sugar," said Maggie Mae, who was writing name-cards. "A few in a vase would be lovely."

But Charlie had already gone.

She came back an hour later pushing a wheelbarrow so heaped with leaves that she had to steer by listening. There were red maple leaves and yellow hickory leaves, brown oak leaves and orange sassafras mittens. She had gathered them from the bottom of the deepest, snuggest leaf piles in the Hickory Woods, where they were thick and soft and smelled of earth.

She tipped them out on the cottage floor in a great rustling heap.

"Now," said Charlie, "garlands!"

She worked all afternoon. She threaded leaves onto strings and hung them over the windows. She tucked leaves behind the picture frames. She made a crown of leaves and put it on Maggie Mae's head, and Maggie Mae, still writing name-cards, let her.

Then, as the sun went down and the cottage grew warm from the fire, something began to happen in the heap of leaves.

It started with one ladybird. It crawled up out of the heap, lifted its red wing-cases, and buzzed onto the window. Then came another. Then ten more. Then twenty.

Maggie Mae looked up.

"Charlie," she said slowly. "What's that on the curtains?"

The curtains were dotted with ladybirds. So was the ceiling. So was the teapot. So, now that she looked, was the tip of Maggie Mae's own nose.

"Oh!" said Charlie. "They must have been in the leaves! Asleep! And the warm woke them up!"

She was quite right. Ladybirds like to spend the winter tucked up together, deep in the leaf piles where it is snug and still. These ones had felt the fire, and thought it must already be spring, and climbed out in a little red river, waving their feelers as if to say, "Good morning! Is it time?"

There were hundreds of them.

They walked across the table. They walked across the name-cards. One of them walked very slowly across the word MAGNIFICENT on Maggie Mae's list, and sat down in the middle of it.

Maggie Mae stood very still in the middle of her kitchen, with a crown of leaves on her head and a ladybird on her nose.

"Are you cross?" said Charlie in a small voice.

Maggie Mae took a slow breath.

"No, sugar," she said. "I'm just thinking."

But her emerald eyes were very bright, and she was holding her pencil so tightly that it creaked.


Chapter Five — The Teacup Tower

Maggie Mae did not sleep well that night.

She lay in bed and thought about the list. There were still twenty-six things on it. The cake had to be baked again. The name-cards had ladybird footprints on them. Every so often, a ladybird flew past her nose in the dark.

And not one of her friends had sent word that they were coming.

On the morning of the party she rose before the sun and went straight to work. She baked a new apple-spice cake, exactly, with one spoon of rising powder, and it came out perfect. She scrubbed and polished. She carried the ladybirds out to the woodpile a pawful at a time, where there was a dry, leafy corner for them to sleep in. Most of them went. Some of them came back.

By the time Charlie arrived, bright and early and ready to help, Maggie Mae had been working for hours, and she looked it. Her bow was crooked. There was flour on her ear. She was talking to herself.

Charlie watched her hurry from the kitchen to the table with one teacup, and back to the kitchen, and back to the table with another, and back again.

That's a lot of walking, thought Charlie. Maggie Mae looks so tired. If I carried all the cups at once, she wouldn't have to do all that walking. She could sit down.

It was a kind thought. It was the kindest thought Charlie had all day.

When Maggie Mae went out to the garden for more roses, Charlie climbed onto a chair and began, very carefully, to build a tower. One cup. One saucer. Another cup on top. Another saucer. It grew taller than Charlie, and then taller than the chair, and at the very top she put the best cup of all: the little pink teacup with the gold rim that had belonged to Maggie Mae's grandmother.

Then she lifted the whole tower in both arms and set off across the kitchen.

It went beautifully for four steps.

On the fifth step, the tower began to lean. Charlie leaned the other way. The tower leaned back. Charlie hopped. The tower swayed like a tall tree in a wind.

Maggie Mae came through the door with her arms full of roses, saw it, dropped the roses, and dived. She caught the tower. All of it.

All except the little pink cup at the very top, which sailed off sideways, turned over once in the air, and landed on the stone floor with a small, sad clink.

Its handle came off.

There was a silence in the kitchen.

Maggie Mae looked at the teacup. She looked at the list, with all its things still unticked. She looked at the roses all over the floor, and the flour on her own paws, and the ladybird sitting on the new cake.

"Charlie," she said, and her voice came out higher and sharper than she had ever heard it. "Please. Just stop. Stop helping. I need to do it myself. Please just go home."

The words were out before she could catch them.

Charlie stood perfectly still.

She did not cry. She did not argue. Her whiskers went down, and her ears went down, and her tail went down. She untied her apron, very carefully, and folded it, and put it on the chair.

"Sorry, Maggie Mae," she said, so quietly that it was barely a word.

Then she went out of the door, and closed it gently behind her.


Chapter Six — The Quiet Cottage

The cottage was very quiet after Charlie had gone.

Maggie Mae worked. She glued the teacup handle and set the cup on the windowsill to dry. She picked up the roses and arranged them so that nobody would notice the bruised ones. She laid the table with her best white cloth and set the perfect cake in the very middle of it on her grandmother's glass stand.

By the time the sun was high, everything was done.

The cottage looked beautiful. It looked exactly like the picture she had held in her head for weeks. Every cup sat in its saucer. Every napkin was folded into a little fan.

She ticked the last thing off the list and stood back.

It was very tidy, and very still, and very, very quiet. There was nobody bouncing on their toes. There was nobody asking whether lists had a smell.

Maggie Mae sat down at her perfect table and found that she did not want to look at it.

The folded apron was still on the chair where Charlie had left it. Maggie Mae picked it up to hang it on its hook, and as she did, something small fell out of the front pocket onto the floor.

It was a little card. It was crumpled at the corners, and slightly damp, the way things are when they have been carried in a chipmunk's cheek. The writing on it was large and wobbly and went slightly uphill, and it said:

For Maggie Mae. The best big sister in the whole wide world. Love from your assistant, Charlie.

Underneath there was a drawing of a skunk with a very large bow and a chipmunk with a very large smile, holding paws, in front of a cake that went all the way up off the top of the card.

Maggie Mae looked at it for a long time.

Charlie must have made it in secret, and carried it about all morning, waiting for the right moment.

And instead of the right moment, she had been sent home.

Maggie Mae stood up. She did not look in the mirror. She did not straighten her bow, or check the cake.

She put the card very carefully in her own pocket, and she went to find Charlie.


Chapter Seven — On Charlie's Doorstep

Charlie's family lived in a snug burrow under the roots of a big beech at the edge of the Hickory Woods, with a round green door and a boot-scraper shaped like an acorn. Charlie was sitting on the doorstep with her knees pulled up and her chin on them.

She saw Maggie Mae coming and looked away.

Maggie Mae sat down on the step beside her. It was a small step, and she was a fluffy skunk, and her tail had to go round the boot-scraper.

For a while, neither of them spoke.

"I found your card," said Maggie Mae at last. "In your apron."

Charlie did not look up.

"I wasn't trying to break things," she said. "I was trying to help. I carried all the cups so you wouldn't have to keep walking. You looked so tired."

Maggie Mae's throat went tight.

"I know you were," she said. "I didn't see it then. I only saw the cup."

"When you said go home," said Charlie, very quietly, "it felt like I was too little and too messy to be your friend. Like I wasn't in the picture."

Maggie Mae closed her eyes for a moment.

"Oh, Charlie," she said. "I'm so sorry. I spoke to you sharply, and you didn't deserve it. I wanted everything to be perfect so badly that I forgot the most important part of a party is the folk who come to it. And you were the first one there."

Charlie sniffed.

"I did put too much rising powder in," she said. "And bring the ladybirds. And build the tower too tall. Those were my fault. I should have asked first. Especially the tower."

"Then we were both a little wrong," said Maggie Mae. "And we can both be sorry. And then we can both be done being sorry, because I need my assistant back. I don't think I can have a party without you."

Charlie looked up. Her blue eyes were shining.

Then she threw her arms round Maggie Mae's neck and buried her face in the soft fur, and Maggie Mae held her very tight, and neither of them said anything for quite a long time.

"What time is it?" said Charlie at last, sitting back and wiping her nose.

Maggie Mae looked at the sun.

"Nearly time," she said. "The party starts at three."

Charlie went suddenly very still. She put a paw up to her cheek, as if remembering something.

"Maggie Mae," she said slowly. "When I took the invitations out of my cheeks, they were a bit damp. And a bit crumply. And Barney said something about free o'clock."

"Free o'clock?"

"I think," said Charlie, in a very small voice, "the writing might have run."

There was a pause.

Maggie Mae thought of her list, and three o'clock exactly, and the perfect table, and the perfect cake, and the guests arriving all at once and in order, which was clearly not going to happen.

And for the first time since she had pinned up her list, she laughed. She laughed so hard that she had to hold on to the boot-scraper.

"Well," she gasped. "I suppose they'll come when they come."


Chapter Eight — Right on Time

It turned out to be the strangest party that Hickory Hollow had ever seen.

Brody arrived first, at half past two, because his card had not said when and he had wanted to be sure. Maggie Mae met him at the rose arch.

"Brody! Right on time," she said warmly. "And you're exactly who I needed. Would you carry the table outside? It's far too lovely a day to be indoors."

Brody beamed and carried the table out under the rose arch as if it weighed nothing.

Barney arrived at three, because free o'clock was close enough, and brought Benny, who had brought a small measuring stick in case the cake needed measuring. "Right on time," said Maggie Mae, and gave Barney the chewy squares of the first cake, because she had a feeling he would like them best. He did.

Maverick arrived at a quarter past three, swooping down over the rose arch with his white head gleaming. "Eagles are never late," he announced, landing on the gatepost. Then he noticed that most of the guests were not there yet, and added, "Exactly on time for a fashionable eagle." Maggie Mae had saved him the gatepost specially, with a cushion on it, and he was enormously pleased.

Reba arrived at four. Her card had said cake, and she had stopped reading there. "Right on time," said Maggie Mae, and gave her a honey jar all of her own, with her name on the lid.

Sadie Belle arrived at three with a pot of chrysanthemums. Rusty arrived at half past four, by a shortcut through the rose bed, and apologised to the roses.

Beau arrived at five, on the right day, because he had decided at the last minute that it probably was. He bounded over the garden wall and landed in the leaf pile, and a small cloud of sleepy ladybirds rose round him like sparks. Sadie Belle laughed, and went pink, and helped him brush them off.

And Great-Grandpappy Buck arrived as the sun was going down, slowly, with his walking-stick and his banjo. His card had smudged so badly that it only said Do come. So he had. Maggie Mae had saved him the big garden chair with a cushion and the quilt from her own bed, and a cup of hot cordial waiting, and he sat down in it with a long, grateful sigh.

Because everybody came at a different time, the party never really started and never really stopped. It just went on, all through the long golden afternoon, getting fuller and happier with every new arrival. The perfect cake was eaten. The chewy squares were eaten too, and Barney said they were his favourite. Nobody used the name-cards, because everybody sat wherever they liked. There was a ladybird in Buck's cordial, which he lifted out gently on a spoon and set on a rose, and another on Benny's measuring stick, which he measured.

Then Barney taught everybody a dance he had made up that morning, which he called the Free O'Clock Shuffle. It was mostly hopping, with a spin and a bow, and then more hopping. Brody danced it so heartily that the garden bench fell over. Maverick refused to dance it, because eagles do not hop, and was later seen hopping behind the rose arch when he thought nobody was looking.

Maggie Mae danced it too, with Charlie, round and round the lawn, until her bow came undone and her tail was full of leaves.

She did not look at her list once.


Chapter Nine — The Teacup

When the sun had gone down behind Weather Ridge, Great-Grandpappy Buck lit the glass lanterns in the rose arch, one by one, with a long taper, and the garden glowed.

Everybody was tired and full of cake and happy in that slow, warm, crumbly way that comes at the end of a good party. Rusty and Barney were having a whispering contest, which Barney was losing, because Barney cannot whisper. Maverick was teaching Beau how to fluff up his feathers to stay warm, and Beau, who had no feathers, was fluffing up his fur instead and looking very pleased with the result.

Charlie went into the kitchen and came out holding something carefully in both paws.

It was the little pink teacup. The glue had dried. The handle was back in its place, with a thin line round it where the break had been.

"It's mended," said Charlie. "You can hardly see."

Maggie Mae took it and turned it in the lantern light.

"You can see a little," she said. "Right there."

Charlie's face fell.

"No, that's the best part," said Maggie Mae, and she held it up so that everybody could see the thin dark line. "My grandmother used to say that every mended thing has a story in it. This one has ours now. The cake that grew. The ladybirds. The invitations. The tower." She smiled at Charlie. "Every time I drink from it, I'll remember today."

"Can we use it now?" said Charlie.

So Maggie Mae poured a little warm cordial into the mended cup, and they passed it round the circle, everybody taking one small sip, very carefully, holding it by the bowl and not the handle. When it came to Great-Grandpappy Buck, he held it for a long moment in his old paws, looking at the thin line in the lamplight.

"That's a fine cup," he said. "A cup with a story in it."

Then he handed it back, and laid his banjo across his knees, and the garden went quiet, because everybody knew what that meant.

Maggie Mae sat down on the step of Rose Arch Cottage, and Charlie climbed up beside her and leaned against her side.

"Grandpappy," said Charlie. "Was it a magnificent party?"

Buck looked round the lantern-lit garden, at the empty plates and the crooked chairs, the leaves in everybody's fur, the ladybirds asleep in the roses, and the young friends tumbled together in the soft autumn dusk.

"Little one," he said, "it was the most magnificent party I ever came to."


Chapter Ten — How You Made Them Feel

Buck began to play.

It was not a dancing tune. The dancing was done. It was a slow, soft, rocking sort of tune, the kind that seems to fold itself round you like a shawl. It went up a little, and came down a little, and turned round again, like a leaf on slow water.

A late moth came and bumped gently against one of the lanterns and went away again. Over Fern Meadow, the first stars came out.

When the tune was over, Buck rested his paws on the strings.

"I've been to a great many parties, young'uns," he said in his low, slow voice. "I couldn't tell you what was on one single table." He looked at Maggie Mae, and his old eyes crinkled. "Nobody remembers what you owned, my dear. Everybody remembers how you made them feel."

Maggie Mae did not answer. She only held Charlie a little closer.

Then, one by one, the guests began to go home.

That was the last part of Maggie Mae's party, and she found she liked it nearly best of all. She stood at the rose arch and said goodnight to every one of them. She gave Brody the last of the cake to take home in his rucksack. She tucked a rose behind Sadie Belle's ear. She told Maverick the gatepost would always be his. Barney did the Free O'Clock Shuffle all the way to the gate. Rusty set off by a shortcut, and came back, and joined the others.

Great-Grandpappy Buck went last, slowly, with his banjo on his back and one of the glass lanterns swinging from his paw, to light the way for Reba and Beau and Rusty and the beaver brothers, whose homes were on his path, with Brody and Sadie Belle and Maverick going along with them as far as the bridge. At the gate he turned and touched his cap.

"Thank you for a lovely evening, Miss Maggie Mae," he said.

And then Maggie Mae and Charlie were alone in the lantern-lit garden.

They did the washing-up together. Maggie Mae washed, and Charlie dried, standing on a chair, one cup at a time, which took a great deal longer than doing it all at once, and neither of them minded.

When it was done, Maggie Mae walked Charlie home through the dark to the round green door under the beech, and Charlie's mother opened it with a candle in her paw, and Charlie, who was so sleepy she could hardly stand, hugged Maggie Mae goodnight.

"Same again next year?" she mumbled.

"Same again next year," said Maggie Mae. "Assistant."

Before he left, Buck had put out the lanterns in the rose arch one by one, and left a lamp burning safely in Maggie Mae's front window. So Maggie Mae walked back home under the stars, only a short way along the edge of the meadow, with that one warm window glowing ahead of her all the way to her gate, as if the cottage were waiting up for her.

The kitchen was quiet and warm. The fire had burned down to a soft red glow. On the windowsill, in the last of the firelight, the little pink teacup stood with its mended handle, and on the wall beside it hung the list, with all forty-one things ticked off.

Maggie Mae looked at the list for a moment.

Then she took it down, folded it in half, and tucked Charlie's card in front of it, where she would see it every morning.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
