Chapter One — The Champion of Winter

The snow came again in the middle of December, deep and soft and perfect, and on the morning after it fell, Beau the bobcat stood on top of the biggest snowdrift in Fern Meadow and made an announcement.

"Friends! Neighbours! Hickory Hollow!" he cried, with his tufted ears up and his golden-yellow neckerchief flapping in the breeze. "I have something to tell you!"

Several heads turned. Barney stopped making a snow-beaver. Charlie popped up out of a drift. Rusty, who had been trotting across the meadow on his way somewhere, sat down to listen with his white-tipped tail curled round his paws.

"I," said Beau, "am the champion of winter."

There was a pause.

"Of what?" said Barney.

"Of everything," said Beau. "Every winter game there is. Snowball throwing. Snow leaping. Sledging. Tracking. Anything. Bobcats are made for snow. Look at my paws. Look at my legs. Look at my magnificent winter coat." He turned round, so that everybody could see how tawny and spotty and thick it was. "I am, without any doubt, the best at winter in the whole of Hickory Hollow."

Beau was a young bobcat with bright white markings, black ear tufts, brilliant blue eyes, a forest-green vest and that golden-yellow neckerchief. He was athletic and daring and loyal and warm-hearted, and almost everybody in the Hollow was very fond of him. He was also, now and then, a little bit clueless. And this morning, standing on top of a snowdrift with the sun on his whiskers, he was feeling more magnificent than he had ever felt in his life.

"Prove it," said Rusty, grinning.

Beau's eyes lit up.

"I will!" he cried. "On Saturday! The Great Winter Games of Hickory Hollow! Everybody can enter. Four events. Snowball throwing, snow leaping, sledging and tracking. And the winner of the most events will be the Champion of Winter." He struck a pose. "Which will be me."

"What if it isn't?" said Rusty.

Beau looked at him with great pity.

"It will be," he said.

At the edge of the meadow, Sadie Belle had stopped on her way to fetch water and was watching with her green eyes very wide. She was a young deer with a pink floral headband and a gentle heart, and she thought, privately, that Beau standing on a snowdrift in the sunshine was the finest thing she had seen all winter.

She did not say so. She never did. She went home and got out her ribbon box instead.

Beau jumped down off the snowdrift, landed beautifully, and slid all the way down the other side on his back.

"Ta-da!" he said, from the bottom, as if it had all been planned.


Chapter Two — Getting Ready

Getting ready for the Great Winter Games took the rest of the week, and everybody helped, whether Beau asked them or not.

Benny measured out the courses. He marked a line in Fern Meadow for the snowball throwing, exactly thirty of Benny's paces from a target. He marked out a run-up and a landing pit of deep, soft snow for the snow leap, and smoothed it flat so that every jump could be measured fairly. "To the nearest whisker," he said, with great satisfaction.

Barney built the snowball target. It was supposed to be a snow-beaver. It ended up looking more like a snow-potato with ears. Barney said that was even better, because it was harder to hit.

Maggie Mae made the prizes. She cut ribbons out of her best red and blue and gold scraps and stitched each one into a neat rosette, with words embroidered in the middle: FIRST. SECOND. THIRD. And one very large gold one, bigger than all the others, that said CHAMPION OF WINTER.

Beau looked at the big gold one for a long time.

"That's mine," he said.

"It's whoever wins," said Maggie Mae.

"Yes," said Beau. "Mine."

Great-Grandpappy Buck and the miller chose the sledging hill. They walked up and down three different slopes before they picked one: the long, gentle hill on the far side of Fern Meadow, with no trees on it, and no rocks, and no creek anywhere near, that ran out at the bottom into a wide flat stretch of soft snow where everything could slow down on its own.

"There," said Buck, leaning on his walking-stick. "That's the one. One at a time down it, and nobody starts till the one before has walked off the bottom."

"What about skating?" said Beau. "I'm brilliant at skating. Can we have skating on the beaver ponds?"

"No," said Buck.

"But the ice looks really thick..."

"It doesn't matter what ice looks like," said Buck. "It only matters what it is. And no youngster can tell that by looking. There'll be no skating on any pond till the grown-ups have checked it and marked a place, and that's not this week." He looked at Beau over his spectacles. "Four events is plenty."

Beau sighed. But he did not argue, because Buck had his firm voice on.

All week, as the courses were made, Beau went about the Hollow telling everybody how he would win.

"I'll throw the snowball so hard it goes right through the target," he told the rabbits.

"I'll leap so far I'll land in next week," he told Reba.

"I'll go down the hill so fast I'll be home before I've started," he told Brody.

And every time, Rusty, who was usually somewhere nearby, said, "We'll see."

On Thursday morning, very early, Maverick happened to be sitting on top of the tallest hickory by Fern Meadow when he saw Beau come out alone to practise.

Beau practised throwing. He threw a snowball at a fence post and missed it by a whole field. "Warming up," he told nobody. He practised leaping, and leapt over the landing pit altogether and landed in a holly bush. "Testing the bush," he said. He practised sledging on a tea tray down the very smallest slope in the meadow, about as high as a rabbit, and the tray went one way and Beau went the other.

"Practice is supposed to go wrong," he announced to the empty meadow, picking snow out of his ear tufts. "That way, on the day, it all goes right."

Up in his hickory, Maverick, who had once said exactly the same thing before landing in a thistle bush, said nothing at all, but fluffed up his feathers thoughtfully, and decided that he would definitely be there on Saturday.

On Friday evening, Sadie Belle came shyly up to Beau on the bridge and held something out to him. It was a little rosette she had made herself, out of pink ribbon, with a small, neat B stitched in the middle.

"For luck," she said. "Tomorrow."

Beau took it and pinned it proudly on his vest.

"I won't need luck," he said cheerfully. "But thank you. It's lovely."

Sadie Belle smiled, and went a little pink, and went home.


Chapter Three — Snowball Throwing

Saturday came cold and bright and sparkling.

By the middle of the morning, half the Hollow had gathered in Fern Meadow. The miller and his wife came, and the rabbit family, and Great-Grandpappy Buck with his folding stool and a flask of hot cordial. Maggie Mae set out her rosettes on a little table. And nearly every youngster in the valley had come to enter.

The first event was snowball throwing.

Everybody lined up at Benny's mark, thirty paces from the snow-potato with ears. Each creature had three throws. You got a point for hitting the target anywhere, and two points for hitting it on the nose.

Brody went first. His throws were enormous and slow, and the first one fell short, and the second one fell short, and the third one hit the snow-potato right on the bottom and knocked off a lump.

"One point," said Benny, writing it down.

Rusty went next. He threw quick and low, and hit the target twice.

Then it was Beau's turn.

He stepped up to the mark. He made a snowball. He made it very big and very round and packed it very hard. He wiggled his shoulders. He looked round at the crowd to make sure they were all watching.

"Here," said Beau, "is how a champion throws."

He drew back his arm, and threw with all his might.

The snowball flew. It flew magnificently. It flew straight and fast and high. It flew right over the top of the snow-potato, and over the end of the meadow, and over the hedge, and disappeared.

There was a faint, far-off plop.

"Too hard," said Beau. "I'll throw softer."

His second snowball he threw so softly that it went about three paces and fell apart in mid-air.

His third snowball went sideways, hit the miller's hat, and knocked it off.

"Sorry!" called Beau.

"No harm done," said the miller, picking up his hat and laughing.

"Zero points," said Benny.

Then it was Charlie's turn.

Charlie could not throw hard. She was the smallest creature there. She made three small snowballs, each one about the size of an acorn, and she did not try to throw them straight. She threw them up, in high, slow loops, like little rainbows. And every one of them came down right on top of the snow-potato's head.

"Six points!" said Benny. "Charlie wins the snowball throwing!"

Charlie squeaked so loudly that the rabbits jumped. Maggie Mae pinned the FIRST rosette on her overalls.

Beau clapped. But only a little bit.

"That was just the first one," he said to Rusty. "The snow leap is my best event."

"We'll see," said Rusty.


Chapter Four — The Snow Leap

The snow leap was simple. You ran along Benny's run-up, and at his mark, you jumped as far as you could into the landing pit of deep, soft snow. Benny measured from the mark to wherever your feet touched down first.

Barney went first, ran very fast, forgot to jump, and ran straight into the pit, and stood up to his ears in snow, laughing.

Sadie Belle went next, with her long legs, and made a graceful, careful leap, and landed neatly on all four hooves.

"Very good," said Benny, measuring. "Four paces and a half."

Rusty jumped five paces exactly.

Then it was Beau.

He walked all the way back to the very start of the run-up, much further back than anybody else, so that everybody would see. He crouched. He wiggled. He stared at the landing pit as if it were the only thing in the world.

Then he ran.

He ran faster than anybody had ever run on Benny's run-up. He hit the mark at full speed. And he leapt.

It was the most beautiful leap anybody in Hickory Hollow had ever seen. Beau sailed through the air with all four legs stretched out and his tail out behind him and his ears flat back, high and long and graceful, over the whole of the landing pit.

He landed right at the very far end of it, nose first, and kept going, head down into the deep soft snow, until all that showed of him was his tail and his back paws, waving in the air.

There was a long, muffled silence from inside the snow.

Then two paws came up, and then a head, covered in snow from ear to ear, spitting.

"How far?" said Beau.

Benny looked at his tape. He looked at Beau. He looked at the tape again.

"Well," he said. "It was the furthest. By a long way. But I measure to where your feet touch down first. And your feet didn't touch down first. Your nose did." He frowned at his rules. "And your feet are still in the air."

"So?"

"So I can't measure it," said Benny. "It doesn't count."

"It DOESN'T COUNT?"

"Rules are rules," said Benny apologetically.

Rusty won the snow leap. Five paces exactly, landing perfectly on all four paws.

Beau brushed the snow out of his ears. He did not clap this time.

"That's not fair," he muttered. "That was the best jump. Everybody saw."

Sadie Belle, beside him, opened her mouth to say that it had been, it had been the most beautiful jump she ever saw. But Beau was already stamping off towards the sledging hill.


Chapter Five — The Sledging and the Tracking

The sledging race was down the long gentle hill, one at a time, on anything you liked. Great-Grandpappy Buck sat at the top on his folding stool with the miller, and said "Go" to each racer, and Benny stood at the bottom with a pocket watch the miller had lent him, timing.

Brody went down on a tea tray, slowly and steadily, beaming all the way.

Reba went down on a big wooden baking board, holding a biscuit in each paw, and did not drop either.

Barney went down on his tummy with no sledge at all, because he said beavers were built for sliding, and it turned out he was right. He went down so fast that he left a long beaver-shaped groove all the way to the bottom.

Then Beau.

He had a proper sledge, a real one, with runners, borrowed from the miller. He lined it up perfectly at the top of the hill. He sat on it. He pushed off.

And for a moment, it was glorious. He shot down the hill, faster and faster, with the wind in his whiskers and the snow spraying up on both sides.

Then the sledge hit a little bump, and spun round.

Beau went the rest of the way down the hill backwards, facing up the slope, with an expression of great surprise. He went past the finish. He went across the wide flat stretch at the bottom. And he came to a very slow, very gentle stop in a soft heap of snow, still sitting on the sledge, still facing the wrong way.

"Barney wins the sledging," said Benny.

"The sledge was wonky," said Beau. "It spun. It's not my fault the sledge spun."

Nobody said anything. Charlie looked at Maggie Mae.

The last event was tracking. Rusty had hidden a red mitten somewhere at the edge of the Hickory Woods, and left a trail of his own footprints in the snow to it, mixed in with lots of other footprints and false trails going every which way. Whoever found the mitten first won.

"Tracking," said Beau, cheering up at once. "Now THIS is a bobcat's game."

They all set off at once.

Beau went fastest. He picked up a trail straight away and followed it at a run, nose to the snow, round a tree, through a thicket, over a log, round another tree, through another thicket, over another log.

He went round and round for quite some time before he looked up and saw that the trail he was following was his own.

By the time he had found Rusty's real trail, Sadie Belle was walking back out of the woods with the red mitten in her mouth.

She had gone slowly. She had looked carefully at every print before she took a step. She had not been in a hurry at all.

"Sadie Belle wins the tracking!" called Benny.

Everybody cheered. Sadie Belle went pink.

And Beau, standing at the edge of the woods with burrs in his fur and snow in his ears, having won not one single thing all day, said something that he had never said before in his life.

He said it loudly, so that everybody heard.

"These games are stupid," he said. "Charlie only won because she's tiny and the target couldn't see her coming. And Benny's rules are silly. And Barney cheated, sliding on his tummy. And Sadie Belle only won because she's slow." He kicked at the snow. "I'm not playing any more."

There was a terrible silence in the meadow.

Charlie's face crumpled.

Beau turned round and walked away into the woods.


Chapter Six — Rusty Follows

It was Rusty who went after him.

He found Beau sitting on a fallen log in a little clearing, with his back to the meadow and his ears flat, pulling burrs out of his tail and throwing them at the snow.

Rusty sat down at the other end of the log, and did not say anything for a while.

"Go away," said Beau.

"No," said Rusty.

They sat. A chickadee landed on a branch above them, looked at them, and decided not to stay.

"I was supposed to win," said Beau at last. "I told everybody. I told the rabbits. I told Reba. I told everybody I was the Champion of Winter. And I didn't win anything." He threw another burr. "Not even one thing. And everybody laughed."

"Everybody laughed when you landed on your nose," said Rusty. "Because it was funny. You were laughing too, the first time. On the snowdrift."

"That was before," said Beau.

Rusty scratched his ear.

"Remember the time I said I knew a shortcut to the Old Mill," he said, "and took everybody through the bramble patch, and we came out at the beaver ponds, and Maggie Mae had a bramble stuck in her bow for a week?"

Beau's mouth twitched, despite himself.

"Everybody laughed at me then," said Rusty. "And it was horrible for about a minute. And then I laughed too. And then it was funny. And now it's one of everybody's favourite stories, and I get to tell it." He looked at Beau. "Being laughed at only hurts while you're the only one not laughing."

Beau did not answer that.

"But that's not the bad bit," said Rusty quietly. "The bad bit's what you said. About Charlie. And Sadie Belle. And Barney."

Beau's ears went down even further.

"Charlie's face went all crumpled," said Rusty. "She won her first ever ribbon, and you said she only won because she's tiny."

"I know," said Beau, very small.

"And Sadie Belle made you that rosette," said Rusty, nodding at the pink ribbon still pinned to Beau's vest, with its little stitched B. "For luck."

Beau looked down at it for a long time.

Rusty stood up.

"I'm going back," he said. "There's cocoa." And he went, leaving Beau alone on the log.

Beau sat for a long while after that.

Then, from somewhere nearby, there came a small sniffing sound.


Chapter Seven — The Littlest Rabbit

It came from the bottom of the sledging hill.

Beau got up and went to look through the trees. And there, at the bottom of the long gentle slope, all on her own, sat the littlest rabbit, holding a tiny wooden tea tray and looking up at the hill.

Everybody else had gone over to the table for cocoa. Nobody had noticed her. Her mother was by the table too, with the other kits, only a little way off, but not looking just then.

Beau went over and sat down beside her in the snow.

"What's the matter?" he said.

The littlest rabbit sniffed.

"I wanted to go down," she said. "In the race. But it's so long. And so high. And everybody went so fast. And I'm scared I'll fall off." She sniffed again. "So I didn't go. And now it's finished."

Beau looked up at the hill. It did not look very long to him. It did not look very high. But then, he was a bobcat, and she was the very smallest rabbit in the Hollow.

He thought about Benny's rules, and the spinning sledge, and landing nose-first in the snow.

"Do you know what," he said slowly. "I fell off three times today."

The littlest rabbit looked up at him.

"Three?"

"I landed on my nose in the snow leap. I went down this hill backwards. And I threw a snowball at the miller's hat." He thought. "That's not falling off, exactly. But it's close."

The littlest rabbit giggled. It was a very small giggle, but it was a giggle.

"Did it hurt?" she asked.

"Not one bit," said Beau. "Snow's soft. That's the best thing about it. You can fall over as much as you like." And then he had an idea. "Would you like me to show you how to fall off properly? So it doesn't matter if you do?"

The littlest rabbit's eyes went very wide.

"There's a properly?"

"Bobcats know all about falling," said Beau. "We do it all the time. We just pretend we meant to."

So he showed her.

First, he showed her on the flat, at the bottom of the hill. How to tuck your chin in and curl up round, like a hedgehog, and roll, so that you landed soft. He did it three times, and she laughed at him. Then she did it, very carefully, and rolled over in the snow, and sat up with snow on her nose, astonished.

"It didn't hurt!"

"Told you," said Beau.

Then he walked a little way up the hill with her, only a very little way, and set her tray down, and held it steady while she sat on it.

"I'll be right here," he said. "At the bottom. I'll catch you if you go too fast. And if you fall off, you just roll."

She went down. It was a very short way, and very slow. Halfway down, she tipped off sideways into the soft snow, and curled up, and rolled, exactly as he had shown her, and sat up at the bottom covered in snow from ear to ear.

And laughed and laughed and laughed.

"AGAIN!" she shouted.


Chapter Eight — The Falling-Over Race

By the time the others came back from the cocoa table, the littlest rabbit had been down the bottom bit of the hill eleven times, and fallen off seven of them, and rolled every time, and was now going down from nearly halfway up, squealing with joy, while Beau ran beside her cheering at the top of his voice.

"Go on! Lean back! LEAN BACK! You're doing it! Oh, that was a brilliant fall! That was the best roll I ever saw! AGAIN!"

Everybody stopped and stared.

The littlest rabbit's mother put her paw to her mouth. Great-Grandpappy Buck, on his stool, leaned forward on his walking-stick, and said nothing, but his eyes crinkled up behind his spectacles.

At the bottom of the hill, the littlest rabbit rolled to a stop, and sat up, and saw her mother, and shouted, "MAMA! I WENT DOWN THE BIG HILL! I FELL OFF SEVEN TIMES! BEAU TAUGHT ME HOW!"

Beau looked round. He saw everybody watching. He saw Charlie, standing beside Maggie Mae with her FIRST rosette, not looking at him.

He took a deep breath, and walked across the snow to her.

"Charlie," he said. "I'm sorry for what I said. You didn't win because you're tiny. You won because you threw them up high, like rainbows, and that was clever, and I never thought of it." He swallowed. "You were the best at snowballs. Properly."

Charlie looked at him for a moment. Then she smiled, a little.

"You threw one at the miller's hat," she said.

"I did," said Beau. "That was terrible."

Then he went to Barney, and told him sliding on his tummy was not cheating, it was brilliant, and that he wanted to learn. And he went to Benny and said the rules were fair, actually, even the nose one. And then he went to Sadie Belle, last of all, and stood in front of her, and looked at his paws.

"You weren't slow," he said. "You were careful. I went round in a circle following myself. You looked at every single print." He touched the little pink rosette on his vest. "Thank you for this. I'm sorry I said I didn't need luck. I needed loads."

Sadie Belle went so pink under her headband that she had to look at the snow.

Then Beau turned round to everybody, and grinned, and the grin was back, the real one.

"I've got an idea for one more event," he said. "A fifth one. The Falling-Over Race."

"The what?" said Benny.

"Everybody goes down the hill," said Beau. "And the winner is whoever falls off the funniest. And rolls the best." He looked at the littlest rabbit. "She's the judge. She's the expert."

So that was what they did. Everybody went down the hill, one at a time, and fell off on purpose. Brody fell off like a sack of flour. Reba fell off holding a biscuit and still did not drop it. Maverick, who had been watching from a fence post all day, came down on a tray and fell off so dramatically, with so much flapping, that he ended up upside down in a drift with his legs in the air, and the littlest rabbit laughed so hard she fell over too, without a tray at all.

Beau went last. He went down backwards, on purpose, waving, and fell off at the bottom, and rolled three times, and stood up, and bowed.

The littlest rabbit thought about it very seriously.

"Beau wins," she said. "Because he's had the most practice."


Chapter Nine — The Rosettes

The sun was going down behind Weather Ridge when Maggie Mae gave out the rosettes.

She did it properly, standing beside her little table in the gold light, with everybody gathered round.

Charlie got FIRST for the snowball throwing, and Rusty got FIRST for the snow leap, and Barney got FIRST for the sledging, and Sadie Belle got FIRST for the tracking. Everybody cheered for each one, and Beau cheered loudest of all.

Then Maggie Mae held up the big gold rosette. CHAMPION OF WINTER.

There was a pause.

"Well," said Maggie Mae. "Nobody won more than one event. So nobody's the Champion. I suppose it stays in the box."

"No," said a small voice.

It was the littlest rabbit. She had climbed up onto the table, which she was not supposed to do, and she was holding out her paw for the gold rosette.

Maggie Mae gave it to her, puzzled.

The littlest rabbit hopped down, and walked across the snow, and held it up to Beau.

"You can't have Champion of Winter," she said seriously. "Because you didn't win anything." She thought. "Except falling over. So you can have this instead."

She had turned it over. On the back, in Maggie Mae's neat stitching, nobody had embroidered anything at all. It was plain gold.

"It doesn't say anything," said Beau.

"I'll tell you what it says," said the littlest rabbit. "It says Best Cheerer."

Beau looked at the plain gold rosette. Then he knelt down in the snow so that she could pin it on his vest, next to Sadie Belle's little pink one with the B. She did it very carefully, with her tongue sticking out, and stuck the pin in his fur twice, and he did not say a word.

When she had finished, he stood up, and looked down at the two rosettes side by side, and found that he had never in his whole life been so pleased with anything.

"Best Cheerer," he said. "That's better than Champion."

"Much better," said Rusty, beside him. "Champions have to be good at things. Cheerers just have to be nice."

"I'm good at being nice," said Beau.

"Sometimes," said Rusty, and Beau laughed, and tumbled backwards into a snowdrift, and pulled Rusty in after him, and they both lay there in the snow, laughing.


Chapter Ten — Where the Ground Is

That evening, they all went up to Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, because the snow was glowing blue in the dusk, and the stars were coming out over Fern Meadow, and nobody wanted the day to end quite yet.

There was hot cocoa, and Reba's biscuits, and the lamp lit by the door. Everybody was tired and wet round the edges and very happy. Beau sat on the top step with both rosettes on his vest. The littlest rabbit sat beside him with her mother, and kept showing everybody how to roll, until her mother made her sit still.

Buck settled into his rocking chair with a long sigh.

"I watched the whole of the games from my stool," he said. "I'll be honest with you. It was the best afternoon I've had all winter."

"Even though I didn't win anything?" said Beau.

"Especially that," said Buck, and his whiskers twitched. "When I was a young beaver, I was the best sledger in the Hollow. Ask anybody my age. Very proud of it, I was. And one winter I went down the steep hill behind the Sugar Grove, showing off, and came off at the bottom and went head first into a snowbank so deep that my father had to pull me out by my tail." He chuckled. "Never forgot it. Learned more from that snowbank than from all the races I ever won."

He rocked slowly for a while, looking out at the blue snow.

"Falling down is how you learn where the ground is, young'uns," he said.

Beau thought about that. He thought about landing on his nose, and the spinning sledge, and the littlest rabbit rolling in the snow at the bottom of the hill, laughing.

Then the littlest rabbit's mother stood up, because it was bedtime, and the littlest rabbit was yawning so widely that you could see all her teeth.

"Thank you, Beau," said her mother quietly. "She's been frightened of that hill all winter."

"She was brilliant," said Beau.

The rabbits set off down the porch steps for home, with their mother's lantern swinging ahead of them. The littlest one went last. At the bottom of the steps she stopped, and turned, and curled up in the snow, and rolled, once, perfectly, and stood up again, and waved.

Beau waved back. He watched the lantern bobbing away along the creek path until it went round the bend and was gone.

Then he sat down on the top step again, next to Sadie Belle, with his chin on his paws and his two rosettes glowing faintly in the lamplight, one pink and one plain gold, and watched the stars come out over the meadow where the sledging hill lay quiet and blue under the snow, with a hundred little roll-marks at the bottom of it.

"Sadie Belle," he said sleepily. "Next year. Will you teach me to track properly? Slowly?"

Sadie Belle looked at him. Then she looked at the stars.

"Yes," she said softly. "I'd like that."

And neither of them said anything else, for a long, warm, quiet while, while the lamp burned low by the door and the snow went on glowing in the dark.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
