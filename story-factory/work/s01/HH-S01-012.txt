Chapter One — The Champion of Winter

The snow came again in the middle of December, deep and soft, and then the weather turned mild, sitting just at the edge of freezing, which is exactly when snow is best for packing. On the Monday morning after it fell, Beau the bobcat stood on top of the biggest snowdrift in Fern Meadow and made an announcement.

"Friends! Neighbours! Hickory Hollow!" he cried, with his tufted ears up and his golden-yellow neckerchief flapping in the breeze. "I have something to tell you!"

Several heads turned. Barney stopped making a snow-beaver. Charlie popped up out of a drift. Rusty, who had been trotting across the meadow on his way somewhere, sat down to listen with his white-tipped tail curled round his paws.

"I," said Beau, "am the champion of winter."

There was a pause.

"Of what?" said Barney.

"Of everything," said Beau. "Every winter game there is. Snowball throwing. Snow leaping. Sledging. Tracking. Anything. Bobcats are made for snow. Look at my paws. Look at my legs. Look at my magnificent winter coat." He turned round, so that everybody could see how tawny and spotty and thick it was. "I am, without any doubt, the best at winter in the whole of Hickory Hollow."

Beau was a young bobcat with bright white markings, black ear tufts, brilliant blue eyes, a forest-green vest and that golden-yellow neckerchief. He was athletic and daring and loyal and warm-hearted, and almost everybody in the Hollow was very fond of him. He was also, now and then, a little bit clueless. And this morning, standing on top of a snowdrift with the sun on his whiskers, he was feeling more magnificent than he had ever felt in his life.

"Prove it," said Rusty, with a twitch of his whiskers.

Beau's ear tufts shot straight up.

"I will!" he cried. "On Saturday! The Great Winter Games of Hickory Hollow! Everybody can enter. Four events. Snowball throwing, snow leaping, sledging and tracking. And the winner of the most events will be the Champion of Winter." He struck a pose. "Which will be me."

"What if it isn't?" said Rusty.

Beau looked at him with great pity.

"It will be," he said.

At the edge of the meadow, Sadie Belle had stopped on her way to fetch water and was watching with her green eyes very wide. She was a young deer with a pink floral headband and a gentle heart, and she thought, privately, that Beau standing on a snowdrift in the sunshine was the finest thing she had seen all winter.

She did not say so. She never did. She went home and got out her ribbon box instead.

Beau jumped down off the snowdrift, landed beautifully, and slid all the way down the other side on his back.

"Ta-da!" he said, from the bottom.

"Ta-da," said Rusty politely.


Chapter Two — Getting Ready

Getting ready for the Great Winter Games took the rest of the week, and everybody helped, whether Beau asked them or not.

Benny measured out the courses. He marked a line in Fern Meadow for the snowball throwing, exactly twenty of Benny's paces from a target, and a second, nearer mark for anybody smaller than a rabbit, because that was only fair. He marked out a run-up and a landing pit of deep, soft snow for the snow leap, and smoothed it flat, and made a long measuring string with a knot tied in it at every length of his own flat tail, which he measured against it very carefully, so that every jump could be measured fairly. "To the nearest knot," he said, with great satisfaction.

Barney built the snowball target. It was supposed to be a snow-beaver. It ended up looking more like a snow-potato with ears. Barney said that was even better, because it was harder to hit.

Maggie Mae made the prizes. She cut ribbons out of her best red and blue scraps and stitched them into four neat rosettes, one for the winner of each event, with FIRST embroidered in the middle of every one. And one very large gold one, bigger than all the others, with CHAMPION OF WINTER on the front. They would all be given out at the end of the day, she said, properly, at her little table.

Beau looked at the big gold one for a long time.

"That's mine," he said.

"It's whoever wins," said Maggie Mae.

"Yes," said Beau. "Mine."

Great-Grandpappy Buck and the miller chose the sledging hill. They walked up and down three different slopes before they picked one: the long, gentle hill on the far side of Fern Meadow, with no trees on it, and no rocks, and no creek anywhere near, that ran out at the bottom into a wide flat stretch of soft snow where everything could slow down on its own.

"There," said Buck, leaning on his walking-stick. "That's the one. One at a time down it, and nobody starts till the one before has walked off the bottom."

"What about skating?" said Beau. "I'm brilliant at skating. Can we have skating on the beaver ponds?"

"No," said Buck.

"But the ice looks really thick..."

"It doesn't matter what ice looks like," said Buck. "It only matters what it is. And no youngster can tell that by looking. There'll be no skating on any pond till the grown-ups have checked it and marked a place, and that's not this week." He looked at Beau over his spectacles. "Four events is plenty."

Beau sighed. But he did not argue, because Buck had his firm voice on.

All week, as the courses were made, Beau went about the Hollow telling everybody how he would win.

"I'll throw the snowball so hard it goes right through the target," he told the rabbits.

"I'll leap so far I'll land in next week," he told Reba.

"I'll go down the hill so fast I'll be home before I've started," he told Brody.

And every time, Rusty, who was usually somewhere nearby, said, "We'll see."

On Thursday morning, very early, Maverick happened to be sitting on top of the tallest hickory by Fern Meadow when he saw Beau come out alone to practise.

Beau practised throwing. He threw a snowball at a fence post and missed it by a whole field. "Warming up," he told nobody. He practised leaping, and leapt over the landing pit altogether and landed in the snow-heaped hedge behind it. "Ow," he said, though it was mostly snow. He practised sledging on a tea tray down the very smallest slope in the meadow, about as high as a rabbit, and the tray went one way and Beau went the other.

"Practice is supposed to go wrong," he announced to the empty meadow, picking snow out of his ear tufts. "That way, on the day, it all goes right."

Up in his hickory, Maverick, who knew a great deal about landings that did not go to plan, said nothing at all, but fluffed up his feathers thoughtfully, and decided that he would definitely be there on Saturday. Somebody, he thought, ought to keep an eye on things from up high.

On Friday evening, Sadie Belle came shyly up to Beau on the bridge and held something out to him. It was a little rosette she had made herself, out of pink ribbon, with a small, neat B stitched in the middle.

"For luck," she said. "Tomorrow."

Beau took it and pinned it proudly on his vest.

"I won't need luck," he said cheerfully. "But thank you. It's lovely."

Sadie Belle smiled, and went home.


Chapter Three — Snowball Throwing

Saturday came bright and mild, with the snow still just damp enough to pack.

By the middle of the morning, half the Hollow had gathered in Fern Meadow. The miller and his wife came, and the rabbit family, and Great-Grandpappy Buck with his folding stool and a flask of hot cordial. Maggie Mae set out her rosettes on a little table. And nearly every youngster in the valley had come to enter. Benny put all the watchers well behind the throwing line, where Buck said they ought to be.

The first event was snowball throwing.

Everybody lined up at Benny's mark, twenty paces from the snow-potato with ears. Each creature had three throws. You got a point for hitting the target anywhere, and two points for hitting it on the head.

Brody went first. His throws were enormous and slow, and the first one fell short, and the second one fell short, and the third one hit the snow-potato right on the bottom and knocked off a lump.

"One point," said Benny, writing it down.

Rusty went next. He threw quick and low, and hit the target twice, both times on the body.

"Two points," said Benny.

Then it was Beau's turn.

He stepped up to the mark. He made a snowball. He made it very big and very round. He wiggled his shoulders. He looked round at the crowd to make sure they were all watching.

"Here," said Beau, "is how a champion throws."

He drew back his arm, and threw with all his might.

The snowball flew. It flew magnificently. It flew straight and fast and high. It flew right over the top of the snow-potato, and over the end of the meadow, and over the hedge, and disappeared.

There was a faint, far-off plop.

"Too hard," said Beau. "I'll throw softer."

His second snowball he threw so softly that it went about three paces and fell apart in mid-air.

His third snowball went straight up, very high, came down behind him over the heads of the watchers, and knocked the miller's hat clean off.

"Sorry!" called Beau.

"No harm done," said the miller, picking up his hat and laughing.

"Zero points," said Benny.

Then it was Charlie's turn.

Charlie could not throw hard. She was the smallest creature there, so she stood at Benny's nearer mark. She made three small snowballs, each one about the size of a walnut, and she did not try to throw them straight. She threw them up, in high, slow loops, like little rainbows. And every one of them came down right on top of the snow-potato's head.

"Three on the head! Six points!" said Benny. "Charlie wins the snowball throwing!"

Charlie squeaked so loudly that the rabbits jumped.

Beau clapped. But only a little bit.

"That was just the first one," he said to Rusty. "The snow leap is my best event."

"We'll see," said Rusty.


Chapter Four — The Snow Leap

The snow leap was simple. You ran along Benny's run-up, and at his mark, you jumped as far as you could into the landing pit of deep, soft snow. Benny measured from the mark to wherever your feet touched down first.

Barney went first, ran very fast, forgot to jump, and ran straight into the pit, and stood up to his ears in snow, laughing.

Sadie Belle went next, with her long legs, and made a graceful, careful leap, and landed neatly on all four hooves.

"Very good," said Benny, measuring with his string. "Nine knots and a half."

Rusty jumped ten knots exactly.

Then it was Beau.

He walked all the way back to the very start of the run-up, much further back than anybody else, so that everybody would see. He crouched. He wiggled. He stared at the landing pit as if it were the only thing in the world.

Then he ran.

He ran faster than anybody had ever run on Benny's run-up. He hit the mark at full speed. And he leapt.

It was the most beautiful leap anybody in Hickory Hollow had ever seen. Beau sailed through the air with all four legs stretched out and his tail out behind him and his ears flat back, high and long and graceful, right across the landing pit.

He came down at the very far end of it, nose first, and slid in up to his shoulders, so that all that showed of him was his back half and his tail, waving.

"Is it a record?" said a muffled voice from inside the snow, at once.

Then two paws came up, and then a head, covered in snow from ear to ear.

"How far?" said Beau.

Benny looked at his string. He looked at Beau. He looked at the string again.

"Well," he said. "It was the furthest. By a long way. But I measure to where your feet touch down first. And your feet didn't touch down first. Your nose did." He frowned at his rules. "And your back feet are still in the air."

"So?"

"So I can't measure it," said Benny. "It doesn't count."

"It DOESN'T COUNT?"

"Rules are rules," said Benny apologetically.

Rusty won the snow leap. Ten knots exactly, landing perfectly on all four paws.

Beau brushed the snow out of his ears. He did not clap this time.

"That's not fair," he muttered. "That was the best jump. Everybody saw."

Sadie Belle, beside him, opened her mouth to say that it had been, it had been the most beautiful jump she ever saw. But Beau was already stamping off towards the sledging hill.


Chapter Five — The Sledging and the Tracking

The sledging race was down the long gentle hill, one at a time, on anything you liked. Great-Grandpappy Buck sat at the top on his folding stool with the miller, and said "Go" to each racer, and Benny stood at the bottom with a pocket watch the miller had lent him, timing.

Brody went down on a tea tray, slowly and steadily, beaming all the way.

Reba went down on a big wooden baking board, holding a biscuit in each paw, and did not drop either.

Barney went down on his tummy with no sledge at all, because he said beavers were built for sliding. Whether they were or not, his broad flat tail made a very good rudder, and he went down so fast that he left a long beaver-shaped groove all the way to the bottom.

Then Beau.

He had a proper sledge, a real one, with runners, borrowed from the miller. He lined it up perfectly at the top of the hill. He sat on it. He pushed off.

And for a moment, it was glorious. He shot down the hill, faster and faster, with the wind in his whiskers and the snow spraying up on both sides.

Then the sledge hit a little bump, spun round, and slewed sideways.

Beau went the last part of the way backwards, facing up the slope, with an expression of great surprise, slower and slower and slower, until the sledge came to a very gentle stop in a soft heap of snow, still a whole sledge-length short of Benny's finish line, with Beau still sitting on it, still facing the wrong way.

"Barney wins the sledging," said Benny.

"The sledge was wonky," said Beau. "It spun. It's not my fault the sledge spun."

Charlie looked at Maggie Mae, and Maggie Mae looked at the sky.

The last event was tracking. Rusty had hidden a blue mitten somewhere at the edge of the Hickory Woods, and left a trail of his own footprints in the snow to it, mixed in with lots of other footprints and false trails going every which way. Whoever found the mitten first won.

"Tracking," said Beau, cheering up at once. "Now THIS is a bobcat's game."

They all set off at once.

Beau went fastest. He picked up a trail straight away, a wide, clear, important-looking trail, and followed it at a run, nose to the snow, out of the woods, along the hedge, round the end of the meadow, faster and faster, certain he was nearly there.

The trail stopped at the cocoa table.

The miller's wife looked down at him over the big cocoa pot she had carried there that morning, along exactly that path, and somewhere along the way had dropped one of her red mittens, which she was still looking for.

"Lost, dear?" she said kindly, and handed him a biscuit.

By the time he had run all the way back and found Rusty's real trail, Sadie Belle was walking back out of the woods with the blue mitten in her mouth.

She had gone slowly. She had looked carefully at every print before she took a step. She had not been in a hurry at all.

"Sadie Belle wins the tracking!" called Benny.

Everybody cheered. Sadie Belle looked at her hooves.

And Beau, standing at the edge of the woods with burrs in his fur and snow in his ears, having won not one single thing all day, said something that he had never said before in his life.

He said it loudly, so that everybody heard.

"These games are no good," he said. "Charlie only won because she's tiny and the target couldn't see her coming. And Benny's rules are silly, and Barney only won by lying on his tummy. And Sadie Belle only won because she's slow." He kicked at the snow. "I'm not playing any more."

There was a terrible silence in the meadow.

Charlie's face crumpled.

Beau turned round and walked away into the woods.


Chapter Six — Rusty Follows

It was Rusty who went after him.

He found Beau sitting on a fallen log in a little clearing, with his back to the meadow and his ears flat, pulling burrs out of his tail and throwing them at the snow.

Rusty sat down at the other end of the log, and did not say anything for a while.

"Go away," said Beau.

"No," said Rusty.

They sat. A chickadee landed on a branch above them, looked at them, and decided not to stay.

"I was supposed to win," said Beau at last. "I told everybody. I told the rabbits. I told Reba. I told everybody I was the Champion of Winter. And I didn't win anything." He threw another burr. "Not even one thing. And everybody laughed."

"Everybody laughed when you landed on your nose," said Rusty. "Because it was funny. You were laughing too, the first time. On the snowdrift."

"That was before," said Beau.

Rusty scratched his ear.

"Remember the time I said I knew a shortcut to the Old Mill," he said, "and took everybody through the bramble patch, and we came out at the beaver ponds, and Maggie Mae had a bramble stuck in her bow for a week?"

Beau's mouth twitched, despite himself.

"Everybody laughed at me then," said Rusty. "And it was horrible for about a minute. And then I laughed too. And then it was funny. And now it's one of everybody's favourite stories, and I get to tell it." He looked at Beau.

Beau did not answer that.

"But that's not the bad bit," said Rusty quietly. "The bad bit's what you said. About Charlie. And Sadie Belle. And Barney."

Beau's ears went down even further.

"Charlie's face went all crumpled," said Rusty. "She won her first ever ribbon, and you said she only won because she's tiny."

"I know," said Beau, very small.

"And Sadie Belle made you that rosette," said Rusty, nodding at the pink ribbon still pinned to Beau's vest, with its little stitched B. "For luck."

Beau looked down at it for a long time.

Rusty stood up.

"I'm going back," he said. "There's cocoa." And he went, leaving Beau alone on the log.

Beau sat for a long while after that.

Then, from somewhere nearby, there came a small sniffing sound.


Chapter Seven — The Littlest Rabbit

It came from the bottom of the sledging hill.

Beau got up and went to look through the trees. And there, at the bottom of the long gentle slope, all on her own, sat the littlest rabbit, holding a tiny wooden tea tray and looking up at the hill.

Everybody else had gone over to the table for cocoa. Nobody had noticed her. Her mother was by the table too, with the other kits, only a little way off, but not looking just then.

Beau went over and sat down beside her in the snow.

"What's the matter?" he said.

The littlest rabbit sniffed.

"I wanted to go down," she said. "In the race. I've been down the little slope behind the Great Barn, lots of times. But that's a baby slope. This is the real hill. And it's so long. And so high. And everybody went so fast. And I'm scared I'll fall off." She sniffed again. "So I didn't go. And now it's finished."

Beau looked up at the hill. It did not look very long to him. It did not look very high. But then, he was a bobcat, and she was the very smallest rabbit in the Hollow.

He thought about Benny's rules, and the spinning sledge, and landing nose-first in the snow.

"Do you know what," he said slowly. "I fell off three times today."

The littlest rabbit looked up at him.

"Three?"

"I landed on my nose in the snow leap. I went down this hill backwards. And I threw a snowball at the miller's hat." He thought. "That's not falling off, exactly. But it's close."

The littlest rabbit giggled. It was a very small giggle, but it was a giggle.

"Did it hurt?" she asked.

"Not one bit," said Beau. "This snow's soft, and Great-Grandpappy Buck picked this hill specially, with nothing hidden under it." And then he had an idea. "Would you like me to show you how to fall off properly? So it doesn't matter if you do?"

The littlest rabbit's eyes went very wide.

"There's a properly?"

"There is," said Beau. "I've had loads of practice this morning." Then he stopped, and looked over at the cocoa table. "But we have to ask your mother first. That's the rule."

"I'll fetch her," said a voice.

It was Rusty. He had come back to fetch Beau for cocoa, and he had seen the whole thing. He trotted off across the snow, and came back a minute later with the rabbit mother, who listened to Beau's idea with her head on one side, and then nodded, and stood at the bottom of the slope to watch.

So Beau showed her.

First, he showed her on the flat, at the bottom of the hill. How to tuck your chin in and keep your arms in and curl up round, like a roly-poly bug, and roll, so that you landed soft. He did it three times, and she laughed at him. Then she did it, very carefully, and rolled over in the snow, and sat up with snow on her nose, astonished.

"It didn't hurt!"

"Told you," said Beau.

Then he walked a little way up the hill with her, only a very little way, no higher than her mother said, and set her tray down, and held it steady while she sat on it, facing forward, feet first.

"I'll walk right beside you," he said. "And if you fall off, you just roll."

She went down. It was a very short way, and very slow. Halfway down, she tipped off sideways into the soft snow, and curled up, and rolled, exactly as he had shown her, and sat up at the bottom covered in snow from ear to ear.

And laughed and laughed and laughed.

"AGAIN!" she shouted.


Chapter Eight — The Falling-Over Race

By the time the others came back from the cocoa table, the littlest rabbit had been down the bottom part of the hill nine times, from a little higher each time, though never higher than her mother allowed, and fallen off four of them, and rolled every single time, while Beau walked beside her tray cheering at the top of his voice and Rusty stood with her mother at the bottom, cheering too.

"Sit up tall! Feet first! You're doing it! Oh, that was a brilliant roll! That was the best roll I ever saw!"

Everybody stopped and stared.

Great-Grandpappy Buck, on his stool, leaned forward on his walking-stick, and said nothing, but his eyes crinkled up behind his spectacles.

At the bottom of the hill, the littlest rabbit rolled to a stop, and sat up, and saw everybody, and shouted, "I WENT DOWN THE BIG HILL! I FELL OFF FOUR TIMES! BEAU TAUGHT ME HOW TO ROLL!"

Beau looked round. He saw everybody watching. He saw Charlie, standing beside Maggie Mae, not looking at him.

He walked straight across the snow to her, before he could change his mind.

"Charlie," he said. "I'm sorry for what I said. You didn't win because you're tiny. You won because you threw them up high, like rainbows, and that was clever, and I never thought of it." He swallowed. "You were the best at snowballs. Properly."

Charlie looked at him for a moment. Then she smiled, a little.

"You threw one at the miller's hat," she said.

"I did," said Beau. "That was terrible."

Then he went to Barney.

"Sliding on your tummy wasn't cheating," he said. "It was brilliant. Will you show me how you steer with your tail?"

"It's all in the wiggle," said Barney happily, and shook his paw.

Then he went to Rusty.

"Ten knots," said Beau. "Exactly. Well done."

"Yours went further," said Rusty.

"Nose first doesn't count," said Beau. "Benny's rules are fair." And Benny, who had heard, wrote something in his notebook and underlined it twice.

And then he went to Sadie Belle, last of all, and stood in front of her, and looked at his paws.

"You weren't slow," he said. "You were careful. I followed a cocoa pot. You looked at every single print." He touched the little pink rosette on his vest. "Thank you for this. I'm sorry I said I didn't need luck. I needed loads." He scuffed the snow. "Next winter, will you teach me to track properly? Slowly?"

"Yes," said Sadie Belle softly, to the snow. "I'd like that."

Then Beau turned round to everybody, the whole meadow.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For what I said by the woods. All of it. It wasn't true, and it wasn't kind."

There was a pause. Then Brody said, "That's all right, Beau," in his big warm voice, and everybody else said it too, more or less at once, and Beau's ears came back up.

"I've got an idea for one more event," he said. "A fifth one. The Roll-Down."

"The what?" said Benny.

"No sledges," said Beau. "Just rolling. Everybody rolls down the gentle bit at the very bottom, where it runs out flat. And the winner is whoever rolls the funniest." He looked at the littlest rabbit. "She's the judge. She's the expert."

Everybody looked at Great-Grandpappy Buck. Buck looked at the gentle foot of the slope, and the wide flat snow beyond it, and nodded.

"One at a time," he said. "And only the bottom bit."

Before anybody started, Maverick, who had been watching from his fence post all day, flew slowly down the whole length of the run-out, low over the snow, peering hard with his sharp eagle eyes, and landed at the far end.

"Not a stick, not a stone," he called. "All clear!"

Brody stamped out a broad flat place to start from with his big paws, so that nobody would begin on a slope. Then everybody rolled, one at a time. Brody rolled like a barrel. Charlie rolled so fast she went twice as far as anybody expected. Reba rolled sideways, slowly, stopping to look at everything on the way down, and came up holding a red mitten she had found in the snow, which was the very one the miller's wife had lost that morning. Maverick rolled once, very carefully, with his wings folded tight, and came up with his feathers full of snow, looking extremely dignified, and the littlest rabbit laughed so hard she rolled over too, without meaning to at all.

Beau went last. He rolled four times, came to a stop at Brody's feet, and sat up with snow in both ear tufts, and bowed.

The littlest rabbit thought about it very seriously.

"Beau wins," she said. "Because he's had the most practice."


Chapter Nine — The Rosettes

The sun was going down behind Weather Ridge when Maggie Mae gave out the rosettes.

She did it properly, standing beside her little table in the gold light, with everybody gathered round.

Charlie went up first, for the snowball throwing, and as Maggie Mae tied the FIRST rosette onto her overalls, Beau put his paws round his mouth and shouted, "Three on the head! Six points! Best snowballs in the Hollow!" so loudly that the rabbits jumped all over again. Then Rusty went up for the snow leap, and Barney for the sledging, and Sadie Belle for the tracking, and Beau cheered every one of them as if they were the last.

Then Maggie Mae held up the big gold rosette. CHAMPION OF WINTER.

There was a pause.

"Well," said Maggie Mae. "Nobody won more than one event. So nobody's the Champion. I suppose it stays in the box."

"No," said a small voice.

It was the littlest rabbit. She had climbed up onto the table, and her mother lifted her straight back down again, but she was still holding out her paw for the gold rosette.

Maggie Mae gave it to her, puzzled. Reba, who could never see anything without wanting to look at the back of it, leaned over and turned it round in the littlest rabbit's paws.

"Look," said Reba. "The back's empty. There's nothing on it at all."

It was true. On the back, nobody had embroidered anything. It was plain gold.

The littlest rabbit hopped down, and walked across the snow, and held it up to Beau, plain side out.

"You can't have Champion of Winter," she said seriously. "Because you didn't win anything." She thought. "Except rolling. So you can have this side instead."

"It doesn't say anything," said Beau.

"I'll tell you what it says," said the littlest rabbit. "It says Best Cheerer."

Beau looked at the plain gold rosette. Then he knelt down in the snow, and Maggie Mae helped the littlest rabbit tie it on to the button of his vest by its ribbons, next to Sadie Belle's little pink one with the B. The littlest rabbit did the knot herself, very carefully, with her tongue sticking out. It took four tries.

When she had finished, he stood up, and looked down at the two rosettes side by side, and found that he had never in his whole life been so pleased with anything.

"Well," said Rusty, beside him. "We saw."

"We did," said Beau, and laughed, and tumbled backwards into a snowdrift, and pulled Rusty in after him, and they both lay there in the snow, laughing.


Chapter Ten — The Bottom of the Hill

When the light went blue, they all walked over together to Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge by Grandpappy's Bridge, with the grown-ups carrying the lanterns, because there was cocoa there, and a fire, and nobody wanted the day to end quite yet.

They crowded onto the porch with their mugs, wet round the edges and very tired. Benny had his notebook open on his knee and was adding up the day's results one last time, because somebody had to. The littlest rabbit sat squashed between her mother and Beau, showing anybody who would look exactly how to tuck your chin in and keep your arms in, until her mother put a paw on her head and said that was enough rolling for one day.

Buck lowered himself into his rocking chair with a long sigh.

"I watched every bit of those games from my stool," he said. "And I'll be honest with you. That was the best afternoon I've had all winter."

"Even though I didn't win anything?" said Beau.

Buck's whiskers twitched.

"When I was a young beaver," he said, "I was the fastest sledger in the Hollow. Ask anybody my age. Very proud of it, I was. One winter there was a race on the long hill behind the Sugar Grove, and I was halfway up with my sledge when I passed my little sister. She was sitting in the snow, crying, because her sledge was too heavy for her to pull and she couldn't get it any higher." He rocked a little. "So I tied her sledge on behind mine, and we pulled them up together, very slowly, one step at a time. By the time we reached the top, the race was over. Somebody else had won it. I can't even remember who."

"What about your sister?" said Charlie.

"She went down that hill nine times before supper," said Buck. "Laughing all the way."

He looked out towards the meadow, where the dusk was gathering under the trees, and was quiet for a little while. Then he said, "Help a neighbour up the hill, and you'll find you climbed it too."

Beau looked down at the littlest rabbit, leaning against his side with her eyes half closed and snow still in her whiskers, and thought about the long, slow walks up the bottom of the hill beside her tray, a step at a time.

Then the rabbit mother stood up, because it was well past bedtime.

"Thank you, Beau," she said quietly. "She's been frightened of that big hill all winter. The little slope behind the barn was one thing. That hill was quite another."

"She was brilliant," said Beau. "She did it all herself. I just walked next to her."

The rabbits set off for home with the miller and his wife, who were going the same way, and her father, who had come up the path to meet them, carried the littlest rabbit home on his shoulders. She was asleep before they reached the bridge.

"Right," said Benny, and stood up with his notebook, and cleared his throat. "Final results of the Great Winter Games of Hickory Hollow. Snowball throwing: Charlie, six points. Snow leap: Rusty, ten knots exactly. Sledging: Barney. Tracking: Sadie Belle. Roll-Down: Beau, for having had the most practice. Champion of Winter..." He looked down at the page. "Nobody. Best Cheerer: Beau. Nose-first leaps: one, not counted." He closed the notebook. "Next winter, I'm going to need a bigger notebook."

Everybody laughed, softly, because it was late. Maggie Mae said that next winter she would make rosettes for second and third as well, and for rolling, and for cheering, and possibly one for Barney's tail, and Barney said that was only fair. Then Buck said "Bed," in the voice that meant it, and the grown-ups lit their lanterns again, and everybody went home in twos and threes along the snowy paths, with somebody's mother or father walking with every group.

And out in Fern Meadow, the sledging hill lay quiet in the dark under its snow. At the bottom of it, in the wide flat run-out, there were a great many round roll-marks, big ones and middle-sized ones and one long beaver-shaped groove, and one very small one, which went on, and on, much further than anybody would have thought, and stopped in a neat little heap right at the very end.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
