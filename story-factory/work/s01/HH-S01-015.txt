Chapter One — The Quiet Morning

In the first week of February, the cold came down on Hickory Hollow like a lid on a pot.

It was not snowy cold. The sky was clear, a hard pale blue all day, and at night it was so full of stars that it looked frosted. But the air was the sharpest it had been all winter. The creek froze along both its edges, and the beaver ponds froze white from bank to bank, and the icicles on the Old Mill grew so long that Benny had to stop measuring them, because his ruler was not long enough.

And on Tuesday morning, Benny woke up, and lay in bed, and knew at once that something was wrong.

He lay very still and listened.

He could hear Barney breathing in the other bed, slow and snuffly, with his nose under the quilt. He could hear the stove ticking as it warmed up downstairs, where their mother was making porridge. He could hear a chickadee somewhere outside, and the creak of the cold in the roof beams.

What he could not hear was the wheel.

All his life, every morning, Benny had woken up to the sound of the Old Mill's great wooden waterwheel turning in the creek. Clack. Splash. Clack. Splash. It was the first sound of the day in the Hollow, as steady as a heartbeat, and so ordinary that most creatures never noticed it at all.

Benny noticed it. Benny noticed everything that could be counted. He had once worked out that the wheel went round eleven times a minute, and he had written it down.

This morning, it was not going round at all.

"Barney," he said. "Barney. Wake up. Listen."

Barney opened one eye.

"I'm listening," he said. "I can't hear anything."

"Exactly," said Benny, and got out of bed.

Ten minutes later, the twins were hurrying along the frozen creek path towards the Old Mill, with their scarves wound up to their noses and their breath puffing out in front of them like two small steam engines. Their mother had said they could go if they stayed on the path and did exactly what the miller told them, and they had both promised.

The Mill stood beside the creek where it always stood, tall and grey and solid, with its stone chimney smoking. But the great wheel at its side was still. It hung over the water like a giant's coat button, and every paddle on it was furred with ice. Icicles hung from its rim in long glassy fringes. It looked less like a wheel than like a huge frozen flower.

The miller was standing on the bank in his big coat, with his floury cap pulled down over his ears, looking at it.

"Morning, lads," he said, without turning round. "Come to see the trouble, I expect."

"It's stopped," said Benny.

"It has," said the miller. "I stopped it. Last night, about midnight." He sighed, and his sigh made a cloud. "And I don't rightly know when I'll get it going again."


Chapter Two — Why It Stopped

The miller did not let them anywhere near the wheel.

Before he would tell them anything, he walked them along the bank to a rope he had strung between two posts, a good long way back from the edge, where the ground was flat and the snow was trodden firm.

"That rope," he said, "is the line. While this cold lasts, no youngster goes past it. Not one step. Not for a dropped mitten, not for a dare, not for anything. The race is deep and fast and the ice at its edges will break under a sparrow. I want to hear you say you understand."

"Understood," said the twins together.

"Good." The miller leaned on the rope beside them, and looked at his wheel, and began to explain. He was a patient creature, the miller, and he liked to explain things, and he could see that Benny would not rest until he understood.

"A waterwheel turns because water pours onto its paddles," he said. "Water comes down the race there, that channel along the side of the Mill, from the pond upstream. Goes through the grate, to keep the sticks and leaves out. Then onto the wheel, and round she goes. Clack, splash. You know."

"Eleven turns a minute," said Benny.

The miller looked at him with respect.

"That's right," he said. "Well. When it gets as cold as this, two things go wrong. First, the splash freezes. Every time the wheel goes round, the spray lands on the paddles, and some of it freezes there, and some more lands on top, and freezes on top of that. So the paddles get heavier, and heavier. And not evenly, either. One side gets heavier than the other. And then the whole wheel's out of balance, and it shakes and strains on its axle, and if you're not careful you crack the axle, or break the gears inside the Mill. That's why I stopped her."

Benny was already writing it down.

"And the second thing?" said Barney.

The miller pointed down the race, to where it ran in under a little wooden bridge to the grate. The water in the race was still moving, dark and quick, steaming faintly in the bitter air. But at the grate, it was not water any more. It was a great grey heap of something like wet sugar, piled up against the bars, thick and slushy and stuck fast.

"That," said the miller. "Ice mush. On a night like last night, clear and still and bitter, the water in the race gets so cold it starts making little needles of ice, right there in the moving water. Millions of them. They float down and pile up against the grate, and they stick to it, and they stick to each other, and in a few hours they've blocked it up solid. And no water gets through to the wheel at all."

"What do you do?" said Benny.

"Rake it out," said the miller. "In the day, with the long rake, standing on the boards up there where it's safe. Knock the ice off the paddles with a wooden mallet. Get her going again." He shook his head. "But then night comes, and it all happens again. And if this cold lasts a week, which the old ones say it might..." He looked towards the Mill, where his wife was standing in the doorway. "We've flour for about a week. After that, there's no new flour in the Hollow till the wheel turns."

"No flour?" said Barney, horrified. "No bread? No cakes? No pancakes?"

"No pancakes," said the miller gravely.

The twins looked at each other.

"We'll help," said Benny. "We'll find out how to stop it happening."

The miller looked at the two young beavers standing at his rope line, with their scarves up to their noses and their eyes very determined, and he did not laugh at them. He only nodded, slowly.

"Find out all you like," he said. "From this side of the rope."


Chapter Three — Two Kinds of Thinking

The trouble was, Benny and Barney did not think in the same way at all.

Benny thought like a measuring stick. Slowly and carefully, one notch at a time, writing everything down, checking it twice.

Barney thought like a firework. Bang, and an idea went off, and then bang, another one, and some of them were brilliant and most of them were very, very silly, and he never knew which was which until afterwards.

They sat at the kitchen table at home that afternoon, with a pot of hot cocoa between them, and thought.

"Hot water," said Barney at once. "We pour hot water on the paddles. Ice melts. Done."

"How much hot water?" said Benny. "It's a very big wheel."

"Lots."

"And then what happens to the hot water, when it's on the paddles, and the air's freezing?"

Barney thought about it. "It goes cold."

"And then?"

"It freezes," admitted Barney. "Into more ice." He thought again. "And anyway the miller would never let us near the wheel with a kettle."

"He wouldn't," agreed Benny.

"All right," said Barney. "We bang it. We get lots of drums and we bang them right next to the Mill, and the ice gets shaken off by the noise."

"Barney."

"What?"

"That isn't how ice works."

"You don't know," said Barney. "Has anybody ever tried?"

"Probably not," said Benny, "because it's silly."

Barney folded his arms. Benny folded his. Their mother, coming in with the washing, looked at the pair of them glaring at each other over the cocoa pot, and went straight out again, because she had been the mother of twins for long enough to know when to leave them to it.

"Fine," said Barney. "What's your idea, then?"

Benny opened his notebook.

"I don't have one yet," he said. "I need to collect information first. How cold it gets. When the ice mush starts. How fast it grows. Then I'll know."

"That'll take ages."

"It'll take as long as it takes," said Benny. "You can't solve something properly if you don't know what it's doing."

"And while you're collecting information," said Barney, "the flour's running out."

They glared at each other again.

Then the kitchen door banged open, and Brody came in, stamping snow off his big feet, with a sack over his shoulder.

"Your mother says you're fighting," he said cheerfully. "I've come to sit on whoever's winning."

He sat down at the table, which creaked, and helped himself to cocoa, and listened while both of them explained at once, very loudly, why the other one was wrong.

When they had finished, Brody drank his cocoa, and thought about it in his slow, steady, Brody way.

"Seems to me," he said at last, putting down his mug, "you could try Barney's ideas small. Here, where it's safe. And Benny could watch what happens, and write it down. Before anybody tries anything at the Mill."

The twins looked at each other.

"Try them small?" said Benny slowly.

"Like a little Mill," said Brody. "A pretend one."

Barney's eyes went wide.

"A MODEL!" he shouted, so loudly that his mother dropped the washing in the next room. "We build a model mill! With a model wheel! And a model race! And we freeze it!"

And for the first time all day, Benny and Barney both said "Yes!" at exactly the same moment.


Chapter Four — The Little Mill

They built the little mill that evening, on the kitchen table, with their mother supervising and their father lending them his small tools and keeping the sharp ones for himself.

It was not a very good mill. But it was a very good wheel.

Benny made the wheel from a wooden cotton reel, with eight paddles cut from the lid of an old cheese box, glued into slots all the way round, and a knitting needle through the middle for an axle. He measured every paddle to be exactly the same size. Barney painted the paddles red, so you could see them going round.

Brody made the race. He bent a long strip of tin from the back of the shed into a channel, and propped it up on a pile of books at one end so that it sloped, and their father helped him file off the sharp edges and tested every one with his thumb.

Then their mother filled a jug with water from the pump, and poured it, very carefully, into the top of the tin race. And the water ran down the channel, and poured onto the little red paddles, and the wheel went round.

Clack, clack, clack, clack.

It went round much faster than eleven times a minute, and water went everywhere, all over the table and the floor and the cat. But it went round.

"It works!" shouted Barney.

"Of course it works," said Benny, mopping his notebook. "Now we have to make it freeze."

So their mother found two old metal buckets, and two small spare cotton reels, and Benny and Barney made two more little wheels, exactly the same as the first. Their father set each wheel on a stick across the top of a bucket, so that the paddles dipped into the water inside. Then he carried both buckets outside into the yard, where the cold was so sharp it made your teeth ache, and set them down on the frozen ground side by side.

"One for Benny's test," said Benny. "And one for Barney's test."

"What's your test?" said Barney.

"Mine's the control," said Benny. "We don't do anything to it at all. We just see what happens to it overnight. That's how you know what normal is."

"Boring," said Barney.

"Necessary," said Benny. "What's yours?"

Barney looked at his bucket. He looked at the stars, glittering cold and hard over the yard. He looked down at the little red wheel, sitting in the dark water, looking, Barney thought, very small and very cold and very alone.

"Mine," said Barney, "is going to have a blanket."

"A what?"

"A blanket." Barney ran inside, and came back out with the old blue blanket from the end of his bed, the one he'd had since he was a baby, which had a hole in one corner and smelled of him. He put a flat board across the top of his bucket, over the wheel, and spread the blanket over the board and all down the sides, and tucked it in round the bottom, and then, for good measure, heaped snow all over the top of it.

"There," he said. "It's cosy."

Benny looked at it.

"That," he said, "is not science."

"It's kindness," said Barney. "It's a cold night."

And they went inside to bed, and lay awake listening to the silence where the wheel should have been.


Chapter Five — What Maverick Saw

The next morning, the Mill was still silent.

The miller and Brody's father had spent most of the day before raking out the grate and knocking the ice from the paddles, and in the afternoon, when the sun was at its warmest, they had got the wheel turning, slowly, for a few hours. But by midnight the ice mush had come back, and by morning the grate was blocked solid again, and the wheel was a frozen flower once more.

The twins did not go straight to the buckets. Benny made them go to the Mill first, because he wanted to see the grate again and write down how big the heap was.

At the rope line, they found Maverick sitting on a post, with his feathers fluffed up against the cold until he was nearly round.

"Morning, young beavers," he said. "I've been up and down that creek since first light. Thought you'd want to know what I saw." He pointed upstream with a great brown wing. "The pond above the Mill is frozen solid. Thick white ice, all across. Not a drop of open water. And it's not making any mush at all."

Benny was writing.

"And then," said Maverick, "the water comes out from under the pond ice, over the little weir, into the race. And as soon as it tumbles over the weir, and it's out in the open air, with nothing on top of it, it starts steaming. Like a kettle. And just below the weir, where it's tumbling and open, I could see the mush starting. Little grey flecks in the water, more and more of them, all the way down the race to the grate." He shook out his feathers. "Under the ice, no mush. Out in the open, mush. That's what I saw."

Benny stopped writing, and looked up at him.

"Under the ice, no mush," he said slowly. "Out in the open, mush."

"That's what I said," said Maverick.

"Thank you, Maverick," said Benny. "That's very important."

Maverick looked pleased, and flew off to find somewhere warmer to sit.

The twins walked home in silence. Benny was thinking so hard that he walked into a gatepost.

When they got to the yard, their father had already brought the two buckets into the warm kitchen and set them on the table, still covered, so the twins could open them together.

Benny lifted the stick off his bucket first. The control.

The water in it had frozen almost solid. There was a thick white lid of ice all across the top, and the little red wheel was frozen into it like a fly in amber, and all round the edges and up the sides of the bucket there was crusty grey ice. The paddles that had been sticking up out of the water were thick with frost.

"Frozen," said Benny, writing. "As expected."

Then Barney lifted the snow off his bucket, and the blanket, and the board.

Underneath, the water was dark. Moving a little as the bucket was set down. A thin skin of ice at the very edges, no thicker than a fingernail. And the little red wheel, when Barney touched it with his paw, turned.

It turned easily. Round and round, in the dark, liquid water, as if it had spent the whole bitter night tucked up in bed.

Barney looked at it.

Benny looked at it.

Their father, at the end of the table, raised his eyebrows a very long way, and said nothing at all.


Chapter Six — The Lid

Benny sat down very slowly on the kitchen bench, with his notebook in his paw, and stared at Barney's bucket.

"It didn't freeze," he said.

"I told you," said Barney. "It was cosy."

"It's not cosy," said Benny. "It's not about cosy. It's..." He stopped. He looked at his notebook. He looked at the bucket. He thought about the pond above the Mill, frozen solid and white, with no mush under it at all. And the water coming out from under the ice, into the open air, steaming, and making mush the moment there was nothing on top of it.

"It's the lid," he said.

"What lid?"

"The ice on the pond is a lid," said Benny, and his voice was getting faster, which it hardly ever did. "It keeps the cold air off the water underneath. So the water underneath doesn't get cold enough to make mush. It's only when the water comes out into the open air that it gets too cold and makes the needles. And your blanket is a lid. The board and the blanket and the snow, they kept the cold air off. So your water didn't make ice." He stood up. "That's why the wheel keeps blocking up. The race is open. All the way from the weir to the grate. It's got no lid."

Barney stared at him.

"So if the race had a lid," he said slowly.

"Then it wouldn't make mush," said Benny. "Or not nearly as much. And the grate wouldn't block. And the water would get through to the wheel."

"A blanket," said Barney. "For the race."

"Not a real blanket," said Benny. "It's a very long race. But something like it. Boards across the top. And something on the boards to keep the cold off. Straw, maybe. And snow on top. Snow's good at keeping cold out, that's why burrows under the snow stay warm."

Barney's mouth fell open.

"It was MY idea," he said. "The blanket. That was my idea."

"It was a silly idea," said Benny.

"It WORKED!"

"It worked by accident! You didn't know it would work! You did it because you felt sorry for a cotton reel!"

"And you'd never have thought of it in a hundred years of measuring!"

They stood on opposite sides of the kitchen table, glaring at each other, and the little red wheel turned gently in its bucket between them.

Then Brody, who had come round to hear the results, and had been standing in the doorway the whole time, cleared his throat.

"Seems to me," he said, "you both found it."

The twins turned round.

"Barney made the blanket," said Brody. "Benny worked out what it meant." He came in and sat down, and the bench creaked. "Now. Who's going to tell the miller?"

Benny looked at Barney. Barney looked at Benny.

"Both of us," they said, at exactly the same moment, and then they both laughed, and the glaring was over, just like that.


Chapter Seven — Telling the Miller

They told the miller together, standing at the rope line, with Brody behind them carrying the bucket with the little red wheel in it, so that the miller could see for himself.

Benny explained the control, and the ice, and what Maverick had seen at the weir, and showed the miller the numbers in his notebook. Barney explained the blanket, and why he had done it, and went a bit pink when he got to the part about feeling sorry for the cotton reel. And then Benny explained the lid, and what it meant, and the miller listened to every word without interrupting once, with his head on one side and his floury cap pushed back.

When they had finished, he was quiet. He looked at the little red wheel, turning in its unfrozen bucket. He looked at his own great wheel, frozen white over the creek. He looked at the open, steaming race, with its grey mush piling up at the grate.

Then he took off his cap, and scratched his head, and said a surprising thing.

"Race-boards," he said.

"What?" said the twins.

"My father had race-boards," said the miller slowly. "When I was a youngster. Long flat boards, made to fit right across the top of the race, all the way from the weir to the grate. In a bad cold he'd put them across, and heap straw on top, and the snow would come down and cover it all over. I remember it. I thought it was to keep the leaves out." He shook his head. "I'd forgotten all about them. It's been so many years since we had a cold like this."

"Where are they now?" said Benny.

"Up in the loft of the old barn behind the Mill, if they're anywhere," said the miller. "Under forty years of sacks and cobwebs." He looked at the twins, and then he put his cap back on and looked at them again, differently, as if they had grown a little since he last saw them. "You worked that out between you. With a cotton reel and a blanket."

"And Brody," said Barney. "Brody told us to do it small first."

"And Maverick," said Benny. "He saw the weir."

The miller nodded.

"Well," he said. "If we're going to do it, we'll need every paw in the Hollow. The grown-ups for the boards and the race and anything near the water. And the youngsters for everything else." He looked at them. "Find me every paw in the Hollow by this afternoon, if you can."

The twins looked at each other.

Then they ran.


Chapter Eight — Every Paw in the Hollow

By the middle of the afternoon, half of Hickory Hollow had gathered at the Old Mill.

The grown-ups climbed up into the loft of the old barn, with the miller leading and Brody's father holding the ladder, and there, under forty years of sacks and cobwebs and one very surprised family of mice, they found the race-boards, stacked against the wall. Long and flat and grey with age, but solid, every one. The miller's father had painted a number on the end of each, one to thirty, so that they would go back in the right order.

"He was a careful creature, my father," said the miller, wiping the dust off number one. "Like you, Benny."

The grown-ups did all the work near the water. They carried the boards down, two at a time, and laid them across the race, from the weir to the grate, one after another, standing on the wide flat stones along the edge where they could keep their footing. Brody's father and the miller raked the ice mush out of the grate one last time with the long rake, from the safe boards on the little bridge. And the miller climbed down onto the wheel's stone platform with a wooden mallet, with a rope round his middle tied to the Mill wall in case he slipped, and knocked the ice from every paddle, one by one, while everybody behind the rope line watched without making a sound.

And the youngsters, behind the rope line, did everything else.

Benny stood at the head of the line of boards with his notebook, reading out the numbers on their ends so that each went in the right place. "Seventeen next. Then eighteen. No, that's twenty-one, it goes further down."

Brody fetched straw. He went to the Great Barn with the farmer, and came back pulling a sledge heaped with bales so high that only his ears showed over the top, and then he went back for another, and another, until his legs shook. He never went past the rope line once. He set every bale down at the line, and the grown-ups carried them the rest of the way.

Barney and Charlie pulled the bales apart into loose straw and stuffed it into sacks, so that the grown-ups could spread it easily over the boards. Barney stuffed the sacks so full that they burst, and Charlie stuffed them so carefully that they took twice as long, and between them they made a heap of straw sacks as tall as Barney.

Reba ran messages up and down the line, because she was quick and she knew where everybody was. Rusty showed the grown-ups the shortest way to bring the second sledge round from the Great Barn without going near the creek. Sadie Belle and Beau stamped the snow flat along the rope line so that nobody would slip. Maverick sat on top of the Mill chimney, where he could see the whole race at once, and called out whenever a board was crooked.

And Maggie Mae and the miller's wife kept a big pot of hot cordial going on the Mill stove, and brought round mugs to everybody, grown-up and youngster alike, so often that Barney said he was more cordial than beaver by the end of it.

As the sun went down behind Weather Ridge, the last board went across, and the grown-ups spread the last sack of straw over the top. The whole race, from the weir to the grate, was a long low hump of boards and straw.

And then, as if it had been listening, it began to snow.

Soft, light, sparkly snow, out of a clear sky turning violet, falling straight down onto the straw, and covering it, until the race had disappeared under a thick white blanket, and you could only tell where it was by the little line of hump in the snow.

Barney looked at it.

"It's got its blanket," he said.


Chapter Nine — Clack, Splash

The twins did not sleep much that night.

They lay in their beds in the dark, side by side, and listened. Outside, the cold was as bitter as ever. The stars were sharp as needles. They could hear the roof beams creak.

But they could not hear the wheel. The miller had left it stopped, to be safe, until morning.

"What if it doesn't work?" whispered Barney into the dark.

"Then we'll find out why," whispered Benny. "And try something else."

There was a pause.

"Benny?"

"What?"

"I'm cold," said Barney. "My blanket's on the bucket. I gave it to the cotton reel."

Benny lay still for a moment. Then he got out of bed, in the dark, and took his own blanket, and spread half of it over Barney's bed, and got back into his own with the other half. It did not quite reach. Both of them had cold feet.

Neither of them minded.

In the morning, before it was even fully light, they were at the rope line, with Brody and half the Hollow behind them, watching the miller and Brody's father lift the end board at the grate.

Underneath, there was water. Dark, quick, moving water, running clear through the grate. A little grey mush, here and there, caught on the bars. But only a little. Only a handful. Nothing like the great frozen heap that had been there every other morning.

The miller looked at it. Then he straightened up, and turned round, and looked at the twins standing at the rope line, and his whole floury face creased up like a paper bag.

"Right," he said. "Let's see if she'll go."

He went into the Mill. They heard the clunk of the big lever. They heard the rush of the water, released, pouring through the grate. They saw it hit the first paddle in a spray of white.

And slowly, stiffly, creaking, the great wheel began to turn.

Clack. Splash.

Clack. Splash.

Clack. Splash.

Everybody cheered. Barney threw his hat in the air and it landed on Brody. Charlie jumped up and down so many times that she sat down in the snow. And Benny stood very still at the rope line, with his notebook in his paw and his lips moving, counting.

"Eleven," he said, at last. "Eleven turns a minute." And he wrote it down.

Great-Grandpappy Buck had come along the creek path, slowly, with his walking-stick, to see. He stood at the rope line among the youngsters for a while, listening to the wheel, with his eyes crinkled up behind his spectacles. He looked at the long white hump of the race under its blanket of snow, and at the twins standing side by side, and at Brody, and the straw on Charlie's ears, and the cordial mugs, and Maverick on the chimney, and the whole crowd of the Hollow, still clapping.

"A job shared is a job halved," he said, "and a day doubled in gladness."


Chapter Ten — The Model on the Windowsill

The cold lasted nine more days, exactly as the old ones had said.

Every one of those nine days, the race-boards stayed on, under their straw and their snow. Every morning the miller lifted the end board, and raked out the little bit of mush, and every morning the wheel went round. Clack, splash. Eleven times a minute. The flour sacks filled up again in the Mill. The miller's wife baked bread. And on the Saturday, because the twins had specially asked, there were pancakes, for everybody, at the Mill, with honey, and Barney ate nine.

When the cold finally broke, on a soft grey morning with the wind coming warm out of the south, the grown-ups lifted the race-boards off, one by one, and scraped them clean, and carried them back up to the loft of the old barn. They put them back in order, one to thirty, just as the miller's father had numbered them.

But not number one.

The miller kept number one back. And the next evening, he came to the twins' house, and knocked, and handed it to them. On the back of it, in fresh white paint, under his father's old number, he had written two new words.

BENNY. BARNEY.

"So the next miller knows who to thank," he said. "And so does the one after that."

The twins carried the board between them up to their bedroom, and propped it against the wall at the foot of their beds, where they could see it.

And on the windowsill, in pride of place, where it would catch the morning light, they put the little mill. The tin race, propped on its books. The bucket. And the little red cotton-reel wheel, with its eight paddles all exactly the same size, all painted red. Barney's old blue blanket, which had come back from the yard a little stiff and a little damp and smelling strongly of snow, went back on the end of his bed, where it belonged.

That night, the twins lay in bed in the dark, and listened.

Outside, the snow was melting off the roof in soft drips. The creek was running loud with meltwater. And through it all, steady, sure, as ordinary as a heartbeat, came the sound of the great wheel at the Old Mill, turning in the dark.

Clack. Splash. Clack. Splash.

"Benny?" whispered Barney.

"What?"

"Next time, can I do the careful bit, and you do the silly bit?"

Benny thought about it.

"No," he said. "But you can help."

And on the windowsill, in the last thin moonlight, the little red wheel stood quite still on its knitting-needle axle, with its eight paddles waiting, as if it were only resting until somebody poured the water in again.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
