Chapter One — The Slate

In the deep of January, Great-Grandpappy Buck lost his voice.

He did not lose it all at once. On Monday it was a little rough round the edges, like a saw that wants sharpening. On Tuesday it was a croak, like a bullfrog in a bucket. And on Wednesday morning, when Charlie came running across Grandpappy's Bridge to say good morning, Buck opened his mouth on the porch to say good morning back, and nothing came out at all except a small wheezy squeak, like a gate.

Charlie stared at him.

Buck stared at Charlie.

Then he held up one paw, went inside, and came back out with a little wooden-framed slate and a stick of chalk.

GOOD MORNING, he wrote, in big careful capitals. I HAVE LOST MY VOICE.

"Where?" said Charlie, looking round the porch, as if it might be under the bench.

Buck's whiskers twitched. He rubbed the slate clean with his sleeve, and wrote again.

COLD IN MY THROAT. NOTHING WORSE. A WEEK OF HONEY AND HUSH, SAYS THE MILLER'S WIFE.

"A week?" said Charlie, and her face fell. "But it's Story Night on Saturday!"

Every winter, on the last Saturday in January, in the deep heart of winter, when the snow lies thickest, the whole of Hickory Hollow gathers in the big warm room of the Old Mill. The miller builds the fire up high. Somebody brings a pot of soup and somebody brings bread, and every family brings a lantern and a blanket and as many cushions as it can carry. And Great-Grandpappy Buck sits in the big chair by the fire, and tells the Winter Tale.

He had told it every year for longer than anybody could remember. The youngsters knew every word. That was the best part of it. They said the words along with him, under their breath, and shivered at the shivery bits, and cheered at the end.

And now he could not say a single word.

Charlie looked so stricken that Buck put down the slate and patted her on the head. Then he picked it up again, and stood with the chalk against his chin, thinking.

Then he began to write. He wrote and wrote. The slate was not very big, so he had to write small.

When he had finished, he turned it round.

STORY NIGHT IS NOT CANCELLED, it said. THIS YEAR, YOU TELL. EVERY YOUNGSTER. ONE STORY EACH. ANY KIND. I WILL LISTEN.

Charlie read it twice. Then she read it a third time, with her mouth open.

"Every youngster?" she said. "Even me?"

Buck nodded.

"In front of everybody? At the Mill?"

Buck nodded again, and underlined EVERY twice.

Charlie turned and ran back across the bridge so fast that her purple bow came undone.

"Buck's lost his voice!" she shouted, all the way along the creek path. "And we've all got to tell a story! On Saturday! In front of everybody!"

Behind her on the porch, Great-Grandpappy Buck sat down in his rocking chair, wrapped his scarf a little tighter round his sore throat, and looked, for somebody who could not say a single word, extremely pleased with himself.


Chapter Two — Ten Kinds of Worried

By lunchtime, every youngster in the Hollow knew, and by teatime every youngster in the Hollow had gone a little bit quiet.

They met at Rose Arch Cottage, because Maggie Mae had sent round a note saying that if everybody had to tell a story, then everybody had better come round and think about it properly, with cake.

"I don't know any stories," said Barney, through a mouthful of cake. "Except the one about the time I fell in the millpond. And everybody knows that one. Because everybody was there."

"I know a story," said Beau confidently. "It's about me. It's about how I'm the best leaper in the Hollow."

"Your leap didn't count," said Rusty. "Nose first."

"Then it'll be a story about how I nearly was," said Beau, not at all put out. "By a nose."

Maverick said that he had a story that would be perfect. It was about his great-great-grandfather, who had flown over seven mountains and three rivers and a waterfall, and it was quite long. When Maggie Mae asked how long, Maverick thought about it and said probably about two hours, if he left out the boring bits, which there were none of.

Reba said she would tell a mystery, but she hadn't decided which one yet, because there were so many, and most of them were about where food had gone. Charlie said she would tell a story about a chipmunk who could not sit still, and everybody said that sounded like a very good story, and Charlie said yes, she thought so too, she had done a lot of research.

But three of them said nothing at all.

Benny sat in the corner with his notebook on his knee, frowning at a blank page.

Brody sat by the fire, very large and very still, staring into the flames.

And Sadie Belle sat by the window with her long legs folded under her, looking out at the snow, and her ears had gone flat against her head, which they only did when she was very worried indeed.

Maggie Mae noticed. Maggie Mae always noticed. She did not say anything in front of everybody. But when the others were busy arguing about whether a story could have two endings, she went and sat beside Sadie Belle at the window, and offered her the last piece of honey cake.

"You're not eating," she said quietly.

Sadie Belle shook her head.

"I can't do it," she whispered. "Not in front of everybody. Not in front of the whole Hollow. I'll open my mouth and nothing will come out, like Buck. And everybody will be looking at me. And waiting." Her ears went even flatter. "I'd rather be in the snow all night."

Maggie Mae did not tell her that she could do it. She did not tell her not to be silly. She just sat beside her and held out the honey cake until Sadie Belle took a very small bite.

"Well," said Maggie Mae. "It's not till Saturday. A lot can happen by Saturday."

Over by the fire, Brody gave a long, low sigh, like a tree settling in the wind.

"What's the matter, Brody?" said Charlie.

"My stories are boring," said Brody simply. "Nothing ever happens in them. I've thought and thought, and every story I know, it's just me and some trees, and something growing very slowly." He looked at his big paws. "Nobody wants to sit in the Mill on Story Night and hear about something growing very slowly."

And in the corner, Benny turned another blank page in his notebook, and frowned at that one too.


Chapter Three — The Practice

On Thursday afternoon, Maggie Mae held a practice.

She set out chairs in a row in her little front room at Rose Arch Cottage, and put her best cushion on a footstool by the fire for the storyteller, and made everybody sit in the chairs, and then she said, "Who wants to go first?"

Nobody wanted to go first.

So Barney went first, because he was sitting nearest the footstool and Maggie Mae looked at him.

Barney told the story of the time he fell in the millpond. It was a very good story. He did all the voices, including the pond's. He did the splash with his whole body, and fell off the footstool, and the chairs all laughed, even though everybody had been there and everybody knew how it ended.

"That was brilliant," said Maggie Mae. "Next."

Benny went next.

He stood up very straight beside the footstool with his notebook open, cleared his throat, and began to read.

"Snow depth on the first of December: two inches. Snow depth on the eighth of December: five inches. Snow depth on the fifteenth of December: nine inches, and a half. Coldest morning so far: the twelfth of January. Number of icicles on the Old Mill on the twelfth of January, counted from the path: forty-one. Longest icicle: one foot, three inches, measured by the miller with his long pole, while I wrote it down..."

He read for some time.

Barney's eyes began to close. Beau, at the end of the row, slid slowly sideways in his chair until his head was resting on Rusty's shoulder. Maverick tucked his beak under his wing for a moment, and then took it out again, guiltily. Charlie fidgeted, then remembered how to sit still, then fidgeted again.

Benny looked up from his notebook and saw them all.

He closed it slowly.

"That's not a story, is it," he said.

"It's very interesting," said Maggie Mae kindly.

"But it's not a story." Benny's ears went pink. "I don't know how to do stories. I only know how to do numbers." He sat down, and looked at his notebook as if it had let him down. "Numbers are true. Stories are made up. I'm no good at made up."

Then it was Brody's turn, and Brody had decided, overnight, that if his stories were boring, he would simply make them exciting.

"Once upon a time," he began, in his big slow voice, "there was a little bear cub who planted a tree. And then..." He paused. "A dragon came."

Everybody sat up.

"A big dragon," said Brody, gathering speed. "And it, um. It breathed fire on the tree. But the tree didn't burn. Because. Because it was a magic tree. And then a storm came. A huge storm. And a flood. And a giant. And the giant said..." Brody stopped. He looked at the ceiling. "I've forgotten what the giant said."

"What happened to the tree?" said Charlie.

Brody looked at her helplessly.

"I don't know," he said. "I think the dragon's sitting on it."

There was a pause. Then Barney gave a tiny snort of laughter, and tried to turn it into a cough, and could not, and then everybody was laughing, and Brody was laughing too, but only on the outside.

Then it was Sadie Belle's turn.

She stood up. She walked to the footstool. She turned round and looked at the row of chairs, at all the faces looking back at her, at Barney and Beau and Rusty and Reba and Maverick and Benny, all waiting.

She opened her mouth.

Nothing came out at all.

She closed it again, and stood there with her ears flat and her long legs trembling, while the fire crackled and the clock on Maggie Mae's mantelpiece ticked very loudly.

Then she said, in a voice so small that only Maggie Mae heard it, "I'm sorry," and went and sat back down by the window, and looked at the snow.


Chapter Four — Why

On Friday morning, Reba found Benny sitting on the steps of the Old Mill, all by himself, with his notebook shut on his knee.

Reba was the most curious creature in the Hollow. She could not see a closed thing without wanting to know what was inside it. A cupboard, a box, a basket, a notebook. She sat down beside him on the step.

"What else is in it?" she said, nodding at the notebook.

"Just numbers," said Benny gloomily.

"Can I see?"

Benny sighed, and handed it to her.

Reba turned the pages. Snow depths. Icicles. The height of the creek. How many acorns on the big oak by the bridge. Then, near the back, she found a page that was different. It was a long list of numbers, written very carefully, one for every day, going back a whole year. They were all quite close together. Four hundred and twelve. Four hundred and twelve. Four hundred and eleven. Four hundred and fifteen. And then, suddenly, in the middle of the spring, a whole row of seven that were nearly twice as big. Nine hundred and three. Nine hundred and eleven. Eight hundred and ninety-six. Nine hundred and twenty.

"What's this one?" said Reba.

Benny looked over her shoulder. His face changed.

"Oh," he said. "That's steps. From our front door to Grandpappy Buck's porch and back home again. Barney and I go round every morning to say good morning to him. I count them. Have done for a year."

"Why?"

"Because it's interesting," said Benny. "It changes. When there's mud, it's more, because you take little steps. When there's snow, it's more again. When it's dry and you're in a hurry, it's less."

Reba put her finger on the row of big numbers in the middle of the spring.

"And these? Nine hundred and something, seven days running? That's twice as many."

Benny was quiet for a moment.

"That was the week Barney had the fever," he said. "In April. He had to stay in bed. He was miserable, because it was the first warm week and everybody was out. So every morning I went round to Buck's on my own, and then I came home the long way, all round Fern Meadow, and picked him the first flowers I could find, and took them up to him, so he'd know it was spring outside even if he couldn't see it." He looked at the row of numbers. "Seven mornings. A different number every time, because I went wherever the flowers were."

Reba looked at the page. Then she looked at Benny.

"Benny," she said. "That's a story."

"It's a number," said Benny.

"It's a number with a story inside it," said Reba. "Like a cupboard with something in it. You just have to open it." She turned the page back. "Why is this one four hundred and fifteen?"

"That was the day it was so windy Barney's hat blew off and I had to chase it."

"And this one? Four hundred and two?"

"That was Barney's birthday. I ran."

Reba closed the notebook, very carefully, and handed it back to him.

"Tell those," she said. "Not the snow. Tell the steps."

Benny held the notebook in both paws and looked at it for a while, as if it were a different notebook from the one he had been carrying round all year.

"Every number's got a why," he said slowly.

"Every single one," said Reba. "That's what I always think. About everything." She stood up and brushed the snow off her tail. "I've got to go. I think somebody's taken the last of my mother's blackberry jam, and I've got my suspicions."


Chapter Five — The Real One

On Friday afternoon, Barney went looking for Brody, and found him exactly where he expected: at the bottom of the long slope behind the Old Mill, standing beside a hickory tree.

It was not a big hickory. It was only a little taller than Brody himself, with a straight young trunk and bare winter branches, and it stood a little apart from the woods, on its own, at the edge of the miller's field.

Brody was standing beside it in the snow with one paw resting on its trunk.

"I knew you'd be here," said Barney. "You always come here when you're thinking." He scuffed the snow. "I'm sorry I laughed. At the practice. At the dragon sitting on the tree."

"It was funny," said Brody. "I just didn't want it to be."

Brody nodded. He did not take his paw off the tree.

"I've been trying to think of a story with no dragon in it," he said. "That's still exciting. And I can't. Every time I take the dragon out, it goes boring again."

Barney looked at the tree.

"What's this tree, anyway?" he said. "You're always here. You're always patting it."

Brody was quiet for a while. Then he said, "I planted it."

Barney's mouth fell open.

"You never!"

"When I was very little," said Brody. "Younger than Charlie is now. Great-Grandpappy Buck gave me a hickory nut, in the autumn, and said if I planted it and looked after it, it would grow into a tree. So I did. I dug a hole, right here. And I put the nut in. And I watered it every day, all spring. And nothing happened." He stroked the bark. "All spring, nothing happened. Everybody else's seeds came up. Mine didn't. And I wanted to dig it up and see if it was still there. Every day I wanted to. And Buck said, don't. Give it time."

"Did you dig it up?" said Barney.

"No," said Brody. "And one morning, at the very beginning of summer, when I'd almost given up, there was a tiny green shoot. Just one. No bigger than a whisker. And I lay down on my tummy in the grass beside it, and watched it all day long." He looked up at the branches. "And every year it's got a bit bigger. And so have I. And now it's taller than me, and I'm the biggest youngster in the Hollow." He smiled, a slow, warm, Brody sort of smile. "That's all. That's the story. Nothing happens."

Barney looked at the tree. Then he looked at Brody. Then he looked at the tree again, all the way up, to the top of its bare branches, against the grey winter sky.

"Brody," he said. "That's not nothing happening. That's a tree happening. Out of a nut. Because of you." He shook his head. "That's the best story I ever heard, and I didn't even fall in anything."

Brody looked at him doubtfully.

"There's no dragon," he said.

"There doesn't need to be a dragon," said Barney. "The tree's better than a dragon. Tell the real one."

Brody looked at his hickory a moment longer. Then he took his paw off the trunk, and nodded, once.

"The real one," he said.


Chapter Six — Charlie's Idea

But by Friday evening, Sadie Belle had still not said a word about her story, and Maggie Mae was worried.

She went round to the little house by the edge of the Hickory Woods where Sadie Belle lived, and knocked, and Sadie Belle's mother let her in and showed her up to Sadie Belle's room, where Sadie Belle was lying on her bed with her nose in her pillow.

"I'm not going," said Sadie Belle, into the pillow. "I've decided. I'll tell Buck I've got a cold too."

"Have you?" said Maggie Mae.

"No," said Sadie Belle miserably. "That's the trouble. I'd have to tell a fib. And I'm no good at fibs either."

Maggie Mae sat down on the end of the bed.

"Have you got a story?" she said. "Not to tell. Just to have."

There was a long pause. Then Sadie Belle rolled over and looked at the ceiling.

"Yes," she said. "It's about the first time I ever walked in snow. When I was tiny. My grandmother took me out, very early in the morning, before anybody else was up, and showed me how to walk in it. And we went all the way across Fern Meadow and back, and there was nobody else's footprints anywhere, only ours. A big set and a little set, side by side." Her voice had gone soft. "And when we got home, she said..." She stopped, and her ears went flat again. "I could tell it to you. Just you. But not to everybody. Not with all those faces looking."

Maggie Mae looked at her. Then she said, "Wait here."

Sadie Belle's mother walked her home through the dusk with a lantern, and at the gate of Rose Arch Cottage they found Charlie waiting, with her father beside her, because Charlie had been worried too and had made him bring her round.

"Is Sadie Belle all right?" said Charlie at once. "She looked so sad at the practice. When nothing came out."

"She's got a story," said Maggie Mae. "A lovely one. She just can't tell it to the whole Hollow. It's too many faces."

Charlie thought about this, very hard, sitting quite still on the doorstep, which she had only lately learned how to do.

"Could she tell it to just one face?" she said.

Maggie Mae looked at her.

"When I learned to sit still," said Charlie slowly, "on the porch, in the snow, Sadie Belle didn't make it about everybody. It was just me and her and the chickadees. And I could do it, because it was just us." She looked up. "So what if, at Story Night, when it's her turn, she doesn't tell it to everybody. What if she just tells it to me? And I sit right in front, close, with my back to everybody, so all she can see is me. And everybody else can listen if they like, but she doesn't have to look at them."

Maggie Mae was quiet for a moment.

"Charlie," she said, "that is the cleverest thing I have heard all week."

"I know," said Charlie modestly. "I've been doing a lot of thinking. Sitting still gives you loads of time for it."


Chapter Seven — Story Night

On Saturday evening, the whole of Hickory Hollow came to the Old Mill.

The miller had built the fire up so high in the big stone hearth that the whole room glowed orange and gold, and the great wooden beams overhead were hung with lanterns, and the floor was covered with cushions and blankets and families. The miller's wife had made a huge pot of soup. The rabbit family had brought bread. Maggie Mae had brought a honey cake the size of a cartwheel. And in the big chair by the fire, wrapped in two scarves, with a mug of honey and hot water at his elbow and his slate on his knee, sat Great-Grandpappy Buck.

He did not say a word. He could not. But when the youngsters came in, one by one, and sat down in a nervous row on the rug in front of him, he looked at each one of them over his spectacles, and his eyes crinkled up, and he wrote on his slate, and held it up.

I AM LISTENING.

Then he settled back in his chair, and folded his paws, and waited.

Barney went first, because somebody had to, and Barney did not mind. He told the story of falling in the millpond, with all the voices, and the miller laughed so hard he had to sit down on a sack of flour, because it had been his millpond, and he remembered.

Beau went next, and told the story of how he had nearly been the best leaper in the Hollow, and how he had leapt so far that he had landed nose-first in the snow, and how it had not counted. He told it so cheerfully, with so much waving of his paws, that by the end everybody was cheering for the nose.

Rusty told the famous bramble-patch shortcut, and Maggie Mae called out, "I had a bramble in my bow for a week!" and spoiled his best bit.

Maverick stood up on a flour sack by the window, spread his great brown wings, and began the story of his great-great-grandfather, who flew over seven mountains and three rivers and a waterfall. After the second mountain, Buck held up his slate: PERHAPS JUST THE WATERFALL? And Maverick, who was a good-natured eagle, flew his great-great-grandfather straight there, and it was magnificent.

Reba told the mystery of the vanishing blackberry jam, and at the very end pointed at her own father, who went quite red under his mask and said he had only had a spoonful, and the whole Mill roared.

Maggie Mae told the story of her autumn party that went wrong, with the ladybirds and the teacup, and every guest who had been there called out their own bit before she could get to it.

Charlie told the story of the chipmunk who could not sit still. It was very short, and very fast, and in the middle of it she stopped, and sat down on the rug, and held out her paw flat, and was perfectly, perfectly still for the count of ten, while the whole room held quiet with her. And then she jumped up and finished it, even faster than before.

Then it was Benny's turn.


Chapter Eight — The Steps and the Tree

Benny stood up beside the fire with his notebook in his paws. His ears were pink. But he did not open it to the snow page.

He opened it to the steps.

"This is a story about a number," he said. "It's four hundred and twelve. That's how many steps it is, from my door to my brother's door. I've counted them every morning for a year."

A few of the grown-ups smiled, politely. Benny took no notice.

"Most days, it's four hundred and twelve," he said. "But not every day. Because every number's got a why." He turned a page. "Four hundred and fifteen was the day the wind took Barney's hat on the bridge, and I had to chase it." He turned another page. "Four hundred and two was Barney's birthday, and I ran home, because I'd made him a present and I couldn't wait to give it to him."

The room had gone quieter. Barney, on the rug, was sitting very still, staring at his brother.

"And these," said Benny, "were in April. When Barney had the fever, and had to stay in bed, and couldn't see the spring. So every morning I came home the long way, all round Fern Meadow, and picked the first flowers I could find, so he'd know it was out there waiting for him." He read them out, slowly. "Nine hundred and three. Nine hundred and eleven. Eight hundred and ninety-six. Nine hundred and twenty. Nine hundred and eight. Eight hundred and ninety-nine. Nine hundred and fifteen." He closed the notebook. "They're the biggest numbers in my whole book." He looked down at Barney. "They're my favourite ones."

Barney's mouth opened, and closed again.

Then Barney got up off the rug, walked straight over to his brother, and hugged him so hard that the notebook fell on the floor, and the whole room began to clap.

Brody went next.

He sat down on the storyteller's stool, which creaked, and put his big paws on his knees, and looked at the fire for a moment. Then he began, in his big slow gentle voice.

"This is a story where nothing happens," he said. "Except a tree."

And he told it. The hickory nut from Buck. The hole in the ground. The watering, every day, all spring, and nothing. The wanting to dig it up and look, and not doing it. And then the one green shoot at the beginning of summer, no bigger than a whisker, and lying on his tummy in the grass all day to watch it.

He did not hurry. Brody never hurried. He told it as the tree had grown, a little at a time, and somehow, as he told it, every creature in the Mill found that they were leaning forward, very slightly, to hear what would happen next, in a story where nothing happened.

"And it's still there," said Brody at the end. "Behind the Mill. At the edge of the miller's field. It's taller than me now." He looked up, a little shyly. "You can go and see it, if you like. It doesn't mind."

There was a soft murmur all round the room.

And in the big chair by the fire, Great-Grandpappy Buck, who had given a very small bear cub a hickory nut a long time ago, and had never once in all those years asked what had become of it, sat with his paws folded and his eyes wet behind his spectacles, and wrote nothing on his slate at all.


Chapter Nine — One Face

Sadie Belle was last.

She had been sitting at the very end of the row, at the edge of the firelight, with her long legs folded under her, getting smaller and smaller all evening. When Maggie Mae touched her shoulder, she jumped.

She stood up slowly. She looked at the stool. She looked at the room behind it, full of lanterns and faces and families, all turning towards her, all waiting.

Her ears went flat.

And then Charlie was there.

She came across the rug quickly and quietly, and sat down on the floor right in front of the storyteller's stool, close, with her back to the whole room, and her face turned up to Sadie Belle, and her paws folded in her lap. She was perfectly still.

"Just me," she said softly. "Tell it just to me. Like the porch."

Sadie Belle looked down at her.

Then, very slowly, she sat down on the stool, and bent her long neck, and looked only at Charlie. Not at the room. Not at the lanterns. Not at the faces. Just at one small, still, striped face, and a pair of bright blue eyes, waiting.

"It's about snow," she began, so quietly that the back of the room had to lean in to hear. "The first time I ever walked in it."

She told it to Charlie. The early morning, before anybody else was up. Her grandmother, waking her with a nudge, and taking her outside into a world gone white. How the snow had been so deep that she had sunk in up to her knees and fallen over and cried. How her grandmother had shown her: walk behind me, little one, and put your hooves in my prints, where I've already made the way. How they had walked all the way across Fern Meadow and back, and the sun had come up over Sunrise Ridge, and there were no footprints anywhere in the whole wide white world except theirs. A big set and a little set, side by side.

The Mill was so quiet that you could hear the fire.

"And when we got home," said Sadie Belle, and her voice wobbled, but only a little, "I looked back, and I said, look, Grandmother, our footprints are all wobbly. Yours are straight and mine go everywhere. And she said..." She took a small breath, and looked at Charlie, and Charlie nodded. "She said, never mind straight, little one. Look how far they went."

She stopped.

"That's all," she said. "That's the story."

Then, from the back of the room, very softly, somebody began to clap. And then somebody else. And then the whole of the Old Mill was clapping, not loudly, but warmly, as people clap for something that has been given to you very carefully and that you want to keep.

Sadie Belle looked up, startled, and saw them all, all the faces, all looking at her. And for once, she did not mind at all.

Charlie, still sitting at her feet, beamed up at her.

"You did it," she whispered.

"We did it," said Sadie Belle.


Chapter Ten — The Croak

When the clapping had died away, the miller's wife put more wood on the fire, and somebody passed round the last of the honey cake, and everybody turned, as they always did at the end of Story Night, towards the big chair by the fire.

Great-Grandpappy Buck put down his slate.

He took a long, slow sip of his honey and hot water. He cleared his throat. It made a sound like a cart going over a cattle grid.

Everybody waited.

Then he leaned forward in his chair, and looked along the row of youngsters on the rug in front of him, one by one, all the way to the end.

And in a voice that was hardly more than a creak, rough and cracked and very, very quiet, so that the whole Mill held still to hear it, he said, "The stream doesn't ask the pebbles to be all the same. That's why it sings."

Then he picked up his slate again, rubbed it clean, and wrote one more thing, and held it up for the room to see.

BEST STORY NIGHT EVER. NOW I'M GOING BACK TO BEING QUIET.

And the whole Mill laughed, softly, so as not to make him want to join in.

It was very late when the families began to gather up their blankets and cushions. The youngsters were yawning so widely you could have posted a letter in any of them. The miller went round putting out the lanterns, one by one, until only the fire was left.

On the way out of the door, Brody stopped beside Great-Grandpappy Buck's chair.

"Grandpappy," he said quietly. "Did you know? About my tree? That it grew?"

Buck looked up at him over his spectacles. He could not speak. But he reached up, and patted Brody's big paw where it rested on the arm of the chair, twice, slowly, the way you would pat the trunk of a tree you were proud of.

Brody went home with his father, smiling all the way.

When everybody had gone, the miller banked down the fire for the night and went round the big room picking up the cushions that families had left behind. In the big chair by the hearth, he found Buck's slate, forgotten, with the chalk still lying beside it, and the last thing Buck had written still on it, in his big careful capitals.

The miller picked it up, and read it, and was just about to rub it clean with his sleeve, as he wiped the flour off everything else.

Then he stopped.

Instead, he hung it up on the nail by the Mill door, carefully, so as not to smudge it, where everybody coming in for their flour all next year would see it. And then he put out the last lantern, and went up to bed, and left the big room to the embers, and the empty cushions, and the slate on its nail by the door, keeping its words safe until next winter.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
