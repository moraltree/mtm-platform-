Chapter One — Four Things

The Blossom Gathering came late that year.

The winter had been long, and the cold spell in February had been the hardest anybody could remember, and the snow had lain on the high slopes well into April. And then the spring rains had come, and gone on, and come again, until Fern Meadow was more pond than meadow. So it was not until the very end of May, when the rains had stopped at last and the meadow had dried out enough to dance on, that Great-Grandpappy Buck stood up on his porch one Monday evening and announced that the Gathering would be held on Saturday.

"And this year," he said, "we'll have guests."

Everybody on the porch looked up.

"The folk from over Weather Ridge," said Buck. "In the next valley. We used to hold the Gathering together, in the old days, when I was a young beaver. They'd come over the ridge with their fiddles, and their young fruit trees for the great planting, and we'd have the finest Gathering you ever saw. And then somehow, over the years, we stopped." He rocked a little. "I've been meaning to ask them back for a long while. This is the year."

"How will they know?" said Charlie.

"That," said Buck, "is the question. Somebody's got to take them a message. And it's a long way over Weather Ridge for a walker. A full day there, and a full day back."

There was a great flapping of wings at the end of the porch, and Maverick hopped up onto the rail, and stood there, very tall, with his white head high and his great brown wings half spread, so that the evening sun shone through the edges of his feathers.

"A walker, yes," he said. "But not a flyer. I could be over Weather Ridge and back before lunch. Leave it to me, Buck. I'll take your message."

Buck looked at him over his spectacles.

"It's an important message," he said. "There's four things they need to know, and they need to know every one of them right."

"Four things?" said Maverick. "Easy. I could carry forty. Mind like a trap, mine. Go on."

So Buck told him, slowly, counting on his claws.

"One. The Gathering's on Saturday. This Saturday. Two. It's in Fern Meadow, by the Great Barn, as it always was. Three. Come at noon, for the great planting, and bring your young apple trees. And four." He held up the fourth claw, and his voice went serious. "This is the one that matters most. Come by the low pass, along the river and round the end of the ridge. Not the high path over the top. There was a rockslide on the high path in the spring rains, and it's not safe. Not for anybody. Low pass only."

"Saturday, meadow, noon, trees, low pass," said Maverick. "Got it."

Benny, who had been sitting on the porch steps with his notebook, looked up.

"Shall I write it down for you?" he said. "So you've got it with you?"

Maverick looked at him with great kindness.

"Benny," he said. "I am an eagle. Eagles do not need notes." He tapped the side of his white head with one wingtip. "It's all up here. Every word."

"But..."

"Tomorrow morning," said Maverick grandly. "First thing. Over the ridge and back before you've finished your porridge."

Benny looked at his notebook. Then, quietly, without anybody seeing, he wrote it down anyway. All four things.

Just in case.


Chapter Two — The Long Way Round

Maverick set off on Tuesday, in the middle of the morning, when the sun had warmed the ridges, from the top of the tallest hickory by Fern Meadow.

Half the youngsters in the Hollow had come to see him go. He let them wait while he stretched each wing, very slowly, one after the other, and fluffed out his chest feathers, and looked out over the valley to Weather Ridge with an expression of noble purpose. Then he spread his great wings, and launched himself off the branch, and soared away over the meadow with a single mighty sweep, and everybody cheered.

"Saturday, meadow, noon, trees, low pass," he said to himself, as the cheering faded behind him. "Saturday, meadow, noon, trees, low pass."

It was a beautiful morning for flying.

The air was clear and cool and fresh, and the sun was warm on his back. Below him, Hickory Hollow lay green and gold and shining, with the creek running silver down the middle of it. He flew up the valley, over the beaver ponds, and the Old Mill, where the miller waved his floury cap, and the Sugar Grove, where the old maples were in full green leaf, and began the long climb up the side of Weather Ridge. The air grew cooler as he climbed. Below him he could see the low pass, curving round the end of the ridge beside the creek, and above him the high path, running straight up over the top, with a great grey scar halfway along it where the rocks had come down in the spring.

And there, just below the crest, he found a thermal.

A thermal is a column of warm air, rising up from the sunny side of a mountain like an invisible chimney, and to an eagle it is the finest thing in the world. You simply spread your wings and lean into it, and it carries you up, and up, and up, round and round in great slow circles, without your having to flap at all.

Maverick had not found a thermal this good in months.

"Just one go," he told himself. "Just one, and then the message."

He went round once. It was glorious. He went round again. And then he saw, a little way off, another eagle, a young one, circling in the same thermal, and it seemed only polite to show it how a really experienced eagle did a roll. And then the young eagle did a roll of its own, which was rather good, actually, so Maverick did a better one. And then the young eagle did a dive, and Maverick did a longer dive, and then they both went right up to the very top of the thermal, so high that the whole of Hickory Hollow looked like a green handkerchief, and came swooping down again side by side, shouting.

By the time Maverick remembered about the message, the sun was quite high.

"Saturday," he said to himself, a little anxiously, as he set off over the crest of Weather Ridge. "Saturday, meadow... meadow... noon. Trees. Something about trees."

He frowned, flying.

"And a path. Something about a path."


Chapter Three — Over the Ridge

The next valley was smaller than Hickory Hollow, and wilder, with a quick brown river running through it, and steep wooded sides, and a little cluster of cabins at the bottom where the river widened. Maverick came gliding down out of the sky and landed on the roof of the biggest cabin with a thump that shook the chimney.

The door opened, and an old badger came out, wiping his paws on his apron, and looked up at him.

"Well, I never," said the badger. "An eagle. On my roof."

"Message!" said Maverick grandly, puffing out his chest. "From Great-Grandpappy Buck of Hickory Hollow. An important message. Very important."

The badger's whole face changed.

"From Buck?" he said. "Old Buck? Well, bless my whiskers. Hang on, hang on." He turned round and called back into the cabin. "Everybody! Come out! There's a message from Hickory Hollow!"

And out of the cabin, and out of all the other cabins, came the folk of the next valley. Badgers and hares and squirrels and an elderly woodchuck, all crowding round in the yard, all looking up at Maverick on the roof, all waiting.

Maverick drew himself up. This was his favourite part. Everybody looking at him.

"Hear ye!" he said. "Great-Grandpappy Buck invites you all to the Blossom Gathering in Hickory Hollow! Like in the old days!"

A great cheer went up from the yard. Several of the younger hares began to dance.

"It's on..." Maverick paused. "Saturday. Definitely Saturday. At the..." He stopped again. Something in the Hollow. Something big. "The Old Mill."

"The Old Mill!" said the badger, a little surprised. "Well, that's new. But if Buck says the Mill..."

Maverick frowned slightly. Something did not feel quite right about that. But everybody was looking at him, and waiting, and he went on quickly.

"At noon," he said. That was right. He was sure about noon. "And you're to bring..." Trees. Something about trees. And a path. What would you bring, from trees? "Your... kettles," he said, a little uncertainly. "Bring your kettles."

"Kettles?" said the badger.

"Kettles," said Maverick, more firmly, because now that he had said it, it seemed to have a sort of ring to it. "For the... tea. There'll be a great deal of tea."

"How many kettles?" said one of the young hares.

"All of them," said Maverick grandly.

"And will there be dancing?" said another.

"A great deal of dancing," said Maverick, who had no idea, but felt that a Gathering with all the kettles in the valley ought certainly to have dancing.

"And what about the planting?" said the elderly woodchuck, from the back. "We always used to do the great planting, at the Gathering. Did Buck say anything about the planting?"

Maverick opened his beak. Something about trees. There had definitely been something about trees.

"Buck," he said, "will be delighted to see you. Every one of you." And before the woodchuck could ask him anything else, he hurried on.

"Well," said the badger. "If Buck wants kettles, he shall have kettles."

"And come..." Maverick thought very hard. There had been something about a path. Something important. High, or low. Over the top, or round the bottom. He looked up at the great ridge behind him, its high path running straight over the top, the quickest way, the way he had just flown. "Come by the high path," he said. "Over the top. It's quickest."

"The high path!" said the badger. "We haven't used that in years. Haven't been up it all spring. But if it's quickest..."

"It's quickest," said Maverick, and nodded, very wisely.

"Then we'll be there," said the badger, beaming. "Saturday. Old Mill. Noon. Kettles. High path." He reached up and shook Maverick's wingtip. "You tell Buck. Tell him it'll be just like the old days."

Maverick launched himself off the roof, to a great cheer, and flew home over Weather Ridge feeling magnificent, and only very slightly uneasy, somewhere at the bottom of his feathers, in a place he did not look at.


Chapter Four — Read It Back

He was back in Hickory Hollow before tea.

He landed on Buck's porch rail with a great flourish, and everybody came running: Benny and Barney and Charlie and Reba and Maggie Mae and Rusty and Beau, and Buck himself, coming out of his front door with his mug.

"Done," said Maverick. "Delivered. They're coming. All of them. They were thrilled. They said to tell you it'll be just like the old days."

"Well done, Maverick," said Buck warmly. "Well done indeed."

Everybody cheered. Maverick bowed.

Benny did not cheer. He had his notebook open on his knee.

"Maverick," he said. "What did you tell them? Exactly?"

"Delivered," said Maverick airily. "They were thrilled."

"Can you read it back?" said Benny. "What you said. Just so we know they've got it right."

Maverick looked at him with mild surprise. Then he shrugged his great shoulders.

"If you like," he said, and cleared his throat. "Blossom Gathering. Saturday. Old Mill. Noon. Bring kettles. High path, it's quickest."

There was a pause on the porch.

"Kettles?" said Reba.

"For the tea," said Maverick.

"The Old Mill?" said Maggie Mae.

"It's... big," said Maverick, a little less certainly.

"It's Fern Meadow," said Benny. He held up his notebook. "By the Great Barn. And they were to bring apple trees, for the great planting. Not kettles." He swallowed. "And it's the low pass. Low pass only. Not the high path."

Maverick's feathers, which had been fluffed up very proudly, went slowly flat.

"The high path," said Great-Grandpappy Buck, very quietly, and put his mug down on the rail. "Where the rockslide was."

Charlie's mouth fell open. Reba's biscuit stopped halfway to her mouth and stayed there.

Maverick looked at Benny's notebook. At the four things, written down, in Benny's neat writing. Saturday. Fern Meadow. Noon, bring apple trees. Low pass ONLY, NOT the high path, rockslide. The last one was underlined twice.

Then he looked at Buck. And for once in his life, Maverick had nothing to say at all.


Chapter Five — Owning It

Maverick flew away from the porch without a word and sat by himself on the very top of his hickory, with his back to the Hollow and his feathers drooping, and would not come down.

It was Benny who went after him. Not up the tree, because beavers do not climb trees, but to the bottom of it, where he sat down on a root, with his notebook, and waited.

"Go away," said Maverick, from the top.

"No," said Benny.

They sat there for a while, one at the top of the tree, one at the bottom.

"There was a thermal," said Maverick at last, still not looking down. "And another eagle. And by the time I got there, it had all gone fuzzy. And they were all looking at me, waiting. So I just said what sounded right." His voice went very small, for an eagle. "I didn't want them to know I'd forgotten."

Benny did not say anything. He just waited.

"Kettles," said Maverick bitterly. "Of all things. Kettles."

Then he was quiet again. And then he said, in a different voice, "The high path, Benny. They haven't been up it all spring. They don't know about the rockslide. They'll come over the top, on Saturday morning, all of them, with their kettles. And it's not safe." He turned round at last on his branch, and looked down. "I've got to go back. I've got to go back and tell them properly."

"Yes," said Benny.

"It's nearly dark. I can't fly in the dark. Eagles don't." Maverick looked at the sky, already turning gold over Weather Ridge. "I'll have to go first thing tomorrow."

"Yes," said Benny again. Then he held up his notebook. "And this time, we're going to plan it."

Maverick looked down at him.

Then, slowly, he came down from the top of the tree, branch by branch, until he was sitting on the lowest branch, right above Benny's head.

"All right," he said. "Plan it."

But first, before anything else, he flew back to Buck's porch, where everybody was still sitting in the gold evening light. He landed on the rail. He did not puff out his chest, or spread his wings, or make a speech.

"I got it wrong," he said, to all of them. "And I made bits up. I'm sorry." He looked at Buck. "I'll put it right tomorrow. First thing."

Buck looked at him over his spectacles. Then he nodded, slowly, once.

"First thing," he said.


Chapter Six — The Plan

They planned it that evening, at Benny's kitchen table, with the lamp lit and Maverick perched on the back of a chair. Benny ran the planning, because Benny was good at planning, but everybody helped.

"Number one," said Benny. "Write it down."

He took a clean piece of card, and wrote the four things on it, in his biggest, clearest, neatest writing. Not a word more than he needed. Not a word less.

BLOSSOM GATHERING — HICKORY HOLLOW
1. SATURDAY. THIS SATURDAY.
2. FERN MEADOW, BY THE GREAT BARN. NOT THE OLD MILL.
3. COME AT NOON. BRING APPLE TREES FOR THE PLANTING.
4. COME BY THE LOW PASS ALONG THE RIVER. NOT THE HIGH PATH. ROCKSLIDE. NOT SAFE.

"No kettles," said Barney, reading over his shoulder.

"No kettles," said Benny.

"Number two," said Rusty, who knew every path in the Hollow and quite a lot of the paths outside it. "Draw them the way. The low pass. So there's no mistake." And he drew it, on the back of the card, a little map: the river in their valley, and the low pass curving round the end of Weather Ridge, and the creek in Hickory Hollow, and Fern Meadow at the end of it, with a flower on it. And across the high path, over the top, he drew a big black cross, and a little pile of tumbled rocks.

"Number three," said Maggie Mae. "Tie it on properly." She found a length of strong ribbon in her pocket, and tied the card to Maverick's leg, in a neat double bow, not too tight, not too loose, so that it would not come off in a thermal, however many loops he did.

"I'm not doing any loops," said Maverick.

"Number four," said Benny. "Read it back."

"I can read it," said Maverick. "It's tied to my leg."

"Not you," said Benny. "Them. When you give it to them, they read it out loud. All four things. And you listen. And if anything they say is different from the card, you stop them, and you say it again, until it's the same."

Maverick looked at the card on his leg.

"And number five," said Charlie suddenly.

Everybody turned round.

"They have to send something back," said Charlie. "So we know they got it. Properly. Like when Maggie Mae sends me to the Mill for flour, and she makes me bring back the miller's ticket, so she knows I really went." She bounced. "They have to send something back that's only from them."

Benny looked at Charlie with enormous respect, and wrote it down.

5. ASK THEM TO SEND SOMETHING BACK.

Maverick looked at the card, and the map, and the ribbon on his leg, and the five things, and all the friends round the kitchen table who had helped.

"It's a lot of planning," he said, "for one message."

"It's a lot of folk," said Benny, "on one high path."

Then Barney stood up, and put the tea cosy on his head, and sat down on the other side of the table, very wide and solemn.

"Practice," he said. "I'm whoever opens the door over the ridge. You're the eagle. Deliver your message."

Maverick looked at the tea cosy. Then, because everybody was waiting, he hopped along the back of the chair, and held out his leg with the card on it. "Please read it out loud," he said. "All of it."

Barney untied the card, peered at it through an imaginary pair of spectacles, and read in a deep, slow, very old voice: "One. Saturday. Two. Fern Meadow. Three. Come at noon, bring... kettles."

"No!" said Maverick. "Apple trees! It says apple trees!"

"Oh, yes," said Barney, in the very old voice. "Apple trees. Silly me. Four. Come by the high path."

"LOW PASS!" said Maverick, flapping so hard that the lamp flickered and Benny's mother had to come and steady it. "Low pass! Read it again! From the top!"

Barney read it again, from the top, and this time he got every word right. Then he took off the tea cosy and beamed at Maverick.

"See?" he said. "Now you'll be listening."


Chapter Seven — The Fog

On Wednesday morning, when Maverick woke on his branch at first light, ready to go, he could not see Fern Meadow.

He could not see anything. The whole Hollow was full of fog. Thick, white, wet, soft fog, lying in the valley like milk in a bowl, so that the trees were grey ghosts and the creek was only a sound and Weather Ridge had vanished altogether.

Maverick sat on his branch with the card tied to his leg, and looked at the fog, and felt every feather on his body itching to go.

"I could go," he said, to nobody. "I could just go up through it. It'll be clear on top. I'll find the ridge."

He spread his wings.

Then he stopped.

He thought about the thermal, and the young eagle, and the rolls, and the kettles. He thought about a whole valley full of folk walking up the high path on Saturday with their apple trees, towards a rockslide.

He thought about Benny saying, it's a lot of folk, on one high path.

If he got lost in the fog, the message would not get there at all.

Maverick folded his wings again, and sat, and waited.

It was the hardest thing he had done all week. Harder than saying sorry. The fog sat in the Hollow all morning, white and thick and still, and Maverick sat on his branch and did not move, while the youngsters came out one by one, and looked up at him anxiously through the fog, and went away again.

Halfway through the morning, Rusty came, with Beau, and stood at the bottom of the hickory.

"We could ask our fathers to walk it," said Rusty. "With Beau and me. By the low pass. If we set off now, we'd be there by tomorrow night."

Maverick looked down at them, and for a moment, he very nearly said yes. It would be so much easier to let somebody else go.

"No," he said. "Thank you. But it's my message." He looked at the fog. "And it'll lift. Fog always lifts, round here, by noon. Buck says."

So Rusty and Beau sat down on the root, and waited with him, and Reba brought them all some cornbread, and nobody went anywhere.

A little later, Benny came and sat on the root too.

"You haven't gone," he said.

"I can't see the ridge," said Maverick. "If I can't see it, I can't be sure I'll get there. So I'm waiting until I can."

Benny wrote that down too.

At a little before noon, the sun began to burn through. The fog thinned, and lifted, and broke up into long white wisps that drifted up the sides of the valley and vanished. And there was Weather Ridge, green and clear and close, with the sky blue above it.

Maverick stood up on his branch, and checked the knot on the card, and spread his wings.

"Saturday," he said. "Fern Meadow. Noon. Apple trees. Low pass. Read it back. Send something back."

And he flew. Straight up the valley, over the beaver ponds, and the Old Mill, and up the side of Weather Ridge, and when he passed the thermal, warm and rising and glorious, just below the crest, he did not even look at it.


Chapter Eight — The Badger Reads It Back

The old badger was in his yard, surrounded by kettles.

There were kettles everywhere. Big kettles and little kettles, copper kettles and black iron kettles, kettles with dents and kettles with spouts like swans' necks, all lined up in rows on the grass. Every family in the valley had brought one.

"Ah!" said the badger, as Maverick came gliding down onto his roof. "The eagle! Look, we've been collecting! Twenty-three kettles! Is it enough, do you think?"

Maverick looked at the twenty-three kettles. He felt his feathers go hot.

Then he hopped down off the roof, onto the fence, so that he was not looking down at anybody, and held out his leg with the card on it.

"I got it wrong," he said. "Yesterday. I'm sorry. I didn't write it down, and I forgot, and I made bits up. Here's what Buck really said. It's all written down. Please, would you read it out? All of it? Out loud?"

The badger looked at him for a moment, a little surprised. Then he untied the card, carefully, and put on a pair of round spectacles, and read it out, slowly, to the whole yard.

"Blossom Gathering, Hickory Hollow. One. Saturday. This Saturday."

"Yes," said Maverick.

"Two. Fern Meadow, by the Great Barn. Not the Old Mill."

"Yes."

"Three. Come at noon. Bring apple trees for the planting." The badger looked up. "Apple trees! Of course! For the planting! Just like the old days!" He looked at the rows of kettles. "Not kettles, then."

"Not kettles," said Maverick. "Sorry."

"Four," read the badger, and his voice changed. "Come by the low pass along the river. Not the high path. Rockslide. Not safe." He looked up at Maverick over his spectacles. "Rockslide?"

"In the spring rains," said Maverick. "On the high path. The one I told you to take."

The yard was very quiet.

The old badger turned the card over, and looked at Rusty's map on the back, with the big black cross on the high path and the little pile of tumbled rocks. Then he looked at Maverick again.

"Good job you came back," he said.

"Yes," said Maverick.

The badger held out his paw, and shook Maverick's wingtip, just as he had the day before, but differently. "Now. Let me read it back to you again. All of it. Properly. From the top. And you stop me if I get anything wrong."

And he did. And he got everything right.


Chapter Nine — Something Back

When the badger had read the card back twice, and the whole yard had read it back once, all together, out loud, Maverick remembered the fifth thing.

"Please," he said. "Would you send something back? So they know at home that you've really got it?"

The badger thought about it. Then he smiled, slowly, and went into his cabin, and came back out with a fiddle. An old fiddle, polished and dark, with a carved scroll on the end. He plucked one string, softly, and it rang out across the yard like a voice. Then, very carefully, he took a spare string out of the little pocket inside the fiddle case, a thin, coiled, silver string, and tied it in a neat bow round Maverick's leg, where the card had been.

"Give that to Buck," he said. "He'll know what it means. He'll know we're bringing the fiddles."

So Maverick flew home with a fiddle string tied round his leg in a silver bow, and he did not take any thermals, and he did not do a single roll, though a young eagle called to him from the ridge, and he only called back, "Can't stop! Message!"

He landed on Buck's porch just before sunset. Everybody was there. They had been waiting all afternoon.

Maverick did not puff out his chest. He held out his leg.

Buck untied the silver bow very slowly, and held the coiled fiddle string up to the last of the light, and looked at it. And then his face went soft, and crinkled all over.

"That's from the old badger," he said quietly. "That's his fiddle string. That means they're coming. Every one of them." He looked at Maverick, and waited.

"They read it back," said Maverick. "All of it. Twice. And the whole valley read it once, together." He paused. "Saturday. Fern Meadow. Noon. Apple trees. Low pass." He paused again. "And about the kettles."

Maverick's feathers went pink at the edges, which nobody had known an eagle's feathers could do.

"They're bringing the kettles anyway," he said. "They said it seemed a shame to waste them. They said there'll be a great deal of tea."

Buck laughed. And then everybody laughed. And Maverick, after a moment, laughed too.

Then Buck held the fiddle string in his old paws, and looked out over the Hollow, where the evening light was turning gold on Weather Ridge, and said, "A promise is a bridge. Don't build one you can't hold."


Chapter Ten — The Gathering

On Saturday, at exactly noon, the folk from over Weather Ridge came along the low pass, beside the creek, into Hickory Hollow.

They came in a long, merry, chattering line. Badgers and hares and squirrels and the elderly woodchuck, and the old badger at the front, with his fiddle on his back. Every family carried a young apple tree, its roots wrapped in sacking. And every family also carried a kettle, which clanked.

The whole of Hickory Hollow was waiting in Fern Meadow to meet them, garlanded with blossom, and there was such a cheering and a hugging and a shaking of paws as nobody had heard in years. Great-Grandpappy Buck and the old badger stood in the middle of the meadow and held each other by both paws and laughed until they had to sit down.

It was the finest Blossom Gathering in years.

They had the great planting first, as the old custom was. The youngsters of both valleys dug the holes, and the grown-ups set in the young apple trees, twenty-three of them, in a long row along the bottom of Fern Meadow, one for every kettle, as Barney pointed out. There was a feast of the first fresh greens, and Reba's mother's cornbread, and the last of the winter's preserves. There was a great deal of tea, made in twenty-three kettles, which the grown-ups boiled and poured, and it was the best tea anybody had ever tasted. And all afternoon the fiddles played, the old badger's fiddle loudest of all, and the youngsters wove crowns of blossom, and danced on the grass.

Reba handed round the cups, and tasted the tea from every one of the twenty-three kettles, once it had cooled, to make sure it was right, until Maggie Mae took the cups away from her. The young hares from over the ridge taught the Hollow youngsters a dance that went round in a ring and then suddenly backwards, so that everybody fell over. Sadie Belle wove a blossom crown for the old badger, who wore it, a little crooked, for the rest of the day, and played the fiddle in it.

Maverick did not dance. He sat on the lowest branch of the old oak at the edge of the meadow, where everybody could see him, and nobody had to look up very far, and watched. Benny came and sat on the root beneath him, with his notebook, and counted the apple trees out loud, twice, to be sure.

"Twenty-three," he said. "And nobody on the high path."

Maverick shuffled along the branch a little, until he was right above Benny's head, and stayed there.

As the sun began to go down behind Weather Ridge, the old badger raised his fiddle, and the whole meadow, both valleys together, began to sing the old Blossom Song, just as it had been sung at every Blossom Gathering since long before Buck was a young beaver.

Maverick stayed for the first verse. Then, as the gold light began to leave the meadow, he lifted off his branch, because eagles do not stay out after dark, not even at the finest Gathering in years. He flew once, low and slow, over the singing, and every youngster in the meadow looked up and waved, both valleys, and he did not do a single roll. Then he was gone, home to his roost before the light went.

The folk from over Weather Ridge were not going home that night. It was much too far, by the low pass, in the dark. So when the song was finished, everybody went into the Great Barn, which stood at the edge of the meadow with its big doors open and its lanterns lit by the grown-ups, and spread out the blankets and the cushions, and the youngsters of both valleys settled down in a great heap on the hay at Great-Grandpappy Buck's feet, just as youngsters always had at the end of a Gathering.

Buck looked at them all, the Hollow youngsters and the hares and the young badgers from over the ridge, all mixed up together in the lantern light, with their blossom crowns slipping, and the old badger beside him with his fiddle across his knees.

"One story," he said. "Then sleep."

And he told them the oldest Blossom Gathering story he knew. The one about two young creatures, long ago, one from each valley, who met by chance at the low pass on a spring morning, each of them lost, each of them carrying a message for the other, and how they sat down together on a rock by the river and read each other's messages out loud, slowly, to be sure, and found they had both been sent to say exactly the same thing.

"Which was?" said the youngest hare, sleepily, from somewhere in the hay.

"Come over," said Buck. "Come and see us. We've missed you."

The old badger laughed softly, and drew his bow once across the strings, very low, a single long note that hung in the warm air of the barn and slowly faded. And outside, in Fern Meadow, in the soft dark, twenty-three young apple trees stood in a long row in their first summer leaves, with the kettles all washed and stacked in a shining heap beside the barn door.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
