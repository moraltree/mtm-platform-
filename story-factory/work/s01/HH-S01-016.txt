Chapter One — The Spring Book

On the first Monday in March, the thaw came to Hickory Hollow, and Maggie Mae slipped on it.

She had been coming down the steps of Rose Arch Cottage in the morning with a basket of seed packets, humming, thinking about her garden, when her foot found a patch of mud where there had been ice the day before. It went one way. She went the other. And she sat down on the bottom step very suddenly, with seed packets all round her like confetti, and said, "Oh!"

By lunchtime, the miller's wife had been, and looked at her ankle, and strapped it up firmly in a long white bandage, and said that it was a sprain and not a break, thank goodness, and that Maggie Mae was to keep it up on a cushion and not walk on it for a week. Not one step. Not even to the garden gate.

"A week?" said Maggie Mae faintly, from the sofa.

"A week," said the miller's wife. "And I'll know if you've been hopping."

When she had gone, Maggie Mae lay on the sofa with her bandaged foot on a cushion and looked out of the window at her garden, where the snow was shrinking away into wet brown earth and the first green tips of things were beginning to poke up, and she gave a very small sigh.

Sadie Belle had come round as soon as she heard, with a pot of honey and a bunch of dried lavender. She was sitting by the sofa, and she heard the sigh.

"Does it hurt very much?" she said.

"Not very much," said Maggie Mae. "It's not the hurting." She reached under the cushion and brought out a book. It was small and green, with a cloth cover worn soft at the corners, and pressed flowers peeping out between some of its pages. "It's this."

Sadie Belle had never seen the book before. She leaned over, very carefully, to look.

"It's my Spring Book," said Maggie Mae. "I've kept it since I was littler than Charlie. Every spring, I go out looking for the very first wildflower in the Hollow. The first one of the whole year. And when I find it, I write it down. What it was, and where, and the date." She opened the book. On the first page, in very wobbly writing, it said: Little white flower. By the creek. Very early. And underneath, in slightly less wobbly writing: Second year. Hepatica. Bank above Silver Creek. 9th March. And on and on, down the page and over onto the next, year after year, in writing that grew neater and neater.

"I've never missed a year," said Maggie Mae. "Not one." She closed the book, and held it on her lap. "And now the snow's going, and it's the very time they come up, and I can't even get to the gate."

Sadie Belle looked at the little green book, and at Maggie Mae's bandaged foot on its cushion, and at Maggie Mae's face, which was trying very hard to be cheerful about it and not quite managing.

Then she stood up, all at once, on her long legs.

"I'll find it," she said. "The first flower. I'll find it for you, and I'll bring it back here, so you can see it, and write it down, and you won't miss a single year."

Maggie Mae looked up at her.

"Oh, Sadie Belle," she said. "Would you?"

"I will," said Sadie Belle. And she meant it with her whole heart, as she meant everything.

She did not know yet how much trouble that word "bring" was going to cause.


Chapter Two — Where Spring Starts First

Sadie Belle did not go looking on her own. She knew better than that. She went to find help.

By the next morning, there was a search party gathered on Grandpappy's Bridge: Sadie Belle, and Charlie, and Reba, and Beau, and Benny and Barney, and Rusty, all wrapped up against the damp, all very keen.

"First wildflower of the spring," said Sadie Belle. "For Maggie Mae's book. We've got to find it before anybody else does. Before it's even properly spring."

"Where do we look?" said Charlie, bouncing.

Nobody knew.

Then Benny said, "Where's the snow gone first?"

They all looked at him.

"Flowers can't come up through snow," said Benny reasonably. "So the first flower will be where the snow went first. Where the ground's warmest."

It was a very good question, and they did not know the answer to it either. So Rusty went to find Maverick, who knew everything there was to know about where the snow lay, because he looked down on it every day.

Maverick came gliding in and landed on the bridge rail with a great flap and a shower of drips from the trees.

"Snow gone first?" he said. "Easy. The south side of everything. The banks that face the sun. Every ridge in the Hollow, the side that looks south, the snow's off it a week before the side that looks north. The bank above Silver Creek, below the beech woods, that's been bare since Saturday. Steep and sunny and out of the wind."

Sadie Belle's ears went up.

"The bank above Silver Creek," she said. "That's what Maggie Mae's book says! For lots of the years!"

So the search party set off along the creek path towards the beech woods, with Benny counting steps and Rusty knowing a shortcut, which nobody took, and Barney finding a great many things that were not flowers.

"FOUND ONE!" he shouted, about ten minutes along the path, and everybody ran.

It was a bottle cap.

"FOUND ONE!" he shouted again, a little further on, and everybody ran again.

It was a scrap of pink ribbon, caught on a bramble, which Charlie recognised at once as Maggie Mae's, from her bow, from last autumn.

"FOUND ONE!" shouted Barney a third time, and this time only Beau ran, and it was a mushroom, and Beau said it did not count, and Barney said it was nearly a flower, it was the right shape, and Beau said it was not, and they argued about it all the way to the beech woods, where Benny made them both be quiet because he had lost count.

And then they came out from under the bare grey beeches, at the top of the steep bank above Silver Creek, and stopped.

The bank was bare of snow, just as Maverick had said. It was brown and damp, covered in last year's fallen leaves, and the sun was full on it, warm on their backs for the first time that year. Below them, the creek ran loud and brown with meltwater.

There was not a flower anywhere.

"Oh," said Sadie Belle. "It's too early."

But Charlie was already down on her tummy among the leaves, with her nose almost touching the ground.

"No it isn't," she said softly. "Look."


Chapter Three — Hepatica

Under the edge of a brown oak leaf, right by Charlie's nose, something was poking up.

It was a stem. A thin stem, no longer than a whisker, and covered all over with fine silvery hairs, so that it looked fuzzy, like a caterpillar. And on top of the stem, half open, turned up to the sun, was a flower. A tiny flower, smaller than a hazelnut, with six soft petals of the palest lavender, almost white, and a little cluster of creamy dots in the middle.

All round it, there were others. Once you had seen one, you saw them everywhere. Some still curled up tight in their fuzzy buds. Some just opening. Some wide open, little lavender stars and pink stars and white ones, scattered among the old brown leaves all the way down the bank, turned up to the sun.

Everybody came and knelt down round them very quietly. Even Barney.

"Hepatica," breathed Sadie Belle. "That's what it says in her book. For lots of the years. Hepatica, bank above Silver Creek." She looked at the tiny lavender star by Charlie's nose. "This is it. This is the first flower."

Then she did what she had come to do.

She reached down, very gently, with her soft mouth, and picked it. Just one. Just the one by Charlie's nose, the very first one they had seen. She nipped through the fuzzy stem, close to the ground, and lifted it up.

Reba had brought a little basket, lined with moss, specially. Sadie Belle laid the flower in the moss as carefully as if it had been made of glass.

"There," she said happily. "Now we'll take it straight back to Maggie Mae. Quick as we can. So it's still fresh."

They went back by the shortest path, which Rusty knew, and which this time everybody took. Sadie Belle carried the basket the whole way. She did not run, in case she jogged it. She did not let anybody else carry it, in case they jogged it. And every few steps she looked down into the moss at the little lavender flower, glowing in the sunshine, and thought of Maggie Mae's face when she saw it.

It took them most of an hour to get back.

The sun went in behind a cloud while they were crossing Fern Meadow, and a cold little wind came up off the creek.

Sadie Belle did not look down into the basket again until they were standing in Maggie Mae's front room, by the sofa, all of them in a row, and she held it out with both front hooves, and said, "We found it! The first flower! Hepatica! From the bank above Silver Creek!"

And Maggie Mae sat up, and leaned forward, and looked into the basket.

Then she looked up at Sadie Belle.

And Sadie Belle looked down into the basket herself.

The little lavender flower was not open any more. Its six soft petals had folded themselves up tight together, like a small closed fist. And its fuzzy stem, which had been so straight and proud on the bank, had gone limp and bent, so that the closed flower lay on its side in the moss, drooping.

It looked, Sadie Belle thought, like something that was very far from home.


Chapter Four — What Maggie Mae Knew

Nobody in the room moved.

Then Sadie Belle's ears went flat, and her eyes filled up, and she said, in a very small voice, "It was open. It was open on the bank. It was so pretty. I carried it so carefully..."

"Oh, Sadie Belle," said Maggie Mae gently. She held out her paws for the basket, and Sadie Belle gave it to her, and Maggie Mae looked down at the little closed flower. Then she patted the sofa beside her. "Come and sit down. It's not your fault. It's just what hepatica does."

Sadie Belle sat down, very carefully, so as not to jog Maggie Mae's foot.

"Hepatica closes up whenever the sun goes in," said Maggie Mae. "Every evening, and on every cloudy day. It folds its petals up tight to keep its middle warm and dry, and it waits for the sun to come back. It's a very sensible little flower." She touched the drooping stem with one claw. "But when it's picked, it can't drink. So it can't stand up any more. Wildflowers mostly don't like being picked. They're not like garden flowers. They wilt almost as soon as you carry them away."

"I didn't know," whispered Sadie Belle.

"Of course you didn't," said Maggie Mae. "I didn't, either, the first year. Look." She opened her Spring Book to the very first page, with the wobbly writing, and there, pressed flat between the pages, was a tiny white flower, brown at the edges, faded almost to nothing. "That's my first one. I picked it, and ran all the way home with it to show my grandmother, and by the time I got there it looked just like yours." She smiled a little. "And my grandmother said..."

She stopped.

"What did she say?" said Charlie.

Maggie Mae looked at the pressed flower, and then she closed the book.

"She said, next time, write it down where it is," said Maggie Mae. "Not where you take it."

Sadie Belle looked down at her own hooves.

"I wanted you to see it," she said. "I wanted you to see the first one. Properly. Open. And now I've spoiled it."

"You haven't spoiled anything," said Maggie Mae. "There were lots of others on that bank, weren't there?"

"Hundreds," said Charlie.

"Then you found it," said Maggie Mae. "The first flower. That's what matters. I'll write it down." She opened the book, and picked up her pencil from the side table, and wrote, in her neat, careful writing: Hepatica. Bank above Silver Creek. 3rd March. Found by Sadie Belle, Charlie, Reba, Beau, Benny, Barney and Rusty.

She held it up to show them.

But Sadie Belle, looking at the words, could not quite smile. Because she knew, and Maggie Mae knew, that Maggie Mae had never seen it at all.

Only a closed, drooping flower in a basket, a long way from home.


Chapter Five — Bloodroot

Sadie Belle did not sleep well that night.

She lay in her bed by the edge of the Hickory Woods and thought about the little closed flower in the moss, and about Maggie Mae's face trying to be cheerful, and about Maggie Mae's grandmother saying, write it down where it is.

And in the morning, she went back to the bank above Silver Creek.

She went with Reba and Beau, because she had promised her mother she would not go anywhere on her own while the creek was running so high, and because Reba wanted to see the flowers again, and Beau wanted to come wherever anybody was going.

The sun was out. The hepaticas were all open again, every one, little lavender and white and pink stars turned up to the sky. Sadie Belle stood at the top of the bank and looked at them, and felt a strange ache in her chest.

"There's something else," said Reba, a little way along the bank. "Come and look. It's a different one."

It was a different one. It was poking up through the leaves at the bottom of an old stump, and it was the strangest flower Sadie Belle had ever seen. It came up out of the ground wrapped in a single grey-green leaf, rolled round its stem like a cloak, and out of the top of the cloak the flower had pushed its way up into the sun: pure white, with eight or ten long petals and a bright gold middle, like a tiny white sun of its own.

"Ooh," said Reba. "What's that?"

Sadie Belle knew. It was in Maggie Mae's book, in the third year. She had looked at the page the night before, over and over, when she could not sleep.

"Bloodroot," she said.

"Bloodroot?" said Beau. "Why's it called that? It's white."

"Because if you break the stem," said Sadie Belle, "it bleeds. The juice inside is bright red. Like the reddest berry you ever saw."

Reba's eyes went very round behind her mask. Reba was the most curious creature in the Hollow, and she had never in her life been able to leave a question alone.

"Really red?" she said. "Truly? I want to see." And her clever little paw went straight out to the white flower's stem.

"Don't!" said Sadie Belle.

Reba stopped, with her paw in the air.

"Don't," said Sadie Belle again, more gently. "It says in Maggie Mae's book. The red juice stings. It isn't for paws, and it's certainly not for mouths. And anyway..." She looked at the white flower, standing up so bravely in its grey-green cloak. "If you break it, it's broken. You can't put it back."

Reba looked at the flower for a while. Then, slowly, she put her paw back down by her side.

"I suppose I don't really need to see the red," she said. "I can imagine it." She sat back on her heels. "It is very white."

"It's the whitest thing I ever saw," said Beau.

The three of them knelt round the bloodroot in the sunshine, not touching it, just looking. A tiny fly came and landed on its gold middle, and walked round, and flew off again.

And Sadie Belle thought: Maggie Mae would love this.

And then she thought: and she can't see it.

And the strange ache in her chest got a little bit bigger.


Chapter Six — The Sweetest Thing

They were just about to go home when Beau sniffed.

"What's that smell?" he said.

They all sniffed. It was faint, but it was there, drifting along the bank on the warm air: sweet and spicy, like honey and cloves, like the inside of a cake tin at the Harvest Home supper.

They followed it.

It led them along the bank to a place where the old leaves lay thick under a big hemlock, and Beau pushed some of them aside with a careful paw, and there, creeping along the ground under the leaves, was a little low trailing plant, with tough, leathery, dark-green leaves, a bit battered from the winter. And tucked in among the leaves were clusters of tiny waxy flowers, pale pink and white, like little trumpets. That was where the smell was coming from.

"Trailing arbutus," said Sadie Belle. It was in the book too, near the end, with a little star drawn beside it, and a note.

"It smells like Maggie Mae's kitchen," said Reba dreamily, with her nose right in it.

Beau sat back on his haunches and thumped his tail on the leaves, because he had an idea.

"I know!" he said. "We can't pick it, because it'll wilt. Right? So we don't pick it. We dig it up! The whole thing! Roots and all. Carefully. And we take it to Rose Arch and plant it in Maggie Mae's garden, right outside her window, and then she can look at it and smell it all week, even with her foot up. And it won't wilt, because it'll still have its roots!" He spread his paws. "Brilliant?"

Reba gasped.

"That is brilliant," she said.

Sadie Belle looked at the little pink-and-white trumpets under the leaves. She thought of Maggie Mae on the sofa, with her foot on a cushion, and the trailing arbutus outside her window, filling the room with that sweet, spicy smell.

It was brilliant.

She opened her mouth to say yes.

And then she remembered the note.

The note in the Spring Book, next to the little drawn star, in Maggie Mae's neat writing. She had read it last night, by lamplight, and not thought much about it.

Trailing arbutus. Never dig it up. It only lives where it has chosen. It has friends in the soil that we cannot see and cannot carry. Moved, it always dies.

Sadie Belle closed her mouth again.

"No," she said slowly. "We can't."

"Why not?" said Beau.

So Sadie Belle told them about the note. About the arbutus that only lived where it had chosen, with friends in the soil that nobody could see and nobody could carry. Beau's ears went down.

"Oh," he said. "So it would just die. In her garden. Outside her window. While she watched."

"Yes," said Sadie Belle.

"That would be worse than the hepatica," said Reba.

They sat in a row under the hemlock and looked at the arbutus for a while, smelling its sweet, spicy smell.

"It's no good," said Sadie Belle at last, sadly. "We can't pick them. We can't dig them up. We can't bring them to her." She stood up. "Maggie Mae just isn't going to see the first flowers this year."

Beau stood up too. Then he stopped, and looked at Sadie Belle with his head on one side, as he always did when an idea was arriving.

"Then she has to come to them," he said.


Chapter Seven — The Carrying Chair

"She can't come to them," said Sadie Belle. "She can't walk. Not one step for a week."

"She doesn't have to walk," said Beau. "She has to be carried."

By that afternoon, the whole search party was in Benny and Barney's back garden, along with Brody, who was large and strong and good at carrying things, and Sadie Belle's mother, who had come along to make sure that whatever they built, nobody got dropped.

"A carrying chair," Beau explained. "Like a king or a queen. She sits in it. And two of us carry it, one at the front and one at the back. And we take her all the way to the bank above Silver Creek, and she sees the flowers where they are."

Benny was already measuring.

They used one of Maggie Mae's own garden chairs, which Charlie fetched from Rose Arch with Maggie Mae's permission, and which Maggie Mae said she would very much like back, please, in one piece. Benny and Brody found two long straight poles in the woodshed, and Sadie Belle's mother tied the chair firmly between them with rope, very tight, and checked every knot twice. Charlie wound some ribbon round the poles, for decoration, and also because she could not help it. Reba found a cushion.

"We need to test it," said Benny. "With somebody in it. Before Maggie Mae."

"ME!" shouted Barney, and was in the chair before anybody could stop him.

Brody picked up the front of the poles. Beau picked up the back.

"Lift," said Benny.

They lifted. Brody lifted his end up to his great shoulders, very smoothly. Beau, who was a great deal shorter than Brody, lifted his end up as high as he could, which was about level with his ears.

The chair tipped backwards at a steep angle, and Barney slid slowly down it, with an expression of mild surprise, until he was lying almost flat on his back with his feet in the air and his tail hanging over the back of the chair.

"Is this how kings go?" he said, from upside down.

"Down," said Sadie Belle's mother firmly. "Gently. Both ends at once."

They put it down gently. Barney climbed out, very pleased with his ride.

They tried again, with Brody bending his knees and Beau standing on his tiptoes. The chair tipped forward this time, and Barney slid down the other way, very slowly, until his nose was resting on the back of Brody's head.

"Better," said Barney, muffled. "But I can only see Brody's ears."

"Down," said Sadie Belle's mother. "Gently."

In the end, it was Sadie Belle who saw it.

"It's not about who's strongest," she said. "It's about who's the same height."

They all looked at each other. Then they all looked at Brody, and at Sadie Belle's mother, who was a tall, strong, grown deer with long steady legs, and was, when Brody stood up straight, almost exactly the same height at the shoulder.

Sadie Belle's mother looked at the poles. Then at Brody. Then she sighed, and smiled, and picked up the back end.

"Lift," said Benny.

They lifted. The chair rose up between them, perfectly level, perfectly steady, with Barney sitting upright in it like a little king, beaming.

"Now," said Barney grandly, "to the palace."

"To Rose Arch Cottage," said Sadie Belle, "to fetch Maggie Mae."


Chapter Eight — Where It Is

They went the next afternoon, when the sun was at its warmest, because Sadie Belle said that the hepaticas would only be open in the sun, and she was not going to have Maggie Mae arrive to find them all closed up like fists.

Maggie Mae sat in her own garden chair, with her bandaged foot propped on Reba's cushion and a blanket over her lap, looking, as Charlie said, exactly like a queen. Brody carried the front. Sadie Belle's mother carried the back. They walked slowly and smoothly, side by side, and the chair did not tip once. The rest of them walked alongside: Charlie on one side, holding Maggie Mae's paw, and Sadie Belle on the other, leading the way, and Benny counting steps, and Barney waving to everybody they passed, as if he were the one in the chair.

At the top of the bank above Silver Creek, they set the chair down gently on a flat place in the sunshine, right at the edge of the beech woods, where the bank fell away below.

And Maggie Mae looked down.

The whole bank, from the beech trees to the creek, was scattered with hepaticas. Hundreds of them. Lavender and white and pink, every one open wide in the afternoon sun, every one turned up to the sky. The sunlight came slanting through the bare grey beeches and lay warm across them, and they seemed almost to shine.

Maggie Mae did not say anything at first. She just looked. Her paw found Charlie's and held it tight.

"Oh," she said at last, very softly. "Oh, look at them. Look at them all."

Sadie Belle came and sat down in the old leaves beside the chair.

"There's a bloodroot, too," she said. "By the stump. And trailing arbutus, under the hemlock. We didn't pick anything. Not one. We left them all where they are."

Maggie Mae looked down at her.

"Where they are," she said.

"So you could see them where they are," said Sadie Belle. "Like your grandmother said."

Maggie Mae looked at her. Then she reached down from the chair, and put her paw on Sadie Belle's head, between her ears, and left it there.

They stayed for an hour. Reba showed her the bloodroot, white as a candle in its grey-green cloak, and told her she had not broken it, even though she had very much wanted to see the red. Beau showed her the arbutus, and Brody and Sadie Belle's mother carried the chair along the bank, very carefully, to the hemlock, so that she could lean down and smell it. She breathed in the sweet, spicy smell for so long that Charlie began to worry she had fallen asleep.

And as they sat there in the sunshine, a bee came. A small, early, rather sleepy bee, the first any of them had seen that year. It bumbled along the bank, and landed on a lavender hepatica right beside Maggie Mae's chair, and crawled round and round the creamy middle, and then flew on to the next one, and the next.

Charlie watched it with her mouth open, and Maggie Mae squeezed her paw.

Then Maggie Mae opened her Spring Book on her lap, and took out her pencil, and turned to the page where she had written, two days before: Hepatica. Bank above Silver Creek. 3rd March. Found by Sadie Belle, Charlie, Reba, Beau, Benny, Barney and Rusty.

Underneath, she wrote one more line.

Seen where it is, 5th March. Thank you.


Chapter Nine — The Way Home

The sun was low over Weather Ridge when they started back.

As they came out from under the beeches, the light went gold and then pink, and the air grew cool, and behind them, all down the steep bank above Silver Creek, the hepaticas began to close. One by one, the little lavender and white and pink stars folded up their petals, tight, for the night.

Maggie Mae turned round in her chair to watch them go.

"Goodnight," she said to them softly. "See you in the morning."

They carried her home the long way, round by Grandpappy's Bridge, so that Brody and Sadie Belle's mother could rest their arms on the porch of Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge.

Buck was sitting in his rocking chair in the last of the light, with a mug of tea. When he saw the procession coming over the bridge, the carrying chair with the ribbons on its poles and Maggie Mae sitting up in it like a queen, and all the youngsters round it, and Barney walking backwards in front waving, he put his mug down very slowly.

"Well now," he said. "That's a sight I've not seen in all my years."

They set the chair down on the porch, gently, and everybody sat down round it on the boards, and Buck's lodge was suddenly full of youngsters, all talking at once. About the hepatica, and how it closed when the sun went in. About the bloodroot, and the red juice that stings, and how Reba had not broken it. About the arbutus that only lived where it chose, with friends in the soil you could not see. About the carrying chair, and Barney going backwards, and the king and the palace. About the first bee.

Buck listened to all of it, rocking slowly, with his eyes crinkling behind his spectacles.

Then Maggie Mae held out her Spring Book, open, and Buck took it, and held it up to the last of the light, and read the two lines at the bottom of the page. He read them twice.

He looked over the top of the book at Sadie Belle, who was sitting at the very edge of the porch, with her long legs folded under her, a little apart from the others.

Then Great-Grandpappy Buck said, "A wild thing left wild is a gift to every creature who comes after."

He handed the book back to Maggie Mae, and she held it in her lap, and stroked its soft green cover.


Chapter Ten — The Bank in the Morning

When Maggie Mae's ankle was better, the very first walk she took was to the bank above Silver Creek.

She went on a bright, soft morning in the middle of March, with Sadie Belle beside her, slowly, because the ankle was still a little stiff, and the miller's wife had said not to overdo it. They took the creek path, and turned up through the beech woods, and came out at the top of the bank, and stopped.

The hepaticas were nearly over. Their petals had dropped, and their fuzzy stems had bent over, and where each flower had been there was a little cluster of green seeds, nodding down towards the earth.

But all around them, the bank had come alive.

There were bloodroots everywhere, more than they had ever seen, white suns in grey-green cloaks. There were tiny pale-blue flowers in the grass, and new green leaves unfurling on the beeches, and the arbutus under the hemlock still in bloom, still sweet. A chickadee was singing in the woods behind them. Down by the creek, two early butterflies, small and yellow, were chasing each other in circles over the water. And there were bees, a great many bees, busy and buzzing from flower to flower all the way down the bank.

"The hepaticas are making seeds," said Maggie Mae, kneeling down carefully beside one, and touching the little green cluster with the tip of her claw. "Look. For next year. And the year after."

Sadie Belle looked at the little nodding seeds.

"So the first flower this year," she said slowly, "is making the first flowers for next year."

"That's right," said Maggie Mae. "And somebody else will find those. Maybe Charlie. Maybe the littlest rabbit. Maybe somebody who isn't even born yet." She stood up, a little stiffly. "And they'll be here. Where they are."

They stood together at the top of the bank in the soft spring morning, looking down.

Then Maggie Mae took out her Spring Book, and her pencil, and wrote, for the first time in all her years of keeping it, not on the first page of the spring, but on the very last.

Next year: bring Sadie Belle.

She showed it to her, and Sadie Belle read it, and her ears went right up.

Then they turned, and walked home together through the beech woods, slowly, side by side, while behind them, on the warm brown bank above Silver Creek, the bees went on from flower to flower, and the little green seeds of next year's first flowers nodded down towards the earth, waiting for their turn.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
