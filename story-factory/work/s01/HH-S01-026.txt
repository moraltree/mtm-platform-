Chapter One — The Tune With No Name

In the middle of August, on a warm, lazy morning when the whole of Hickory Hollow smelled of cut hay and ripe apples, Brody went up the path to Great-Grandpappy Buck's lodge for his banjo lesson, and heard something lovely.

Buck was sitting in his rocking chair on the porch, in his dungarees and his old cap, with his banjo across his knees. He was not playing for anybody. He was just playing, as some folk hum while they shell peas, without quite noticing they are doing it. And the tune that was coming out of the banjo was the loveliest little tune Brody had ever heard.

It was slow, and gentle, and it went along like somebody walking home on a summer evening with nowhere particular to be. It went up a little, and down a little, and round, and then, right at the end, it went up again, softly, like a question.

Brody stopped at the bottom of the porch steps and stood quite still, with his green rucksack on his back and his mouth a little bit open, and listened.

Then Buck looked up and saw him, and the tune stopped.

"Morning, young feller," said Buck. "You're early."

"What was that?" said Brody. "That tune. What's it called?"

Buck looked down at his banjo, as if the banjo might know.

"Which tune?"

"The one you were just playing. The slow one. That went up at the end."

Buck thought about it. He put his fingers on the strings and played a few notes. Then a few different notes. Then he stopped, and frowned, and scratched under his cap.

"Well, now," he said slowly. "I was just noodling. Letting my fingers wander. I've got a hundred tunes in these old paws, Brody, and most of them have got names, so I can call them when I want them. But every so often one turns up that hasn't got a name. And a tune with no name is a hard thing to call back." He tried a few more notes, and shook his head. "It's gone off somewhere. It'll come back when it's ready."

Brody looked at him in dismay.

"But I want to learn it," he said. "I want to learn it now."

Buck smiled at him, over his spectacles, and patted the step beside his chair.

"Then you'd best sit down and learn your scales," he said. "That's what we're doing this morning."

Brody sat down on the step and took out the little banjo that Buck had lent him at the start of the summer, and played his scales, up and down, up and down, as he did every lesson.

Brody loved his lessons. He loved the bright, twangy sound the banjo made, and the smell of the old wood, and the way Buck nodded when a note came out clean. But they were hard. A banjo is built for small, quick fingers, and Brody had big, soft, careful bear paws, which were very good for reaching high berries and carrying little rabbits and not much good at all for pressing one string at a time without leaning on the next. Some days the scales came out like water running downhill. Some days they came out like somebody dropping a box of spoons. Buck never seemed to mind which.

But all the way through, his head was full of the tune with no name. And by the end of the lesson, he had made up his mind.

If the tune would not come back by itself, he was going to go and find it.


Chapter Two — Four Notes

The trouble was, Brody could only remember four notes of it.

He sat on the big flat rock by Grandpappy's Bridge that afternoon, with the little banjo on his knee, and tried to play them. The first note was easy. The second note was nearly easy. The third note took him three goes. And the fourth note he found almost by accident, and played it, and sat up very straight, because it was right.

Four notes. That was the beginning.

Then he tried to play the rest.

He did not know the rest. So he played what he thought the rest probably was. He played it very fast, so as to get to the end before he forgot the beginning, with his big soft paws bumping into each other on the strings. The banjo went plink, plunk, twang, buzz, plonk.

A kingfisher, who had been sitting on a branch over the creek, looked at him with great disapproval and flew away downstream.

Brody tried again, faster. Plink, plunk, plunk, twang, BUZZ.

"That's not it," he told the banjo.

The banjo did not argue.

"Brody?" said a voice. It was Charlie, coming over the bridge with Maggie Mae, both of them carrying baskets of apples. "What are you playing?"

"Nothing," said Brody gloomily. "That's the trouble. It's supposed to be the tune Grandpappy Buck played this morning. The one with no name. But I've only got four notes of it, and the rest has gone."

Charlie sat down on the rock beside him and put her basket down.

"Play me the four notes," she said.

Brody played them, slowly, carefully. One, two, three, four.

Charlie's eyes went wide.

"I know that one," she said. "Grandpappy Buck plays it sometimes, when he's fishing. I heard it last week, at Grandpappy's Pool. It goes..." She stopped, and frowned, and hummed a little, and stopped again. "I can't remember how it goes. But I remember the end. It goes up at the end. Like a question."

"I know it goes up at the end," said Brody. "That's not enough. I need the middle."

Maggie Mae, who had been listening with her head on one side, put down her basket too.

"I've heard it," she said thoughtfully. "When he walks past my garden in the evenings, on his way home from the Mill. He hums it." She thought hard. "I don't remember the notes. But I remember that it was slow. Very slow. Slower than you were playing it, Brody, by a mile."

Brody looked from one of them to the other.

"So other folk have heard it," he said slowly. "Bits of it. Different bits."

"Lots of folk, I should think," said Maggie Mae. "He's always humming."

Brody stood up, so suddenly that the banjo twanged.

"Then I'll ask everybody," he said. "And I'll get a bit from everybody. And I'll put all the bits together. And I'll have the whole tune by tomorrow."

Maggie Mae and Charlie looked at each other.

"By tomorrow?" said Charlie.

"By teatime," said Brody, and set off up the path at a run, with his banjo bouncing on his back.


Chapter Three — Everybody's Bit

Brody spent the whole of the rest of the afternoon asking everybody in the Hollow about the tune with no name.

Rusty had heard it. Rusty had heard it while he was sitting on Buck's porch steps waiting for a story, and because Rusty could whistle back almost anything he heard, birds included, he stood up and whistled a bit of it. It was a twiddly bit, quick and bright, that went round and round.

"Are you sure that's it?" said Brody.

"Pretty sure," said Rusty. "Maybe. It might have been a wren."

Benny had heard it too, but Benny did not remember the notes. Benny remembered the counting.

"It goes in threes," said Benny. "One, two, three. One, two, three. I noticed, because most of Grandpappy Buck's tunes go in fours, and that one didn't." He tapped it out on the rail of the bridge. "One, two, three. It's very unusual."

Barney had definitely heard it. Barney sang it at once, with great confidence, marching up and down the path with his tail going like a drum.

"Dum, dum, de-dum, DUM, DUM!"

"That's not it," said Brody. "That's a marching song."

"Is it?" said Barney, surprised. "Then what's the one I'm thinking of?"

Nobody knew. Barney went off still singing it, to find out.

Beau had heard it, he said, and it was a good tune, but it needed a big finish.

"Something like this," said Beau, and leapt onto a stump with his paws in the air, and sang a long, high, wobbling note that went on and on until he ran out of breath and fell off the stump.

"It didn't have a big finish," said Brody. "It went up at the end. Quietly. Like a question."

"It should have had a big finish," said Beau, from the ground.

Reba thought she had heard it, but every time she tried to hum it, it turned into the tune the miller's wife sang when she was taking pies out of the oven, and then Reba got hungry and could not concentrate.

And Sadie Belle, when Brody found her at the edge of the Wildflower Glades, closed her eyes and listened to something nobody else could hear, and said, "It was slow. And it was kind. It sounded like somebody sitting with you when you're sad, and not saying anything."

Brody looked at her for a while.

"That's beautiful, Sadie Belle," he said. "But what were the notes?"

Sadie Belle opened her eyes.

"I don't know," she said, apologetically. "I don't remember notes. Only what things feel like."

By teatime, Brody had four notes of his own, and a twiddly bit that might have been a wren, and a count of three, and a marching song that was not it, and a big finish that was not it either, and something about pies, and something about being sad and not saying anything.

He sat down on the step of Buck's lodge with all of it whirling round in his head, and his banjo on his knee, and did not know where to start.


Chapter Four — The Tune That Fell Downstairs

So he started everywhere at once.

That evening, all his friends gathered on Buck's porch to hear him play it, because he had told them he would. Buck was there too, rocking slowly in his chair at the far end of the porch, with his eyes half closed.

Brody stood up in front of everybody with the little banjo, and took a deep breath.

"This is the tune with no name," he said. "All of it. I put it together."

And he began.

He played his four notes. That part was right. Then he played Rusty's twiddly bit, very fast, so as not to lose it. Then he tried to make it go in threes, for Benny, but his fingers were still going in the twiddly bit, so it came out in sort of three-and-a-halves. Then he remembered it was supposed to be slow, for Maggie Mae, and slowed right down in the middle of a note. Then he remembered Beau's big finish, and could not stop himself trying it, and then he remembered it was supposed to end like a question, and tried to do both at once.

The banjo went plink, plunk, plinkety-plinkety-plink, twaaang, plonk. And then a long, high, wobbling BUZZ, like a bee falling down a drainpipe.

There was a pause on the porch.

"That," said Maverick, from the rail, "sounded like a tune falling down the stairs."

"With a big finish," said Beau loyally.

"It was very unusual," said Benny.

Brody looked down at the banjo. His ears had gone hot.

"It's wrong," he said. "It's all wrong. It doesn't sound anything like it."

He tried again, faster, crosser, plucking the strings so hard that his paws stung. And on the very last note, there was a sharp little ping, and one of the strings broke, and curled up like a fern, and the banjo went silent.

Brody stood there, holding the broken banjo, with everybody looking at him.

"I can't do it," he said, in a very small voice for such a big bear. "I've got all the bits, and I can't make them go together. And now I've broken a string."

Great-Grandpappy Buck opened his eyes and got up out of his rocking chair, slowly, as he did everything, and came along the porch, and held out his paw for the banjo.

"Strings break," he said. "That's what they're for. Breaking, so the banjo doesn't." He turned the little banjo over in his big old paws. "I've got a spare in the drawer. Come back in the morning, and she'll be good as new."

He did not say anything about the tune.

Brody went home that night with his paws stinging and his head aching, and the tune with no name further away than ever.


Chapter Five — Too Fast

Brody did not sleep very well.

He tried to hear the tune in his head, as he had heard it on Buck's porch that first morning, slow and gentle and going up at the end. But every time he got near it, Rusty's twiddly bit came whirling in, and Barney's marching song, and Beau's big finish, and the whole thing fell downstairs again.

In the morning, he went up the path to Buck's lodge very early, before the dew was off the grass.

Maverick was sitting on the fence post at the corner of Buck's meadow, warming his wings in the first of the sun, and he called down as Brody went by.

"I've been thinking about your tune," he said. "I've heard it, you know. Lots of times. From up there." He pointed his beak at the sky. "I fly over the porch on my way home most evenings. And I've noticed something."

Brody stopped.

"Grandpappy Buck only ever plays it when there's nobody about," said Maverick. "When everybody's gone home and the porch is empty and he's just sitting there, rocking. Never when anybody asks him. Never when there's a crowd." He ruffled his feathers. "It's a very shy sort of tune, I think. It doesn't come when it's called."

Brody thought about that all the way up the path.

Buck was already on the porch. The little banjo was lying across his knees, with a bright new string on it, and he was turning the pegs, slowly, one at a time, listening to each string as he tuned it.

Brody sat down on the step.

"I'm sorry about the string," he said.

"Strings break," said Buck again, and went on tuning. Plink. A little turn of the peg. Plink. Another little turn. Plink. He tuned that one string for so long that Brody began to fidget.

"Can't you just tune it quickly?" said Brody.

Buck smiled, and went on tuning, exactly as slowly as before.

"You can tune a banjo quick," he said. "Or you can tune it right. Took me about sixty years to find out you can't do both." Plink. He listened, and nodded, and handed the banjo over. "There. Now she's ready. Let's see about you."

Brody took the banjo, and looked at it.

"I don't know how to find the tune," he said. "I've got all the bits, but they won't go."

Buck leaned back in his rocking chair. It creaked. He rocked, slowly. Creak, creak. Creak, creak.

"I'm going to walk down to Grandpappy's Pool," he said, "and see if the fish are in a talking mood. You can come along, if you like. Bring the banjo."

Brody stood up at once, with the banjo in his paws.

"Right now?" he said, already halfway down the steps.

"Right now," said Buck. "At my speed."


Chapter Six — Buck's Speed

Buck's speed, it turned out, was very slow indeed.

He walked down the path as he did everything, one careful step at a time, with his fishing rod over his shoulder and his old cap pulled down against the sun. He stopped to look at a spider's web. He stopped to say good morning to the miller's wife, who was sitting outside the Mill. He stopped to watch a dragonfly, until it flew away.

Brody kept going ahead, and then having to stop, and wait, and come back.

He offered to carry the fishing rod, so that Buck could go faster. Buck gave it to him, and thanked him, and went exactly as fast as before. He offered to carry the bait tin as well. Buck gave him that too, and went no faster at all. By the time they reached Grandpappy's Bridge, Brody was carrying the rod, the bait tin, the banjo and his own rucksack, and Buck was carrying nothing whatsoever, and had stopped in the middle of the bridge to watch the minnows.

"It's only a little further," said Brody, for the third time.

"Then we'll get there," said Buck, and leaned on the rail. "Look at that. There's a whole family of them, down there in the shadow. See how they hang in the current? Not swimming forwards. Not swimming back. Just keeping still, and letting the creek bring their breakfast to them."

Brody looked down at the minnows, hanging in the clear brown water under the bridge, every one of them pointing the same way.

"They look like they're waiting," he said.

"Do they, now," said Buck, and walked on, and then stopped again almost at once to look at a beetle.

By the time they got to Grandpappy's Pool, Brody felt as if he had been walking for a week. Buck sat down on his favourite rock, very slowly, and cast his line into the deep green water, and leaned back against the warm stone, and sighed with great contentment.

Brody sat down on the grass beside him, with the banjo, and tried not to fidget.

And it was there, at Grandpappy's Pool, with nothing to do and nowhere to hurry to, that his friends found him.

They came one by one along the creek path, because word had got about that Brody was down at the pool with Buck and the banjo. Charlie and Maggie Mae came first. Then Benny and Barney, and Rusty, and Sadie Belle, and Reba with a bag of apples. Then Beau, at a run, and Maverick, gliding down onto a branch over the water.

They all sat down in the grass along the bank. And because Buck was fishing, and you do not make a racket beside a fishing line, they were all very quiet.

It was Benny who noticed it first.

"Brody," he whispered. "Look at Grandpappy Buck."

Brody looked. Buck was sitting on his rock with his eyes half closed and his line in the water, and he was rocking, very slightly, forwards and backwards. Not in a rocking chair. Just rocking, as you do when you have rocked in the same chair for so many years that your body keeps doing it on its own.

Forwards, and back. Forwards, and back. Slowly. Very slowly.

"Count it," whispered Benny.

Brody counted. Forwards, two, three. Back, two, three. Forwards, two, three.

"Threes," said Brody.

"Threes," said Benny, with deep satisfaction. "Just like the tune. And just as slow as Maggie Mae said."

Brody stared at Buck, rocking on his rock. Then he put the banjo on his knee.

He did not play anything. Not yet. First he just rocked, just as Buck was rocking. Forwards, two, three. Back, two, three. Slowly, until the rocking felt as natural as breathing. Until he was not thinking about it at all.

And then he played his four notes. In time with the rocking. Slowly.

And they sounded completely different.


Chapter Seven — One Note at a Time

They sounded right.

Brody played them again. One, two, three, four, forwards and back, slow as a summer evening. And then, because his paws were going at Buck's speed now instead of his own, he found the fifth note. Just one. He did not go hunting for it. It was simply there, waiting, like a step that is there when you are walking down the stairs in the dark.

He stopped, and looked up.

All his friends were sitting very still on the bank, watching him.

"That's it," whispered Charlie. "That's the beginning."

Brody did not try to play the rest. He had learned that much. He played the five notes again, slowly, and stopped, and played them again.

"Rusty," he said quietly. "Whistle me your bit. But slowly. Really slowly. As slow as Grandpappy Buck walks."

Rusty looked surprised. Then he whistled his twiddly bit, very slowly, and as soon as it was slow, everybody could hear that it was not twiddly at all. It was not a wren. It was just a little run of notes, going round, gentle and easy, like water round a stone.

"Oh," said Rusty. "It was too fast. It wasn't twiddly. I was just whistling it twiddly."

Brody found Rusty's notes on the banjo, one at a time. It took him a long while, and he got three of them wrong first. But he did not get cross, and he did not hurry. He just tried again, at Buck's speed, until each note was right.

Then he put them after his five notes. And they fitted.

Beau, who had been lying on his back in the grass, sat up.

"Play that again," he said. "The slow way."

Brody played it again, the slow way.

Beau listened with his black-tufted ears pricked right up, and his blue eyes very round, and when it was finished he was quiet for a moment, which for Beau was a long time.

"Huh," he said at last. "It doesn't need a big finish at all, does it." And he lay back down in the grass and put his paws behind his head, looking rather surprised at himself.

"I told you it was slow," said Maggie Mae, very comfortably, to nobody in particular.

Out on his rock, Great-Grandpappy Buck went on fishing, with his eyes half closed, and did not say a word. But Maverick, who could see his face from the branch above, said afterwards that he was smiling.

They stayed by the pool all morning. Brody did not get the whole tune. By lunchtime he had the beginning, and Rusty's bit, and nothing else. But it was right, every note of it, and it was slow, and it went in threes.

"Tomorrow," said Brody, packing up the banjo. And for the first time, he did not sound as if tomorrow were too long to wait.


Chapter Eight — The Middle and the End

It took Brody six more days to find the rest of the tune with no name.

Every morning, he went up the path to Buck's lodge for his lesson. And every morning, before they started, Buck sat in his rocking chair and rocked, and Brody sat on the step and rocked with him, until they were both going at the same speed. Then Brody played the part of the tune he had found so far, and Buck listened with his eyes closed, and said nothing, and Brody tried to find the next note.

Some mornings he found two. Some mornings he found none at all, and played his scales instead, up and down, up and down. And on those mornings, he found that he did not mind as much as he would have done a week ago. His scales were getting better. His paws were getting surer. The notes he had already found were getting easier and easier to play, until he could play them without even thinking, like walking along a path you know.

His friends helped, in their own ways.

Sadie Belle came and sat on the porch one morning, and when Brody played the middle part, she shook her head gently.

"It's right," she said. "But it doesn't feel like sitting with somebody yet. You're playing it as if you're worried it will run away. Play it as if it's going to stay."

So Brody played it again, as if it were going to stay. And it sounded kinder.

Barney came and marched up and down the porch singing his marching song, which still was not it, but which made Brody laugh so much that he forgot to be careful, and found the next three notes by accident while he was laughing.

Benny tapped the threes on the porch rail, very quietly, whenever Brody started to hurry.

And on the fourth morning, Reba came running up the path, very out of breath, with flour on her nose.

"I've got it," she panted. "I've got my bit. I was helping the miller's wife with her pies, and she started humming, as she always does when she takes them out of the oven, and I listened properly this time, instead of thinking about the pies. And it isn't her tune at all. The middle of it is Grandpappy Buck's tune. She's heard him humming it past the Mill every evening for years and years, and it's got mixed in with hers, and she never even noticed." She took a big breath. "Only she hums it too fast, because she's always in a hurry to get the pies out."

She hummed the middle part, slowly, at Buck's speed, with her eyes shut so that she would not think about pies. And there it was. The bit Brody had been missing, the bit between Rusty's notes and the end, going gently down and then gently round again.

It took Brody the whole of that morning to find it on the banjo. But he found it.

And Charlie sat on the step below him every single day, waiting for the end.

"You'll know it when you get to it," she said. "It goes up. Like a question."

On the sixth morning, Brody played everything he had found. The four notes, and the fifth, and Rusty's run of notes that went round like water. The middle that felt like sitting with somebody. Barney's accidental three. All of it, slowly, in threes, forwards and back, with Buck rocking beside him.

And when he got to the end of everything he had found, his paw went on, all by itself, without him telling it to. Up one note. And then, softly, up one more.

Like a question.

He stopped, and sat very still, with his paw on the last string.

Charlie had both paws over her mouth.

Brody looked up at Great-Grandpappy Buck.

Buck had stopped rocking.


Chapter Nine — Where It Came From

"Play it again," said Buck. "All of it. From the start."

So Brody played it again. The whole of it, from the four notes to the question at the end, slowly, in threes, the way it was meant to be played. He did not hurry once. He played it as if it were going to stay.

When he had finished, Buck sat very quietly in his rocking chair for a while, looking out over the meadow.

"Well, I'll be," he said at last, softly. "Now I know where that came from."

"Where?" said Brody.

Buck looked down at him over his spectacles.

"You," he said.

Brody stared at him.

"You weren't much bigger than a pumpkin," said Buck. "Your folks brought you up to the porch one summer evening, and set you down on that very step, and you sat there humming to yourself. Not a song anybody had taught you. Just a little tune of your own, going up and down, as little ones do. And right at the end of it, it went up. Like you were asking something." He smiled. "I liked it so much I picked it up on the banjo that same night, so it wouldn't get lost. And I've been playing it ever since, on and off, whenever my fingers went wandering. But I never gave it a name. And somewhere along the way, I forgot whose it was."

Brody looked down at the little banjo in his paws.

"It was mine," he said slowly. "All along."

"It was yours," said Buck. "And now you've gone and fetched it back."

Brody did not say anything for a while. He did not need to. Sadie Belle, sitting at the end of the step, wiped her eyes very quickly, and pretended she had something in them.

That evening, everybody came to the porch, because word had gone round, as word always did. The miller came, and the miller's wife, in the cart. Granny Opal came along the creek path in her shawl. Brody's folks came and stood at the back, by the steps, where Brody could see them. And all his friends sat along the porch boards in a row, very quietly, with their paws in their laps.

Brody had never played for so many folk before. His paws felt bigger than ever, and his mouth had gone dry. For a moment, standing up in front of everybody, he very nearly played it fast, just to get it over with.

Then he looked at Buck, rocking slowly in his chair. And he sat down on the step instead of standing, and rocked too, until he was going at Buck's speed. Forwards, two, three. Back, two, three.

And Brody played the tune for the whole of Hickory Hollow. He played it slowly, sitting on the step, rocking in threes, with Buck rocking in his chair beside him. Nobody hurried him. Nobody needed a big finish. And when he got to the end, and the tune went up, softly, like a question, the whole porch sat and listened to it fade away into the warm summer evening as if it were the answer to something.

Then Great-Grandpappy Buck said, "Give it time, little one. Time's the one thing the mountains have plenty of."


Chapter Ten — Katydids

The children went home in the long blue dusk, along the creek path and across the meadow, with Brody humming the tune all the way, and the others joining in where they knew it, which by now was most of it.

Buck stayed out on the porch.

He sat in his rocking chair for a while, with the big banjo across his knees, and watched the light go out of the sky behind Weather Ridge. The air was soft and warm, and the hay in the meadow smelled sweet, and down in the trees by the creek the katydids had begun to sing, as they do on late summer nights.

Katy-did. Katy-didn't. Katy-did.

Three notes. Over and over, from every tree in the Hollow, slow and steady, in the dark. Down by the creek, the water ran over the stones with a sound like somebody very quietly turning the pages of a book. Up on the ridge, the last of the light faded to grey, and then to nothing, and the warm night settled over the meadow, soft and deep.

From somewhere across the meadow, very faint, came the sound of somebody humming on the way home. It was a bear-sized hum, a little out of tune, but slow. Very slow. In threes. It went up at the end, and then it was gone round the bend of the path.

Buck listened to them for a while. Then he put his old fingers on the strings, and played the tune with no name, once through, very softly, from the four notes at the beginning to the question at the end, so that his fingers would know the way back to it next time.

Then he sat still, and rocked.

Forwards, two, three. Back, two, three.

Out in the trees, the katydids kept time with him. Katy-did. Katy-didn't. Katy-did. On and on, all down the valley, as they had every late summer night for longer than even Buck could remember.

After a while, Buck said to nobody at all, "Brody's Tune."

It seemed to be the right name. The katydids did not disagree.

He rocked a little longer, until his eyes grew heavy. And then he gathered up the banjo, and went in, and left the porch to the katydids, who sang the whole valley to sleep.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
