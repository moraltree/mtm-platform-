Chapter One — The Hottest Day

The last day of that long hot week was the hottest of all.

It was a Saturday, in the first week of September, and by the time the sun had cleared Sunrise Ridge, the whole of Hickory Hollow was already shimmering. The air above Fern Meadow wobbled like water. The leaves on the hickory trees hung straight down. Silver Creek, which had been running lower and lower all summer, was so low now that it was hardly a creek at all, just a thin bright ribbon of water winding between dry grey stones, with whole stretches of its bed lying open to the sky.

And Benny and Barney were standing in the middle of it.

They were standing on the dry stones, right in the middle of the creek bed, just below Grandpappy's Bridge, where on any ordinary day the water would have been up to Benny's chin. Benny had his notebook. Barney had a stick. And they were both looking downstream, towards the bend where the creek ran away out of sight, past the Mill, past the Beaver Ponds, and out of the Hollow altogether.

"Where does it go?" said Barney.

"I was thinking about that half the night," said Benny.

He had, too. Ever since the evening before, when the whole Hollow had sat by the creek with their paws in the water, and he had watched the low water sliding away down the valley in the dusk, Benny had not been able to stop thinking about it. Where it went. What it passed. What was at the other end.

"Grandpappy Buck says it runs on past the place where the low pass turns off round Weather Ridge," said Benny, "and down through the gap at the very end of the Hollow, and out into the wide country beyond. And then into another creek. And then a river." He looked at his notebook, where he had drawn a long wriggly line that ran off the edge of the page. "And then he doesn't know."

Barney looked at the wriggly line.

"We could go," he said.

Benny looked at him.

"Not all the way," said Barney quickly. "Not to the river. Just to the edge. Just to where it leaves the Hollow. To see." He swung his stick. "Folk go as far as the low pass. Nobody goes on past it, down the creek to the very end. Have you ever been to the very end?"

Benny thought about it.

"No," he said slowly. "There's never been a reason to."

"There's a reason now," said Barney. "We want to see."

They stood in the dry creek bed, in the blazing morning, and looked downstream at the bend.

"We'd have to ask Grandpappy Buck," said Benny.

"Then let's ask," said Barney, and was already scrambling up the bank.


Chapter Two — Not Alone

Great-Grandpappy Buck was sitting on his porch, in what shade there was, with a jug of cold spring water beside him, which Rusty had carried down from the woods that morning.

He listened to the brothers without saying anything. Benny explained, very carefully, with the notebook. Barney explained, very fast, with the stick. When they had both finished, Buck sat quietly for a while, rocking, looking out over the shimmering meadow towards the far end of the Hollow, where the ridges came together and the creek ran out through the gap.

"The end of the Hollow," he said at last. "That's a long way for young legs. On the hottest day of a hot summer."

"We'd take water," said Benny. "And keep to the shade."

"It's past the Beaver Ponds," said Buck. "Past where the low pass turns off. Past the last of the meadows. It's wild down there. Nobody's cut that path in years." He rocked. "I'd not want you going on your own. Not two young beavers, by yourselves, that far from home."

The brothers' faces fell.

"But," said Buck, "I'd not want you not to go, either. Not if you've a mind to see." He looked at them over his spectacles. "So you'll go with a grown-up. And with your friends. And you'll be back well before dark."

Barney whooped. Benny wrote it down.

It turned out that a great many folk wanted to go to the end of the Hollow, once they heard about it.

Charlie wanted to go, because she had never been further than the Beaver Ponds in her life. Maggie Mae wanted to go, because Charlie was going, and because somebody ought to bring a proper lunch. Rusty wanted to go because it was an expedition, and there was nothing Rusty liked better than an expedition, unless it was a shortcut. Brody wanted to go because his friends were going. Reba wanted to go because of the lunch. Sadie Belle wanted to go because she had heard there were wildflowers down there that grew nowhere else in the Hollow. Beau wanted to go because everybody else was going and he could not bear to be left out of anything.

And Maverick said that he would fly ahead and scout the way, and report back, and be home well before dark, naturally.

Rusty's father said he would come, and so did Sadie Belle's mother, who knew the old paths better than anybody.

Then there was the packing.

Benny made a list, of course. WATER. LUNCH. HATS. NOTEBOOK. PENCIL. SPARE PENCIL. He read it out on Grandpappy's Bridge, and everybody packed accordingly, more or less.

Barney packed his drum.

"No," said everybody.

"In case we need to signal," said Barney.

"No," said everybody again, and Barney took the drum home, and returned with a very long stick instead, which he said was for pointing at things, and which turned out to be extremely useful for pointing at things all day.

Reba packed a second lunch, in case something happened to the first lunch, and then a small third lunch, in case something happened to the second. Charlie packed a daisy chain for luck. And Brody, who had not been told to pack anything, turned up with his little banjo on his back, because, he said, you never knew when you might want a tune.

And so, a little before nine o'clock, with the sun already hot on their backs, ten youngsters and two grown-ups set off along the creek, downstream, towards the end of the Hollow.


Chapter Three — Downstream

It was a long walk, and a hot one, and a wonderful one.

They went past the Mill, where the miller's wife waved from her open door, and the great wheel stood still for lack of water. They went past the Beaver Ponds, which were lower than anybody had ever seen them, with a wide rim of drying mud all round the edges, and the beaver lodges standing up out of the water far higher than usual.

Barney stopped and looked at them.

"Our pond's gone shy," he said.

"It'll come back up," said Benny. "When it rains." But he looked at the pond for a while before they went on.

They passed the place where the low pass turned off, curving away round the end of Weather Ridge towards the next valley over. And then they did not turn off. They went straight on, along the creek, past the last of the meadows, past the last of the cottages, into a part of the Hollow that hardly anybody ever came to.

It was wild down there, as Buck had said. The path was overgrown. The trees grew close and tall and old, and their shade was deep and green, the coolest it had been all week. The creek, what was left of it, ran thin and quiet between big mossy boulders, and ferns grew out of every crack.

The old path was hard to follow. Twice it vanished altogether under brambles and fallen leaves, and twice everybody stopped, and looked about, and did not know which way to go. The first time, Sadie Belle found it again, by bending down very low and spotting the faint narrow line through the ferns where the deer still came and went. The second time, Maverick came gliding down out of the trees and landed on a stump and pointed with one wing, and said, "That way. Round the big grey rock. I can see it from above," and was very pleased to be useful.

There was a mossy bank that Barney slid down on his bottom, by accident the first time and on purpose the next four, until Rusty's father said that was enough sliding for one expedition.

There was a dry gully across the path, wide and stony, and Beau crouched at the edge of it and wiggled his hindquarters, getting ready to leap it in a single bound.

"Round," said Sadie Belle's mother firmly. "Not over."

So Beau went round, with great dignity, by the long way, and tripped over a root on the far side, and fell into a clump of ferns anyway, and lay there laughing with ferns in his ears.

There was a fallen tree across the path, much too big to move, and the grown-ups helped everybody climb over it, one at a time, and Brody knelt down beside it so that the smallest ones could use his back for a step, which he said he did not mind in the least, though Reba was carrying three lunches.

And all the way, Benny drew. He drew the path in his notebook, every turn of it, every big rock, every fallen tree, so that, he said, they could find their way home again. And Rusty, walking beside him, kept stopping to look at every bend and every boulder, and saying things like, "If you cut across from that rock to that one..." and then, catching Rusty's father's eye, "but not today."

Sadie Belle found her wildflowers, in a damp shady hollow under a cliff of grey rock: tall spikes of deep, bright blue, standing up in the shade.

"Great blue lobelia," said Sadie Belle's mother softly. "It likes the wet places. You'll not see it anywhere else in the Hollow."

Sadie Belle did not pick any. She just knelt down beside them and looked.

Maverick came gliding back down through the trees again, landing on a branch with his feathers all ruffled.

"I can see the gap," he said. "Where the creek goes out. It's not far now. And beyond it..." He stopped.

"Beyond it what?" said Barney.

Maverick shook his feathers.

"You'll see," he said, and took off again.

They stopped for lunch on a flat rock in the shade, and Maggie Mae unpacked the most proper lunch anybody had ever eaten on an expedition: cold cornbread, and cheese, and apples, and a whole jar of honey, and a stone bottle of spring water that was still nearly cold. Reba said it was the best lunch of her life, and then unpacked her own second lunch to be on the safe side, and was too busy eating to say anything else for some time.

Afterwards, while everybody rested in the shade with their paws in the thin cool trickle of the creek, Brody took out his little banjo and played Buck's Speed, very quietly, in threes, and the tune went drifting away down the creek ahead of them, round the bend, towards wherever they were going.

And then they went on, round one last bend, up one last steep bank.

And came to the end of the Hollow.


Chapter Four — The Edge

The end of the Hollow was a narrow knuckle of rock, mossy and rough, jutting out from the side of the gap at the very top of the falls, where the two ridges came together and the creek ran out between them.

They came out onto it from the trees, one after another. And Sadie Belle's mother stopped everybody a good long step back from the lip, and they stood there, in a row, and looked.

Below them, the creek went tumbling down over the rocks in a little series of falls, thin and silver in the low summer water. And below the falls, the land just opened. It was a great wide country of hills and woods and fields, rolling away and away into a blue haze, further than any of them had ever seen in their lives. And winding through the middle of it, far below, shining in the afternoon sun, was the creek, joining another creek, and the two of them together running on, wider and wider, until, right at the very edge of seeing, they met something else.

Something wide, and slow, and silver, curling away into the haze.

"A river," breathed Benny.

The falls made a soft, steady roar below them, even with so little water in them, and a cool damp breath came up off the spray, the only cool air for miles. Far out over the wide country, two big birds were turning slow circles in the haze, so far off they were no bigger than specks. And somewhere down there, very faint, so faint you could not be sure you had heard it at all, a bell rang, once, from somewhere none of them had ever been.

Nobody else spoke. Rusty, who had found every shortcut in the Hollow, stood with his mouth open. Brody stood very still, with Charlie holding on to his paw. Beau, for once in his life, could not think of a single thing to say. Even Reba forgot about the lunch.

Maverick landed on the rock beside them, and folded his wings.

"I've been over Weather Ridge," he said quietly. "To the next valley over, and back. But I've never come down the creek this way. You can't see this from over the ridge." He looked out at the blue distance. "It just goes on."

Barney took a step forward. Rusty's father put a paw on his shoulder, gently, and Barney stopped.

"It's all right," said Barney. "I'm not going anywhere. I just want to see it properly."

He stood there, at the very end of everything he had ever known, and looked out at the great wide world with his eyes very round. And beside him, Benny stood with his notebook in his paws, and his pencil still behind his ear, and did not write a single word. There was nothing big enough to write.

Then, after a while, Benny took the pencil from behind his ear. He did not write words. He drew. He drew the knuckle of rock, and the falls, and the wide country, and the two creeks joining, and right at the very edge of the page, the river, curling away. He drew it very carefully, and very small, because the page was not big enough for it.

"So we don't forget what it looks like," he said.

They stood there until the sun had moved a good way across the sky.

Then Sadie Belle's mother said, very gently, that it was time to start for home, if they were to be back before dark.

And they turned, one by one, and went back into the trees, with Rusty's father walking last of all, right behind Benny and Barney, who kept turning to look until the trees had closed in and there was nothing to see but green.


Chapter Five — The Question

The walk home was quieter than the walk out.

Everybody was tired, and hot, and full of what they had seen. But it was more than that. Something had changed, on that knuckle of rock, and everybody had felt it, and nobody quite knew how to say it.

It was Charlie who said it, in the end. She was walking beside Benny and Barney on the old overgrown path, under the deep green trees, and she had been quiet for a long way.

"Are you going to go?" she said.

The brothers looked at her.

"Go where?" said Barney.

"There," said Charlie. "To the river. And beyond. Into all that." She looked at her feet. "You looked at it like you wanted to go."

There was a pause on the path.

Everybody had heard. Everybody had stopped walking. Maggie Mae had stopped, and Brody, and Rusty, and Reba, and Sadie Belle, and Beau. They were all looking at the brothers.

"Not now," said Benny honestly. "We're not old enough. And we wouldn't know the way. And Grandpappy Buck's going to mend the big dam below the ponds this winter, and he says we can help, on the bank, fetching and carrying and measuring, while the grown-ups do the water work. It's our first proper job."

"But one day," said Barney. "Maybe. When we're bigger." He looked down the path, towards the end of the Hollow, and the river, and the great wide world. "I'd like to see where the river goes."

Nobody answered.

Charlie's eyes had filled up. She did not want them to, but they had.

"I don't want you to go," she said, in a very small voice.

Benny knelt down on the path in front of her, so that they were the same height.

"We're not going," he said. "Not for years and years. And if we ever do go, one day, a long way off, we'll come back and tell you every single thing we saw."

Charlie wiped her eyes on her sleeve.

"Promise?" she said.

"Promise," said Benny.

Then everybody looked at Barney.

Barney did not answer straight away. He looked back down the path, the way they had come, towards the river he could not see any more. He stood there quite a while, with his pointing stick in his paw, not pointing at anything.

Then he looked at Charlie's face. And he nodded.

"Promise," said Barney.

They walked on. Maggie Mae took Charlie's paw on one side, and Brody walked close on the other, and after a while, without anybody saying anything about it, Brody lifted Charlie up onto his back, because her legs were tired.

Barney walked at the front with his long stick, pointing at things, and Rusty walked beside him, and the two of them found a beetle the colour of a jewel, and a stone with a hole right through it, which Barney gave to Charlie, up on Brody's back, to keep.

But the question stayed with them, all the long way home, walking beside them on the path like an extra friend.


Chapter Six — The Hickory Tree

They got back to Grandpappy's Bridge in the late afternoon, hot and tired and dusty, with the sun sliding down towards Weather Ridge.

And everybody else was there.

Word gets about in Hickory Hollow. The whole Hollow knew that ten youngsters had gone to the end of the Hollow, and the whole Hollow had come out to meet them coming home. Granny Opal was there, and the miller, and the miller's wife in the cart, and the rabbits, all nine of them, and Brody's folks, and Rusty's mother, and a great many others besides. And Great-Grandpappy Buck was standing at the end of the bridge, leaning on his stick, waiting.

"Well," he said, when they came up the bank. "You've been to the end."

"We saw the river," said Barney. "Grandpappy Buck, we saw a river."

Buck nodded slowly.

"I saw it once," he said. "When I was about your size. My own grandpappy took me." He was quiet, remembering. "It's a sight, isn't it."

Benny opened his notebook and showed him the drawing: the falls, and the two creeks, and the little river curling off the edge of the page. And all the pages before it, with every turn of the old path drawn in, every rock and fallen tree, all the way there.

Buck looked at it for a good while, holding the notebook very carefully in his old paws, turning the pages one by one.

"Well, now," he said softly, and handed it back, and did not say anything else about it. But he kept looking at Benny afterwards, over his spectacles, as if he were seeing something a long way off.

It was then that Benny noticed the tree.

It was the big old hickory beside Buck's lodge, the one that had stood there longer than anybody, longer even than Buck, so old and so broad that it took five of them holding paws to reach round its trunk. Its great branches spread right out over the bank, and over the creek. They had climbed it, and played under it, and sat in its shade, a thousand times.

And on one of its branches, the one that reached out furthest over the water, a single leaf had turned.

Just one. The only one, on the whole great green tree. While every other leaf hung green and still in the heat, that one leaf had gone gold, bright, buttery gold, from tip to stalk.

"Look," said Benny quietly.

Everybody looked.

"First leaf of autumn," said Buck. "That old tree always turns one leaf before all the others, after a hot dry summer. Just one. To let us know." He looked up at it. "Summer's nearly done."

And as if the old tree had been waiting for them to see it, the wind came.


Chapter Seven — The Wind from the North

It came from the north, over High Ridge, the tallest of the three, where nobody could see it coming.

It was not a storm. There was no thunder, and no great dark cloud. It was just a wind. A long, cool, steady wind, the first cool wind in weeks, pouring down over the ridge and into the Hollow like cold water poured into a hot bath.

It went through the hickory trees, and every leaf that had hung still all week began to move, all at once, rustling and turning, silver and green. It went across Fern Meadow, and the pale crackly grass bent and rippled in long waves. It went over Grandpappy's Bridge, and over everybody standing on it, and blew their fur up the wrong way, and everybody gasped, and laughed, and turned their faces into it.

"Oh," said Maggie Mae, holding her bow on with one paw, and her eyes closed. "Oh, that's wonderful."

"It's cold," said Barney, astonished. "It's actually cold."

All over the bridge, everybody was turning round and round in it, with their arms out. Granny Opal pulled her shawl round her shoulders for the first time in a week, and laughed out loud. The little rabbits ran in circles. The miller took off his hat and let the wind blow through his ears. And Reba stood at the very top of the bridge with her eyes shut and her nose in the air, sniffing, and said that it smelled of apples, and woodsmoke, and hickory nuts, and pumpkins, and everything good that was coming.

It was not really cold. But after the long hot week, it felt like the coolest thing in the world.

Great-Grandpappy Buck stood at the end of the bridge, with his old cap held in his paw and the wind ruffling the fur on his head, and his eyes closed, and smiled.

"That," he said, "is the end of summer."

Then, behind the wind, it began to rain.

Not the great roaring summer rain of the storm at the end of July. A soft, light, cool rain. The kind that comes at the turn of the seasons, falling gently and steadily out of a grey sky, pattering on the leaves and on the bridge and on the dry stones of the creek bed, where every drop made a little dark spot that dried, and then did not dry, and then joined up with the next.

Nobody ran for shelter.

Nobody wanted to. They stood on Grandpappy's Bridge, the whole Hollow, old and young, with their faces turned up to the cool soft rain, after the long hot week, and let it fall on them.

And under the bridge, in the dry creek bed, for the first time in weeks, a little trickle of water began to grow.


Chapter Eight — Promises

They gathered under the old hickory tree to get out of the rain, in the end, because it went on, soft and steady, and the evening was coming on.

"No thunder in it," said Rusty's father, looking up at the sky. "If there were, we'd all be indoors."

The great tree's branches spread out so wide, and so thick with leaves, that underneath it was almost dry, and smelled of wet bark and earth and green things. Everybody squeezed in. The whole Hollow, under one tree, shoulder to shoulder, damp and laughing, with the smell of the rain all round them and the first cool air of the year on their fur. Buck in his rocking chair, which Brody and his father carried out from the porch. Granny Opal on a stool. The rabbits in a heap. And all the youngsters, sitting on the dry ground among the roots, in a ring, close together, with the rain pattering on the leaves above them.

It was Barney who started the promises.

"Benny made a promise," he said, "on the way home. To Charlie. And so did I." He looked round the ring. "I think everybody should make one. Here. Under the tree. So we remember."

Everybody thought about it.

"I promise," said Maggie Mae, "to keep having picnics. Even when it's cold. Indoor ones."

"I promise," said Rusty, "to find a shortcut to the end of the Hollow. A proper one. So it doesn't take all day next time."

"I promise," said Reba, "to bring the lunch."

Brody thought for a good while.

"I promise," he said slowly, "to keep playing Buck's Speed. Every week. So it never gets lost again."

Then the grown-ups wanted to make promises too, which nobody had expected. Rusty's father promised to go to the end of the Hollow again next summer, and take anybody who wanted to come. And Granny Opal, from her stool, said that she was too old to promise anything very much, but that she would knit every single one of them a scarf before the first frost, in whatever colour they liked, and that Beau could have two, because he was always cold.

"I'm never cold," said Beau.

"You will be," said Granny Opal comfortably.

Charlie promised to be brave, when things changed, even if she did not want them to.

Then everybody looked at Benny and Barney.

The brothers looked at each other.

"We promise," said Benny, "to stay. For now. For a long while. And to help Grandpappy Buck mend the big dam this winter. On the bank."

"And one day, maybe," said Barney, "when we're bigger, we might go and see where the river goes." He looked at his brother. "But not till Charlie's bigger than me."

Everybody looked at Charlie, who was the smallest of all of them by a very long way.

"That's never," said Charlie, delighted.

"Exactly," said Barney.

And the whole ring laughed, under the dripping tree, so that Benny had to stop writing the promises down in his notebook until he could hold the pencil straight again.

Maverick made his promise last, while there was still a little light in the grey sky: to fly a little way down the creek past the falls, one day, in the daylight, and tell them what was beyond the next bend. Then he flew home to his roost, before the dark. And the grown-ups lit the lanterns, and hung them from the lowest branches of the old hickory, well clear of the leaves, so that a soft light hung under the tree, and the rain fell silver past it.


Chapter Nine — What Buck Said

For a while after that, there was only the rain, pattering on the leaves above them.

Great-Grandpappy Buck sat in his rocking chair at the foot of the old hickory, with his paws folded on his stick, and looked at Benny and Barney, sitting side by side in the middle of the ring, with their paws touching.

He was remembering a knuckle of mossy rock at the top of the falls, a very long time ago, and a young beaver about Barney's size standing on it, looking out at a silver river curling away into the haze. And a big old paw resting on his shoulder, very gently, the whole time he looked. His grandpappy's paw. He could still feel the weight of it.

He smiled to himself, in the lantern light.

And Great-Grandpappy Buck said, in his slow, warm voice, "Home isn't a place. It's the folk who are glad you came back."


Chapter Ten — The Leaf

It was nearly dark when the grown-ups began to gather everybody up for home.

The rain had eased to almost nothing, just a soft fine mist that hung in the lantern light. The wind had dropped to a cool, gentle breath. And all over the Hollow, after the long hot week, everything smelled of rain, and wet earth, and the first faint edge of autumn.

Benny and Barney went out onto Grandpappy's Bridge, while everybody else was saying goodnight under the tree. Rusty's father came with them, carrying his lantern, and stood beside them at the rail.

Below them, the creek was running again.

Not much. Not yet. But where that morning there had been only dry grey stones and a thin bright ribbon of water, now there was a little creek again, small and quick and busy, finding its old way between the stones in the lantern light, filling up the little dry hollows one by one.

"It'll take a lot more rain than this to fill it," said Benny. "But it's started."

"And it's going," said Barney. "Down past the Mill. And the Beaver Ponds. And out through the gap. And down to the river."

"And on," said Benny.

"And on," said Barney.

Above them, the last breath of the north wind stirred the old hickory, out on its long branch over the water. And something let go.

It came drifting down out of the dark, turning over and over in the soft air, gold in the lantern light as it passed. It was the leaf. The one gold leaf, from the branch that reached furthest out over the creek. And it fell, very slowly, past the rail of the bridge, right in front of them, and landed on the water below.

The brothers leaned on the rail and looked down at it.

It turned round once, on the little new creek, as if it were looking up at them. And then the current took it, gently, and it began to float away. Under the bridge. Round the first stones. Out of the lantern light, into the dark, towards the Mill, and the Beaver Ponds, and the gap at the end of the Hollow, and the wide country, and the far silver river curling away into the haze.

Benny and Barney watched it go, until they could not see it any more.

They did not follow it. Not tonight. Not for years and years.

Benny took Barney's paw on the rail of Grandpappy's Bridge. And the two brothers stood there together, in the middle of the Hollow, with Rusty's father's lantern beside them and the old hickory dripping softly over their heads, and the little creek running on beneath their feet, and stayed exactly where they were.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
