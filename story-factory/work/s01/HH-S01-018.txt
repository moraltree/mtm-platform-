Chapter One — The Day Off

On a Wednesday in the middle of May, Charlie woke up with the best idea she had ever had.

She had been thinking about it for a week. Ever since the evening she had sat on Maggie Mae's garden wall and watched Maggie Mae go in and out of her front door seven times in one hour, with baskets and parcels and jugs and a watering can, and come back each time looking a little more tired, and say, every time, "Nearly done now, Charlie. Just one more thing."

There was always one more thing.

Maggie Mae was Charlie's best friend, and something like a big sister. She was a young skunk, black and white and elegant, with emerald-green eyes and a pink polka-dot bow, and she lived in Rose Arch Cottage, which had roses climbing all over the front of it and the prettiest garden in the whole of Hickory Hollow. She had looked after Charlie ever since Charlie was small enough to ride in her basket. She had taught her to whistle, and to sew a button, and to say "please" and "thank you" in a voice people could actually hear.

And she never, ever, had a day off.

So Charlie was going to give her one.

She was at Rose Arch Cottage before breakfast, knocking on the round green door with both paws.

"Charlie!" said Maggie Mae, opening it in her apron, with flour on her nose. "Whatever's the matter?"

"Nothing's the matter," said Charlie, bouncing. "It's a surprise. A big-sister surprise. For you. Today, you are having a day off."

"A day off?" said Maggie Mae.

"A whole day. You're going to sit in your garden chair, in the sun, with your feet up, and drink lemonade, and read a book, and do absolutely nothing. And I," said Charlie, drawing herself up to her full height, which was not very much, "am going to do all your jobs."

Maggie Mae looked at her for a moment. Then she looked over her shoulder, into the cottage, where a basket stood half-packed on the kitchen table and a list was pinned to the wall beside the door.

"Oh, Charlie," she said. "That's a lovely thought. But I've got rather a lot of jobs today. It's Wednesday."

"I know," said Charlie. "That's why it's a surprise."

Maggie Mae opened her mouth to argue. Then she looked down at Charlie's small, determined, shining face, and closed it again. And something in her own face went soft.

"Well," she said slowly. "Perhaps just this once."

She took the list down from the wall beside the door, and handed it to Charlie.

Charlie looked at it. It was written in Maggie Mae's neat, careful hand, on a piece of pale blue card. It said:

WEDNESDAY.
Buck. Clock.
Mill. Line.
Opal. Bridge, 11.
Rabbits. Story, after lunch.
Rusty. Scarf.
Roses.
Me. Six.

That was all.

"That's easy," said Charlie. "That's hardly anything. Seven words. Well, fourteen."

Maggie Mae looked at her with an odd little smile.

"Is it?" she said.


Chapter Two — Buck. Clock.

Charlie settled Maggie Mae in her garden chair first, because that was the most important part. She put a cushion behind her back, and a footstool under her feet, and a straw hat on her head, and a glass of lemonade at her elbow, and a book in her lap, and tucked a blanket round her knees, even though it was May and quite warm.

"There," she said. "Don't move."

"I won't," said Maggie Mae, a little uncertainly.

Then Charlie set off with the list.

Buck. Clock.

That was easy. Great-Grandpappy Buck had a clock. Everybody knew it. A tall old clock in the corner of his front room, with a brass face and a long slow tick. Maggie Mae must want her to check what the time was. Or tell Buck the time. Or possibly ask Buck to tell her the time, in case Maggie Mae's own clock had stopped.

Charlie ran all the way to the lodge, across Grandpappy's Bridge, and up onto the porch, and knocked.

"Good morning, Grandpappy!" she said, when Buck opened the door. "It's half past eight! I'm doing Maggie Mae's jobs today, and the first one is your clock, and I've done it."

Buck looked down at her over his spectacles.

"Half past eight?" he said. "Well, thank you, little one." He turned, and looked at the tall old clock in the corner of his front room. Charlie looked too.

The clock said ten past four.

Its long brass pendulum, which usually swung slowly back and forth behind its little glass window, was hanging perfectly still.

"Oh," said Charlie. "It's stopped."

"It has," said Buck. "It always stops on a Wednesday morning. Runs for exactly one week, that clock, and then it wants winding." He put one paw to the small of his back. "And the key goes in right at the top, there, and these old legs aren't what they were for standing on chairs. So every Wednesday morning, regular as the clock itself, Maggie Mae comes round and climbs up on the chair and winds it for me."

Charlie stared at him.

"Every Wednesday?"

"Every Wednesday," said Buck, "for three years. Ever since I fell off the chair myself." He smiled. "She'd never let me try again."

Charlie looked at the tall clock, and the key on its hook beside it, and the chair.

She climbed up on the chair. It was a very tall clock, and she was a very small chipmunk. She stood on her tiptoes on the chair, and stretched, and reached, and could not get anywhere near the keyhole.

She got down again, a little pink.

"Benny," she said. "Benny's tall. I'll fetch Benny."

She fetched Benny. Benny climbed on the chair, with Buck holding it steady, and wound the clock, carefully, counting the turns, and set the hands right, and started the pendulum swinging. Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.

Buck sighed happily, and sat down in his armchair.

"Lovely," he said. "I do miss it when it stops. It's like having a friend in the room who's gone quiet."

Charlie looked at Benny, and Benny looked at Charlie.

"I didn't know Maggie Mae did that," said Charlie slowly.

"Nor did I," said Benny.

"Most folk don't," said Buck comfortably, picking up his newspaper. "She's not one for telling."


Chapter Three — Mill. Line.

Mill. Line.

Charlie stood on the Old Mill step and thought about it for a long time.

A line. A line could be a queue, like the line of families waiting for flour. A line could be a line of writing. A line could be the line on Benny's ruler. Or a fishing line. Or a washing line.

The miller was out, at a farm along the creek. But the miller's wife was in the kitchen behind the Mill, sitting in her big chair by the stove, with a shawl round her shoulders and a cushion behind her bad back, shelling peas.

"Hello, dear," she said. "Are you Maggie Mae today? She said you might be."

Charlie's mouth fell open. "She told you?"

"She popped a note under the door last night," said the miller's wife, smiling. "She always lets me know if she can't come, so I don't worry. She said you'd be coming instead, and I wasn't to let on." She waved a pea pod at the back door. "It's the line, dear. Out the back. It always needs bringing in, Wednesdays."

Charlie went out of the back door.

There, in the little yard behind the Mill, strung between two posts, was a long washing line, high up, sagging with sheets and towels and pillowcases and the miller's enormous floury shirts, all flapping in the May breeze, and all, very clearly, dry.

And there, on the ground by the post, was a basket, and a prop, and a pegbag.

Charlie looked up at the washing line. It was a very high washing line. It was higher than the clock.

"Oh," she said. "Of course. Her back."

She ran back to the kitchen door.

"Does Maggie Mae bring your washing in every Wednesday?" she said.

"Every Wednesday," said the miller's wife. "And she puts it out every Monday. And she folds it, and puts it away in the press, because I can't bend to the bottom drawers." She smiled. "Since last autumn, when my back went. She just turned up one morning with a pegbag. Never said a word about it."

Charlie went back out into the yard, and looked up at the high line, and thought very hard.

Then she went to find Brody.

Brody was in Fern Meadow, lying on his back in the grass, watching a beetle climb a stalk. He came at once. He was the tallest youngster in the Hollow, and he could reach the high line without even stretching. He unpegged every sheet and every towel and every enormous shirt, and Charlie stood at the bottom with the basket and the pegbag, and caught the pegs as he dropped them, and folded everything, very carefully, the way she had seen Maggie Mae fold things at Rose Arch, with the corners all neat.

Then they carried the basket in, and Charlie put everything away in the press, even the bottom drawers, which she could reach very easily, being small.

"There," said the miller's wife, beaming from her chair. "Just like Maggie Mae does it."

"Not quite," said Charlie honestly, looking at one of the miller's shirts, which she had folded into a sort of lumpy square. "But nearly."

As they walked back along the creek path, Brody said, "Charlie? How long has Maggie Mae been doing that?"

"Since last autumn," said Charlie. "Every week. And she never said."

They walked on for a while without talking.

"How many other things," said Brody slowly, "do you think she's never said?"

Charlie looked down at the blue card in her paw.

There were still five lines to go.


Chapter Four — Opal. Bridge, 11.

Opal. Bridge, 11.

That one, Charlie understood. Or she thought she did.

Granny Opal was the old opossum who lived at the far end of the creek path, under the elderberry thicket. She had come to the Hollow at the end of last autumn, and she knitted mittens for anybody whose paws were cold, and she was very shy. Charlie liked her very much. She knitted faster than anybody Charlie had ever seen.

"She must want wool," said Charlie. "Maggie Mae must be taking her wool, at the bridge, at eleven. I'll take her some wool."

So she went to Rose Arch first, very quietly, in case Maggie Mae was asleep in the chair. Maggie Mae was not asleep. She was sitting bolt upright in the chair, with the book open on her lap at the very same page it had been open at when Charlie left, looking at her roses, and drumming her fingers on the arm of the chair.

"How's it going?" she said, rather quickly. "Is everything all right? Did Buck's clock...?"

"It's fine," said Charlie. "Everything's fine. You're on your day off. Stop thinking about it." She found a basket of spare wool in the kitchen, and took the whole lot, and went out again before Maggie Mae could ask any more questions.

She got to Grandpappy's Bridge at five to eleven, with the basket of wool, and waited.

At exactly eleven o'clock, a long pale grey face looked out from the bushes at the end of the creek path, on the far side. Then, slowly, nervously, Granny Opal came out, in her knitted shawl, and stopped at the edge of the path. She looked all round her with her bright, shy black eyes. And then she saw Charlie on the bridge, and her face fell a little.

"Oh," she said, in her small creaky voice. "Where's Maggie Mae?"

"She's having a day off," said Charlie proudly. "I'm doing her jobs. I've brought you wool." She held out the basket.

Granny Opal looked at the wool.

"That's very kind of you, dear," she said. "But I've plenty of wool. Folk have been very good." She looked down the creek path behind Charlie, past the bridge, towards the Old Mill and the cottages and all the busy middle of the Hollow, where the doors were opening and folk were coming and going in the May sunshine, and she drew her shawl a little tighter round her shoulders. "It's just that Wednesday's my flour day, you see. At the Mill. And it's a long way through the middle of the Hollow, with everybody about. And I still get a little..." She stopped. "Maggie Mae walks with me. From the bridge to the Mill, and back. Every Wednesday. Just so I'm not on my own."

Charlie stood very still on the bridge, holding the basket of wool that nobody needed.

"Oh," she said.

She put the basket down, very carefully, at the end of the bridge, where she could fetch it later.

Then she walked across to Granny Opal, and held out her paw.

"I'll walk with you," she said. "If that's all right. I'm not as good as Maggie Mae. But I'll walk with you."

Granny Opal looked at the small paw for a moment. Then she took it.

They walked to the Mill together, slowly, through the busy middle of the Hollow, past all the open doors and the folk coming and going. Charlie talked the whole way, about the clock and the washing line and Brody and the beetle, so that Granny Opal did not have to say anything at all, and did not have to look at anybody, and only had to listen. And by the time they got to the Mill, Granny Opal was laughing her small creaky laugh, and had not once looked over her shoulder.

On the way back, she squeezed Charlie's paw.

"You're a lot like her, you know," she said. "Maggie Mae. You notice things."

Charlie thought about the basket of wool, sitting at the end of the bridge, which nobody had needed.

"Not always," she said. "But I'm learning."


Chapter Five — Rabbits. Story, after lunch.

Rabbits. Story, after lunch.

Charlie was very good at stories. She knew a great many of them, and she told them very fast, with all the voices, and a great deal of jumping about.

So after lunch, she went to the rabbit family's burrow at the bottom of Fern Meadow, and the rabbit mother let her in, looking a little surprised, with the littlest rabbit peeping out from behind her.

"Maggie Mae's on her day off," explained Charlie. "So I'm doing the story."

The rabbit mother had seven little ones, and a great many jobs of her own, and she looked very grateful. She settled the littlest rabbit in her bed, in the corner of the burrow, and went off to the other end to do the mending.

Charlie sat down beside the bed.

"Once upon a time," she began, at top speed, "there was a FOX, and he was VERY HUNGRY, and he went running through the woods, RUN RUN RUN, and he met a BEAR, and the bear said GRRRRR..."

She did the bear. She did the fox. She did the running, round and round the burrow. She did a thunderstorm. She did a very loud owl.

When she stopped for breath, she found that the littlest rabbit was sitting bolt upright in bed, with her eyes as round as buttons and her ears flat, and her little nose twitching very fast.

"Did you like it?" said Charlie hopefully.

"It was very exciting," said the littlest rabbit, in a small voice. And then her lip wobbled. "But I'm not sleepy any more. And Maggie Mae's story makes me sleepy."

Charlie looked at her.

"Sleepy?"

"It's after lunch," said the littlest rabbit. "It's my nap. Maggie Mae comes on Wednesdays after lunch, because Mama's got the mending, and she tells me the same story, every time, the slow one, about the little boat, and her voice goes all quiet and soft, and I always fall asleep before the boat gets home." Her lip wobbled harder. "I never find out if the boat gets home."

Charlie sat back on her heels.

So it was not a story the littlest rabbit needed. It was a nap. And a nap needed a quiet voice, and a slow story, and the same one every time, so there were no surprises.

Charlie did not know the story about the little boat.

But she had learned, that winter, on a snowy porch, how to be still.

So she sat down very close to the bed, and folded her paws, and took a slow, quiet breath, the way Sadie Belle had shown her. And then she began again, in the softest, slowest voice she had.

"Once upon a time," she said, "there was a little boat. And it was going home."

She did not know what happened next. So she made it up, slowly. The little boat went down a long, slow, quiet river. Past a tree. Past another tree. Past a sleepy duck, sitting on the bank. The water went lap, lap, lap against its sides. The sun was warm. The boat went slower. And slower.

By the time the little boat had passed the third sleepy duck, the littlest rabbit's eyes had closed.

Charlie went on, very softly, for a little while longer, just to be sure. Then she stopped, and sat very still, and listened to the littlest rabbit breathing, slow and even.

Then she tiptoed out.

"She's asleep," she whispered to the rabbit mother, at the other end of the burrow.

The rabbit mother looked up from her mending, and her whole face went soft with relief.

"Bless you," she whispered. "That's the only hour of the whole week I get to sit down."

Charlie walked back up Fern Meadow very slowly, thinking.

An hour to sit down. Once a week. And Maggie Mae gave it to her, every Wednesday, with a slow story about a little boat.

Charlie wondered if the boat ever did get home.


Chapter Six — Rusty. Scarf.

Rusty. Scarf.

This one, Charlie was certain about. Rusty had a scarf. A long green one, that he wore all winter, and still wore on cold spring mornings, wound round and round his neck with the ends flying out behind him when he ran.

Maggie Mae must have knitted it for him. And now she must be knitting him a new one. Or the old one needed washing. Or...

Charlie found Rusty in Fern Meadow, practising pouncing on a dandelion clock.

"Rusty," she said. "What does Maggie Mae do with your scarf?"

Rusty stopped pouncing. His ears went flat, and he looked very slightly embarrassed.

"She mends it," he said. "Every week or so. I catch it on brambles. When I'm taking shortcuts." He pulled the green scarf out of his pocket, and held it up. There was a large hole in one end, and a long pulled loop of wool, and several burrs stuck in it. "I give it to her, and she mends it, and gives it back. She's been doing it since I was little. It was my grandmother's, you see. It's the only thing I've got of hers." He looked at the scarf. "So I can't get a new one. It's got to be this one."

Charlie took the scarf, very carefully. She had never known that.

"I'll mend it," she said.

Charlie had learned to sew a button. She had not learned to mend a hole. She took the scarf back to Rose Arch, and sat on the back step, where Maggie Mae could not see her from the garden chair, with Maggie Mae's darning needle and a ball of green wool, and tried.

It did not go well.

The hole got smaller. Then it got lumpier. Then, somehow, it got bigger again. The green wool was not quite the same green. The needle went in where it should have come out, and out where it should have gone in, and after half an hour the end of Rusty's grandmother's scarf looked like a small green bird's nest.

Charlie looked at it. Her eyes stung.

Then she went round the side of the cottage to the garden, where Maggie Mae was sitting in her chair, with her book upside down in her lap and her lemonade untouched, staring at the roses, and drumming her fingers again.

"Maggie Mae," said Charlie, in a very small voice. "I know you're having a day off. But I need help."

Maggie Mae sat up so fast that her straw hat fell off.

"What is it?" she said. "What's wrong? Is somebody hurt? Is it the clock?"

Charlie held out the scarf.

Maggie Mae looked at it. Then she looked at Charlie's face. And then, to Charlie's great surprise, she laughed. Not unkindly. With relief, as if something had come undone in her.

"Oh, Charlie," she said. "Give it here. Sit down. I'll show you."

And she did. She showed Charlie how to unpick the bird's nest, gently, without pulling. How to make a little frame of stitches across the hole, like the strings of a loom, and then weave over and under them, slowly, row by row, until the hole was filled. She did not do it for her. She held the scarf, and Charlie held the needle, and Maggie Mae said, "Under. Over. Under. That's it. Gently."

It took an hour. When it was finished, there was a little patch on the end of Rusty's grandmother's scarf, of a very slightly different green, and very slightly lumpy, but whole.

Maggie Mae looked at it, and then at Charlie, and her eyes were bright.

"That's a good mend," she said. "That'll hold."

Charlie looked at her. She had not seen Maggie Mae look so happy all day.

And she began to wonder, for the first time, whether a day off was really what Maggie Mae had wanted at all.


Chapter Seven — Roses.

Roses.

There were only two lines left on the blue card. Charlie took Rusty his scarf, and he held it for a long time without saying anything, and then said, "That's my grandmother's scarf," in a funny voice, and hugged her.

Then she went back to Rose Arch, and stood at the garden gate, and looked at the roses.

Roses.

The roses climbed all over the front of Rose Arch Cottage, up and over the porch and round the windows and across the arch over the gate, which was how the cottage had got its name. They were just coming into bud, hundreds of tight little buds, with the first few beginning to open, pale pink and deep red and creamy white.

What did roses need, on a Wednesday?

Charlie looked at the watering can by the step. She looked at the roses. Water. That must be it. She fetched the can, and filled it at the pump, and was just lifting it up to water the nearest rose, all over its leaves and buds, when a voice said, "Not on the leaves, Charlie."

Charlie jumped.

It was Maggie Mae, leaning out of her garden chair.

"Sorry," said Maggie Mae quickly. "I know. Day off. But roses don't like wet leaves. It gives them spots. Water the roots, at the bottom, under the leaves. Gently." She sat back. "Sorry."

Charlie watered the roots, at the bottom, gently. Then she stood back and looked at the roses again.

"Is that it?" she said. "Is that all roses is? Water?"

Maggie Mae looked at her for a moment. Then she sighed, and put her book down, and got up out of the chair. Charlie opened her mouth to object, but Maggie Mae held up a paw.

"I'm not doing it," she said. "I'm only showing you. That's allowed."

She knelt down by the nearest rose, and gently lifted one of its leaves, and turned it over.

Underneath, there were little green creatures. Dozens of them. Tiny and soft and pale green, clustered on the underside of the leaf, and along the stem, and round the bottom of the newest bud.

"Greenfly," said Maggie Mae. "They suck the sap out of the new buds, and the buds wither, and they never open. Every Wednesday, in spring, I go along every rose, leaf by leaf, and look underneath." She pointed. "And look there."

On the next leaf along, there was a ladybird. Small and red and round and shiny, with black spots, walking slowly along the stem towards the greenfly.

"The ladybirds eat them," said Maggie Mae. "So I don't squash them, or spray them. I just look, every week. If there's too many greenfly, I wash them gently off with a soft cloth and water. If there's ladybirds, I leave them be, and let the ladybirds do the work." She smiled. "Most weeks, the ladybirds do it all, and I just watch."

Charlie knelt beside her and looked at the ladybird, plodding along the stem.

"How long does it take?" she said. "Looking at every leaf?"

"About an hour," said Maggie Mae. "Every Wednesday."

Charlie looked along the front of Rose Arch Cottage, at the hundreds and hundreds of leaves, on the hundreds of stems, climbing all over the porch and the windows and the arch.

She thought about Buck's clock. The washing line at the Mill. The walk to the bridge. The little boat. Rusty's grandmother's scarf. And now the roses, leaf by leaf, every week.

Nobody had ever told her. Nobody had ever seen.

"Maggie Mae," she said slowly. "Does anybody ever do anything for you?"

Maggie Mae looked at her in surprise.

"For me?" she said. Then she laughed a little. "Well, I've got today, haven't I? My big-sister surprise." She looked down at her paws. "Though I'll be honest with you, Charlie. I've been sitting in that chair all day thinking about the clock."

Charlie looked at her.

Then she knelt down next to the roses, and lifted the next leaf, and turned it over, and looked underneath.

"Show me which ones are too many," she said.


Chapter Eight — Me. Six.

They did the roses together, all of them, leaf by leaf, all the way along the porch and round the windows and up the arch over the gate, with Charlie standing on the garden wall for the high ones. Maggie Mae showed her how to tell greenfly from a ladybird's babies, which were strange little black-and-orange creatures that looked like tiny crocodiles, and which ate even more greenfly than the grown-up ladybirds. Charlie found four. She named them all.

It took a little over an hour. When they had finished, the sun was going down towards Weather Ridge, and the light was turning gold.

Charlie climbed down off the wall and looked at the blue card.

There was one line left.

Me. Six.

"What does this one mean?" she said, holding it up. "Who's 'me'? What happens at six?"

Maggie Mae looked at the card, and then she looked away, at the garden, and for the first time all day she seemed a little bit shy.

"That's nothing," she said. "That's just..." She stopped. "It's silly."

Charlie waited.

"At six o'clock," said Maggie Mae at last, "every evening, when all the jobs are done, I make a cup of tea. And I sit on the front step. Just there. And I drink it, and I look at the garden, and I don't do anything at all, for ten minutes. Nobody comes. Nobody needs anything. It's just me." She shrugged a little. "That's all. It's the only line on the list that's for me. I put it on every day so I don't forget."

Charlie looked at the front step of Rose Arch Cottage, in the gold evening light, with the roses all round it.

Then she looked at the garden chair, with its cushion and its footstool and its blanket and its untouched lemonade, where Maggie Mae had sat all day, drumming her fingers, thinking about the clock.

And all at once, Charlie understood something.

Maggie Mae had not needed a whole day off. She loved her jobs. She loved Buck's clock and the miller's wife's washing and walking with Granny Opal and the little boat and Rusty's scarf and the roses, leaf by leaf. Taking them all away had not made her happy. It had made her worry all day long.

What Maggie Mae needed was ten minutes. Ten minutes on the step, at six o'clock, with a cup of tea, and nobody needing anything.

And Charlie, who had been needing things from Maggie Mae ever since she was small enough to ride in her basket, had never once thought to give her that.

Charlie went into the kitchen of Rose Arch Cottage without a word.

She filled the kettle at the pump. Maggie Mae lit the stove for her, without being asked, because Charlie was not allowed to light it, and went back outside. Charlie waited for the kettle to boil, and then called Maggie Mae in to pour, because Charlie was not allowed to pour boiling water either. But it was Charlie who chose the cup: the best one, the pink one with the roses on it. And Charlie who put in the milk, just a little, the way she had watched Maggie Mae have it for years without ever really noticing that she was watching. And Charlie who carried it out, very slowly, with both paws, so as not to spill a drop, and set it down on the front step.

It was one minute to six.

"Sit down," said Charlie.

Maggie Mae looked at the tea, and at the step, and at Charlie.

"Sit down," said Charlie again, gently. "It's your line."

Maggie Mae sat down on the front step, and picked up the pink cup, and held it in both paws.

And Charlie, who had never in her whole life been able to leave anybody alone when she could be talking to them instead, picked up the blue card, and turned round, and went quietly out of the garden gate, and closed it behind her, and left Maggie Mae alone.


Chapter Nine — The Lane

Charlie sat on the bank of the lane outside Rose Arch Cottage, with her back to the garden wall, and waited for ten minutes to pass.

It was a very long ten minutes. She counted the roses she could see over the wall. She counted the clouds. She watched a beetle. She did not peep over the wall at all, though she wanted to very much.

Great-Grandpappy Buck came slowly along the lane with his walking-stick, on his way home from taking a cutting of honeysuckle to the miller's wife. He saw Charlie sitting by herself on the bank, quite still, and stopped.

"Well, now," he said. "This is a new thing. Charlie, sitting still on her own."

"I'm waiting," said Charlie. "Maggie Mae's having her ten minutes. On the step. With her tea. I'm not allowed in." She looked up at him. "I'm not allowing myself in."

Buck lowered himself down onto the bank beside her, with a creak of old knees.

"How did the day off go?" he said.

So Charlie told him. All of it. The clock, and Benny on the chair. The high washing line, and Brody, and the lumpy shirt. The basket of wool that nobody wanted, and the walk with Granny Opal. The fox and the bear and the thunderstorm that had woken the littlest rabbit up, and the slow little boat that put her back to sleep. Rusty's grandmother's scarf, and the bird's nest, and Maggie Mae laughing, and the mend that would hold. The greenfly and the ladybirds and the four tiny crocodiles. And the last line on the card.

"I thought," she said, "that she needed a day off. Because that's what I'd want. So that's what I gave her." She looked at the blue card in her paw. "But every single job, I got it wrong first. Because I didn't know what they needed. The clock didn't need the time, it needed winding. The wool lady didn't need wool. The littlest rabbit didn't need an exciting story. And Maggie Mae didn't need a day off." She turned the card over. "She needed ten minutes. And a cup of tea. And nobody needing anything. And I only found out because I stopped doing things for a minute, and asked, and listened."

Buck did not say anything for a little while. He sat beside her on the bank in the gold evening light, with his paws folded on the top of his walking-stick, and listened to the evening: a blackbird on the roof of Rose Arch, a bee in the honeysuckle, and, from the other side of the garden wall, the very small clink of a pink cup being set down on a stone step.

Then he said, "There's more to be learned in listening than in ten years of talking."


Chapter Ten — The Other Cup

At exactly ten minutes past six, the garden gate of Rose Arch Cottage opened.

Maggie Mae stood in the gateway, with the evening sun behind her, holding the pink cup, empty now, in one paw. Her face looked different. Softer. Rested. As if she had put something heavy down.

"Charlie," she said. "Have you been sitting out here all this time?"

"Ten minutes," said Charlie. "I counted."

Maggie Mae looked at her, and then at Buck on the bank beside her, and then at the blue card in Charlie's paw.

"Every line?" she said.

"Every line," said Charlie. "But I needed help with most of them. Benny did the clock. Brody did the washing line. And you did the roses, really. And the scarf." She held out the card. "I'm not very good at being you."

Maggie Mae took the card, and looked at it for a moment, and then she knelt down in the lane, right there in front of Charlie, so that their faces were level.

"You were very good at being you," she said. "That's better."

Then she stood up, and held the gate open.

"Come on," she said. "Both of you. There's tea in the pot. Enough for three."

So they went in. Buck went first, slowly, with his stick, and sat down in the garden chair that Maggie Mae had sat in all day, with its cushion and its footstool, and said, "Oh, now, this is comfortable," and put his feet up on the footstool, and closed his eyes, and smiled.

And Maggie Mae and Charlie sat down together on the front step of Rose Arch Cottage, side by side, in the last of the evening sun, with the roses all round them and the ladybirds walking slowly along the stems.

Maggie Mae poured the tea. There was the pink cup with the roses, for her. And there was another cup, a small one, with a chipped handle and a picture of a blue boat on it, that Charlie had never seen before.

"That one's yours," said Maggie Mae. "From now on. For Wednesdays. At six."

Charlie looked at the little blue boat on the cup.

"Does the boat ever get home?" she said. "In the littlest rabbit's story?"

Maggie Mae smiled into her tea.

"I don't know," she said. "Nobody's ever stayed awake long enough to find out."

Charlie held the little cup in both paws, and drank her tea, very slowly, and did not say anything at all for ten whole minutes, which was the longest she had ever been quiet in her life. Beside her, Maggie Mae was quiet too. And in the garden chair, Great-Grandpappy Buck was quietest of all, because he had fallen fast asleep with his feet on the footstool.

On the step between them lay the blue card. Somebody, at some point, had taken a pencil, and added one more line to the bottom of it, in writing much rounder and wobblier than Maggie Mae's.

Charlie. Six. Tea.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
