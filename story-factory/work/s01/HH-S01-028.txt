Chapter One — Beau's Plan

At the end of August, when the days were still long and golden but the evenings had begun to smell faintly of apples and woodsmoke, Beau decided to give a picnic.

It came to him all at once, on a Tuesday afternoon, while he was lying on his back on Maggie Mae's lawn eating his third slice of her peach cake.

"Maggie Mae," he said, with his mouth full. "How many picnics have you given? This summer?"

Maggie Mae thought about it. She was sitting in her garden chair under the rose arch, with her fluffy tail curled neatly round her feet and her pink polka-dot bow very straight, pouring lemonade.

"Six," she said. "No. Seven. There was the one for Charlie's frog, when it went back to the pond."

"Seven," said Beau. He sat up. "And how many have I been to?"

"Seven," said Charlie, who was sitting on the grass beside him, making a daisy chain.

"And how many have I given?"

Maggie Mae and Charlie looked at each other.

"None," said Charlie kindly. "But that's all right, Beau. You don't have to."

But Beau was already on his feet, with his black-tufted ears pricked right up and a plan written all over his face.

"I'm going to give one," he said. "For you. Both of you. To say thank you for all the picnics. A surprise picnic. On Saturday." He spread out his paws, as if he could already see it. "And it's going to be the most magnificent picnic Hickory Hollow has ever seen."

"Beau," said Maggie Mae carefully, "picnics are a lot of work, you know. There's the food, and the place, and the cloth, and the weather..."

"I know," said Beau. "I've been to seven. I've watched you do it. How hard can it be?"

Maggie Mae opened her mouth. Then she closed it again, and smiled, and poured him another lemonade.

"Saturday," she said. "We'd love to."

"Don't do anything," said Beau. "Don't bring anything. Don't help. It's a surprise. Just meet me at Grandpappy's Bridge at twelve o'clock on Saturday, and leave everything else to me."

And he bounded off across the lawn, over the gate in a single magnificent leap, and away down the lane, already making lists in his head.

Charlie looked at Maggie Mae.

"Will it be all right?" she said.

Maggie Mae looked at the gate, which was still swinging.

"I think," she said slowly, "that it will be an adventure."


Chapter Two — Getting Ready

Beau spent the whole of the rest of the week getting ready.

He made a list, with Benny's help, because Beau's spelling went its own way, like Beau. It said:

PLACE. CLOTH. FOOD. LEMONADE. A SURPRISE.

The place was easy. Beau knew the most beautiful spot in the whole Hollow for a picnic: a little sunny bank above Sunny Bend, where the creek curled round in a lazy loop, and the grass was soft and short, and you could see all the way up the valley to the ridges. He went to look at it on Wednesday, and it was perfect.

The cloth he borrowed from his mother. It was big and red and white checked, and she said he could have it as long as it came back in one piece.

The food took longer. Beau could not cook, but his mother could, and she helped him, on Thursday and Friday, in the kitchen of their house at the edge of the woods. They made cheese sandwiches, cut into triangles, because triangles were more magnificent than squares. They made little honey cakes, and a big jug of lemonade, and a bowl of strawberries from Rusty's family's patch. And on Friday evening, Beau's mother made a blackberry and apple pie, the last of the summer's blackberries, golden-brown and crumbly, and Beau stood beside the oven and watched her take it out, and could hardly bear to wait.

"And the surprise," said his mother, putting the pie on the windowsill to cool. "I've been wondering all week about the surprise."

Beau looked mysterious.

"You'll see," he said.

The surprise was a kite. Beau had made it himself, from sticks and paper and string, with a little help from Benny, who had made sure it was the right shape to fly. It was bright yellow, the same colour as Beau's neckerchief, with a long tail of knotted ribbons, and Beau had painted two enormous blue eyes on it. At the end of the picnic, he was going to fly it high over Sunny Bend, so that Charlie and Maggie Mae could watch it dancing in the sky, and know how magnificent the whole thing had been.

On Friday night, Beau packed everything into the big picnic basket, very carefully. The cloth on the bottom. The sandwiches. The honey cakes. The strawberries. The lemonade, wrapped in a towel so that it would not spill. And the pie, right on top, in its dish, under a clean white napkin.

The kite he would carry separately, under his arm.

On Thursday afternoon, while the honey cakes were cooling, Sadie Belle came by.

She stood at the gate, with her pink floral headband very neat, and asked whether she could help with anything. Beau, who was in the garden practising his bow, said that it was very kind of her, but it was a surprise, and nobody was allowed to help.

"Of course," said Sadie Belle. She did not go away, though. She watched him practise his bow, which he did seven times, each one lower and grander than the last, until on the seventh he bowed so low that he fell into the lavender.

"Beau," said Sadie Belle gently, while he was climbing out. "Can I tell you one thing? Not help. Just tell."

Beau, with lavender in his ears, said she could.

"The best part of any of Maggie Mae's picnics," said Sadie Belle, "isn't the food. Or the cloth. Or the place. It's that she's there, and she's glad we came." She looked down at her hooves. "That's all. I just thought you might like to know."

And she went away down the lane before he could answer, very quickly, with her ears pink.

Beau watched her go, puzzled. Then he shook the lavender out of his ears, and went back to practising his bow, and forgot all about it, because Beau was Beau. But somewhere at the back of his head, the thing she had said stayed, and waited.

He went to bed early on Friday, and lay awake for a long time, thinking about how perfect it was all going to be.

Outside his window, the wind was getting up a little. Beau did not notice. He was asleep by the time it began to blow.


Chapter Three — Wind

Saturday was bright, and blue, and very, very windy.

It was the kind of late-summer wind that comes rushing down off the ridges for no reason at all, tossing the tops of the hickory trees and turning the leaves inside out, silver and green, silver and green. It flattened the long grass in Fern Meadow in great rolling waves. It blew the washing off Granny Opal's line. It blew the miller's hat off, twice.

Beau met Charlie and Maggie Mae at Grandpappy's Bridge at twelve o'clock exactly, with the picnic basket in one paw and the kite under the other arm, and his golden neckerchief streaming out sideways in the wind.

"Good afternoon," he said grandly, and bowed, and the wind nearly took his vest. "Follow me."

They followed him along the creek path towards Sunny Bend. It was hard going. The wind kept pushing them sideways. Maggie Mae had to hold her bow on with one paw. Charlie's ears kept blowing inside out. Halfway along, Rusty came hurtling round a bend in the path in the opposite direction, chasing his own cap, which the wind had snatched off his head and was rolling along the path like a wheel. "Can't stop!" he called, as he shot past. "Shortcut!" And he vanished into the bushes after it. They heard him for some time afterwards, crashing about in the bushes and calling to his cap, very politely, as if it might come back if only he asked it nicely enough.

And all the while, Beau's kite kept trying to fly away on its own, tugging under his arm, so that he had to walk with it clamped against his side, leaning into the wind like somebody walking up a steep hill.

"It's a little breezy," said Beau cheerfully, over his shoulder. "But that's good. Good for the kite."

They reached the sunny bank above Sunny Bend. It was just as beautiful as Beau had said. The creek curled round below them, sparkling, and the whole valley spread out before them, all the way to the ridges.

"Here," said Beau proudly, and set down the basket, and took out his mother's big red and white checked cloth, and shook it out.

The wind took it.

It took it right out of his paws, in one great gust, and lifted it up into the air like an enormous red and white bird, flapping and billowing, higher and higher. It sailed out over the bank. It sailed out over the creek. It hung there for a moment, high above the water, as if it were waving goodbye.

And then it came down, very slowly and very gracefully, in the middle of Sunny Bend, and floated away downstream on the current, round the bend, and out of sight.

Beau stood on the bank with his paws still held out, as if he were holding a cloth.

"That," he said at last, "was my mother's cloth."

"It'll fetch up somewhere," said Maggie Mae comfortingly. "Cloths always do. We'll ask the grown-ups to look along the bank on the way home."

"We'll sit on the grass," said Charlie. "Grass is nicer than cloth anyway. It's softer."

Beau looked at the empty air where the cloth had been. Then he took a deep breath, and nodded.

"The grass," he said. "Of course. The grass is much more magnificent." And he opened the basket.


Chapter Four — Everything Else

He had just taken out the sandwiches, and set them on the grass in their neat little triangles, when Charlie said, "Beau. What's that?"

She was pointing at the grass right beside the sandwiches. There was a small hole in it. And out of the small hole, in a long, busy, determined line, were marching ants. Hundreds of them. And every single one of them was heading straight for the cheese sandwiches.

"Ah," said Beau.

He picked up the sandwiches. The ants turned round and marched after him. He moved them to the other side of the basket. The ants followed. He moved them onto a flat stone. The ants climbed the stone. He held them up over his head, as high as he could reach, standing on tiptoe.

The ants stood at his feet in a little black crowd, and waited, very patiently, as if they had all the time in the world and knew perfectly well that his arms would get tired before their legs did.

"They really like cheese," said Charlie, impressed.

"We'll move," said Beau. "Just a little way along. Just away from the anthill."

So they moved, a little way along the bank, carrying everything. Beau set the basket down again on a nice flat patch of grass with no holes in it at all, and checked it very carefully for ants, and found none, and took out the lemonade.

It was then that Maggie Mae, standing very still, said, very quietly, "Beau. Don't move."

Beau did not move.

"There's a wasps' nest," said Maggie Mae, in the same quiet voice. "In the bank. Just there. Under that root. Right beside the basket."

Beau looked down. There, in the earth of the bank, a hand's width from his foot, was a small dark hole, and wasps were going in and out of it, busily, quite unconcerned.

"Walk away," said Maggie Mae. "Slowly. Don't flap. Leave the basket. Just walk."

They walked away. Slowly. Without flapping. All three of them, back along the bank, step by careful step, until they were well clear. The wasps went on going in and out of their hole, and did not take the slightest notice of them.

"The basket," said Beau, in a strangled voice. "The pie's in the basket."

"We'll fetch a grown-up," said Maggie Mae firmly. "The miller knows about wasps. He'll get it, quietly. A pie isn't worth a sting."

So Charlie ran to the Mill, which was not far, and came back with the miller, who walked up to the basket as calmly as anything, picked it up by the handle without a single jerk, and carried it away down the bank, while every wasp went on about its business as if nothing had happened.

"There you go," said the miller, handing it over. "Wasps are all right if you leave them be. They were here first, I expect." And he went back to the Mill, whistling.

Beau took the basket, and held it very tightly, and let out a long breath.

"Well," he said, a little shakily. "At least we've still got the pie."

And it was at exactly that moment that the wind, which had been blowing steadily all morning, dropped completely, and Maverick came gliding down out of the sky and landed on a branch above them, ruffled and urgent.

"Rain," he said. "Coming over Weather Ridge. Not a storm, just a shower, but a big wet one. It'll be here in a minute. You'd better find somewhere to get under."

Everybody looked west. A great grey curtain of cloud was sweeping down the valley towards them, trailing long soft streaks of rain.

"Of course it is," said Beau.


Chapter Five — Wet

They ran for the Hollow Tree.

It was the nearest shelter, an enormous old tree at the edge of the meadow with a great hollow in its trunk big enough for three, and there was no thunder in the shower, Maverick said, not a rumble, just rain, so it was safe to shelter under it. Beau carried the basket. Maggie Mae carried the kite. Charlie carried the lemonade. And Maverick flew along above them, shouting encouragement and warnings about puddles.

They got there just as the rain arrived.

It came down all at once, in a great warm roaring rush, hammering on the leaves and bouncing off the path and turning the meadow silver in a moment. They squashed themselves into the hollow of the old tree, all three of them, with the basket in Beau's lap and the kite on top of Charlie's head, and watched it pour.

It poured for twenty minutes.

It was very cosy in the hollow, in a damp sort of way. The old wood smelled of moss and mushrooms. The rain made a curtain across the opening, so thick that the meadow beyond it went grey and blurry, like a painting somebody had left out in the wet. Charlie counted the drips coming through a crack in the roof of the hollow, and got to forty-one before she lost count. Maggie Mae told them about the year the Blossom Gathering had been rained on, and everybody had eaten their sandwiches standing up in the Great Barn, and Beau said that was nothing, he had once been rained on so hard that his whiskers went straight.

Nobody looked inside the basket. Beau sat with it on his lap, holding it very tightly, as if that might keep the rain out.

When it stopped, as suddenly as it had started, the whole Hollow was dripping and shining and steaming gently in the sun. Everything smelled of wet earth and grass.

Beau looked down at the basket in his lap.

It had leaked. The rain had come in through the wicker on the side that had been nearest the open edge of the hollow, and it had come in a lot. The sandwiches had gone soft and soggy. The honey cakes had gone soft and soggy and sticky. The strawberries were floating. And the pie, under its white napkin, at the very top of the basket, which had got the worst of it of all...

Beau lifted the napkin.

The pie was not a pie any more. It was a sort of warm purple pudding, with crumbs floating in it.

And the kite, which had been on top of Charlie's head, and then on top of the basket, and then, at some point, squashed between Charlie and the back of the hollow, had a large hole in the middle of it, right between its two enormous blue eyes.

Beau sat very still.

Charlie looked at Maggie Mae. Maggie Mae looked at Charlie.

Then Beau put the napkin back over the pie, very gently, and got up, and climbed out of the hollow, and walked a little way off along the wet path, and sat down on a stone, with his back to them, and his ears down flat, and did not say anything for quite a while.

"I wanted it to be magnificent," he said at last, to nobody in particular. "For you. Because you always do such magnificent ones for everybody else. And it's the worst picnic there's ever been. In the whole history of picnics. It's the world's worst picnic."

There was a pause.

And then, behind him, there was a strange little noise.

Beau turned round.

Charlie had both paws clamped over her mouth, and her shoulders were shaking. And beside her, Maggie Mae, elegant, sophisticated Maggie Mae, with her pink polka-dot bow drooping wetly over one eye, was laughing so hard that she had to hold on to the tree.


Chapter Six — The World's Worst

"I'm sorry," gasped Maggie Mae. "Oh, Beau, I'm so sorry. I'm not laughing at you. I promise I'm not. It's just..." She waved a paw helplessly at the basket, and the kite, and the dripping tree, and the purple pudding. "The cloth. Flying away over the creek like that. Like it was going on holiday."

"And the ants," said Charlie, through her paws. "Following the sandwiches. Wherever you put them."

"And the miller," said Maggie Mae, wiping her eyes. "Walking up to the wasps like he was fetching the post."

"And the pie," said Charlie, and that set them both off again.

Beau looked at them. His ears were still down. But something at the corner of his mouth had started to twitch.

"And Maverick," he said slowly. "Coming down out of the sky like a messenger in a play. 'Rain! Coming over Weather Ridge!'"

And he did Maverick's voice, exactly, and puffed out his chest exactly the way Maverick did, and Charlie fell over sideways into the wet grass.

"I did not sound like that," said Maverick, from the branch above.

"You did a bit," said Maggie Mae.

And then Beau was laughing too. He could not help it. He laughed until his ears came back up, and his tail curled round, and he had to sit down on the wet path beside the others. They sat there, all three of them, soaked through and purple-pawed and completely without a picnic, and laughed until they ached.

When they had laughed themselves out, Beau wiped his eyes and looked at the basket.

"It really is the world's worst picnic, isn't it," he said.

"It's the most memorable one," said Maggie Mae. "I've given seven picnics this summer, and I promise you, I don't remember a single thing about any of them half as well as I'm going to remember this one."

Beau looked at her. Then he looked at the soggy basket, and the holey kite, and the sun coming out over the dripping meadow.

"Then it's not finished," he said, and stood up. "If it's going to be the world's worst picnic, it might as well be the very best world's worst picnic there's ever been. Come on."

"Where?" said Charlie.

"The Great Barn," said Beau. "It's dry, and it's close, and it's got a roof that doesn't leak." He picked up the basket. "And I've got an idea."


Chapter Seven — Prizes

By the time they got to the Great Barn, they were not three any more. They were ten.

Word gets about in Hickory Hollow, especially when there are soggy sandwiches involved. Rusty had seen the cloth float past Sunny Bend, and had followed it down the creek, and had come running to tell them. Benny and Barney had heard about the wasps from the miller. Brody had heard about the pie from Maverick. And Reba had heard the word "picnic" from somewhere, and had come to see whether there was anything left of it. Sadie Belle came because Charlie went and fetched her specially. And Maverick came because he had been there all along, and had no intention of missing the end.

They all gathered on the big wooden floor of the Great Barn, with the doors open to the dripping meadow and the sun coming in in long golden stripes.

"Welcome," said Beau grandly, standing in the middle, "to the World's Worst Picnic."

Everybody cheered.

"There will be prizes," said Beau.

Everybody cheered louder.

And so there were. Beau gave out the prizes himself, with tremendous ceremony, while Benny wrote them down.

The prize for the Worst Sandwich went to the soggiest triangle in the basket, which Barney held up dripping, to great applause, and then ate anyway, and said was not bad at all.

The prize for the Worst Pie went to the pie, which by now was a sort of purple soup. But Reba fetched spoons from the Mill, and everybody had a spoonful, and it turned out that a blackberry and apple pie, when it has been rained on and turned into soup, still tastes very much like blackberry and apple pie, only runnier. Reba had three spoonfuls and gave it a special second prize of her own, for Surprisingly Good Soup.

The prize for the Worst Weather went to the wind, in its absence.

The prize for Worst Guests went to the ants, who had not been invited at all. The prize for Best Guest went to the miller, who was sent for and given a honey cake, which had gone so soft and sticky it was more like honey with a bit of cake in it. The miller ate it, and said it was the finest honey he had ever had with a bit of cake in it, and went back to the Mill still licking his paws.

The prize for Best Messenger went to Maverick, who accepted it with a bow almost as low as Beau's, and did not fall into anything.

And then Sadie Belle, who had been quiet all afternoon, said that she had a prize to give too, if that was allowed.

"The prize for Best Host," said Sadie Belle, "goes to Beau. Because he was glad we came."

Everybody clapped. Beau looked astonished, and then very pleased, and bowed to Sadie Belle, quite low, and did not notice at all that she had gone pink to the tips of her ears. But Maggie Mae noticed. And she smiled to herself, and said nothing.

And the prize for the Worst Kite went to Beau's kite, with the hole between its eyes.

Benny picked it up, and looked at it, and looked at the hole, and turned it over.

"It's only the paper," he said. "The sticks aren't broken. If we had some more paper, and a bit of paste..."

"Maggie Mae's got paper," said Charlie at once. "And Reba can make paste. Out of flour and water. Can't you, Reba?"

"I can make anything out of flour," said Reba.

So that is what they did.


Chapter Eight — The Patch

It took them all afternoon, there on the floor of the Great Barn, with the sun moving slowly round the doorway.

Maggie Mae went home and came back with a big sheet of pink paper, the kind she used for wrapping presents, with little white polka dots on it. Reba mixed a bowl of paste out of flour and water, and only tasted it twice. Benny cut a patch to exactly the right size. Brody held the kite flat while Benny pasted. And Charlie, very carefully, with her tongue between her teeth, painted a new blue eye in the middle of the pink patch, because the hole had been right between the other two, and it seemed a shame to waste the space.

When it was finished, the kite had three eyes. Two big blue ones, and one slightly smaller blue one, in the middle, on a pink polka-dot patch.

Everybody stood round and looked at it.

"It looks surprised," said Rusty.

"It looks magnificent," said Beau.

It did, a bit, in its own way. It looked like a kite that had been through a great deal, and come out the other side, and was quite pleased about it.

"Can we fly it?" said Charlie. "Now?"

Beau went to the big barn door and looked out.

The rain had gone. The meadow was still glistening, but the sun was low now, golden and warm, slanting across the grass. And the wind had come back. Not the wild, rushing wind of the morning, that had stolen the cloth and turned the leaves inside out. A softer one. A steady, friendly, late-afternoon breeze, blowing gently down the valley, just right.

Beau turned round, with his ears pricked right up.

"Now," he said.

They all ran out into Fern Meadow, along the mown path, to the big open space in the middle, where there were no trees to catch on. Beau held the kite up, high over his head. Brody held the string. Benny shouted the instructions. And when the next gust came, Beau let go.

The three-eyed kite went up.

It went up and up, wobbling at first, then steadying, then soaring, with its long ribbon tail streaming out behind it and its pink polka-dot patch catching the low sun. It went up over the Great Barn. It went up over the meadow. It went up so high that it was only a little yellow speck against the blue, dancing, and dipping, and dancing again.

Everybody stood in the wet grass with their heads tipped right back, watching it.

"It's the most magnificent thing I've ever seen," said Charlie.

Beau, holding the string beside Brody, said nothing at all. He just watched his kite, with his ears up and his tail curled, as if it had been the plan all along.


Chapter Nine — What Came Home

They flew the kite until the sun touched Weather Ridge. Then they reeled it in, very carefully, and carried it back to the Great Barn, and sat on the big wooden floor in the last of the golden light, sharing out what was left of the honey cakes.

That was where the grown-ups found them, when they came to fetch everybody home for supper. And Great-Grandpappy Buck came too, walking slowly across the meadow from his lodge, because he had heard, as everybody in the Hollow had heard by now, all about the World's Worst Picnic, and he wanted to see the kite.

And with them came Rusty's father, holding something folded over his arm.

It was big, and red and white checked, and very wet.

"Found this," said Rusty's father, "caught on a willow root, down below Sunny Bend. Rusty showed me where. It's had a wash in the creek, but it's all in one piece."

Beau took it, and looked at it, and hugged it, wet as it was.

"My mother's cloth," he said. "It came home."

"Cloths always do," said Maggie Mae.

Beau laughed. Then he spread his mother's cloth out on the floor of the Great Barn, wet and crumpled and creek-washed, and they all sat down on it together, and shared out the very last of the honey cakes on top of it, so that, in the end, it was a proper picnic after all, with a cloth, and food, and friends, and a three-eyed kite propped up against the wall, watching over everybody.

Buck had looked at the three-eyed kite for a long while, propped up against the barn wall, with its pink polka-dot patch and its surprised middle eye. He had walked all round it, slowly, and examined the patch, and the ribbon tail, and the paste, which was still a little damp. Then he had nodded, once, as if the kite had passed some sort of test, and turned to the others, and listened to the whole story of the day from the beginning, all of them telling it at once, with Beau doing Maverick's voice again for the rain, and Barney doing the ants.

Now Great-Grandpappy Buck sat down on an upturned bucket by the door, very slowly, and looked round at them all, damp and sticky and purple-pawed, and laughing, on a wet cloth in a barn, at the end of the world's worst picnic.

Then he said, in his slow, warm voice, "A river meets a rock by going around, not by giving up."


Chapter Ten — Drying Out

Beau walked home with his father, in the soft blue dusk, carrying the picnic basket in one paw and the folded wet cloth over his arm, with the wet grass brushing his ankles and the air smelling of rain and apples and woodsmoke. The kite he had left in the Great Barn overnight, propped up against the wall, because Benny said the paste needed to dry properly, and because Beau rather liked the idea of it keeping an eye on things.

At home, his mother hung the checked cloth up over the rail by the stove to dry, and said it looked cleaner than it had ever been, and that perhaps he should take it to the creek every week.

Then Beau went up to bed.

He took off his forest-green vest, which was still damp round the edges, and hung it on the hook by the window. He untied his golden neckerchief, which was crumpled and creased and had a small purple stain on it from the pie soup, and hung that on the hook beside it, to dry.

And he looked out of the window.

The sky had cleared completely now, and was going from blue to deep blue over the ridges, and the whole of the Hollow below him was quiet, and damp, and cool after the rain. He could see the lights of the Mill, and the dark line of the creek, and the roof of Rose Arch Cottage, where Maggie Mae would be drying her bow by the stove, probably, and laughing all over again.

Down the valley, in the middle of Fern Meadow, the Great Barn stood dark against the last of the light, with its big doors closed for the night. Somewhere inside it, Beau knew, his kite was leaning against the wall in the dark, with its two big blue eyes and its one small surprised one, smelling faintly of flour paste and blackberries, drying slowly.

Outside, the last drops of the afternoon's rain were still falling from the leaves of the hickory tree by the window. Drip. And then, a little while later, drip. Slower and slower, as the night came on, each drop taking a little longer than the last.

Beau leaned on the windowsill with his chin on his paws and listened to them, and thought about the cloth flying away over the creek like it was going on holiday, and laughed, very quietly, all by himself.

On the hook beside him, his golden neckerchief hung beside his vest, drying in the warm night air, a little crumpled, a little stained, a little purple at one corner, and not one bit the worse for it.

Drip, went the hickory tree. And then, after a long, long while, one last time, drip.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
