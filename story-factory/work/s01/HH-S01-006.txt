Chapter One — The Stone That Was Too Round

Sadie Belle liked Silver Creek best in the early autumn mornings, when the mist was still lying on the water and nobody else was about.

She liked the way the creek talked quietly to itself before the day got busy. She liked the cold, clean smell of the water, and the first yellow hickory leaves turning slowly on the current like little boats with nowhere to be. She liked to walk along the bank on her long, careful legs, with her pink floral headband a little crooked and her green eyes looking at everything, and find one small new thing every day.

Sadie Belle was a young deer, gentle and kind and quietly curious. Other friends rushed. Sadie Belle noticed.

On this particular morning, at the wide, slow bend below the Old Mill where the bank was low and grassy, the small new thing was a stone.

It was sitting in the grass a little way back from the water, half covered with moss, and it was not like the other stones. The creek stones were grey and brown and all sorts of shapes. This one was white, a creamy, glowing white, like a lump of sugar, and it was round and smooth as an egg, and it had been set into the earth so neatly that only its top showed.

Sadie Belle bent her head and looked at it for a long time.

Then she looked up, and a little way further along the bank, in a straight line, she saw another one.

And beyond that, another.

She walked slowly along the bank, counting. There were seven of them, seven round white stones, each one half-hidden in moss and grass, each one set into the earth about a deer's stride apart, in a line as straight as a stretched string. The line ran from the edge of the creek, up the gentle slope of the bank, and stopped at a big flat grey rock at the top, with a little hollow worn in the middle of it, just the size for sitting in.

Somebody, a long time ago, had put them there on purpose.

Sadie Belle stood at the top beside the flat rock, with the mist curling round her legs, and felt a small, bright, fizzing feeling in her chest, the feeling of having found a secret that the whole valley had forgotten.

"Who put you here?" she whispered to the stones. "And why?"

The stones did not say.

From the woods behind her, there came a crashing, a tumbling, a shower of leaves and a loud "Whoa!" and a young bobcat burst out of a holly bush, turned a somersault he had clearly not meant to turn, and landed sitting up in the grass right beside her, with leaves in his ear tufts and his blue eyes very wide.

"Good morning, Sadie Belle!" said Beau. "I wasn't following you. I was practising sneaking. Did you hear me?"

"Not until the holly bush," said Sadie Belle kindly.

"The holly bush was a mistake," Beau admitted. He looked about. "What are you looking at?"

Sadie Belle hesitated. It was her secret. She had only just found it.

But it was Beau.

"Come and see," she said.


Chapter Two — Beau Has a Theory

Beau looked at the seven white stones with tremendous interest. He walked all the way down the line to the creek, and all the way back up. He sniffed the top stone. He sniffed the bottom stone. He tapped one with his paw.

"I know what it is," he announced.

"You do?"

"It's a racetrack," said Beau. "Obviously. For hopping. You start at the creek and you hop on every stone, all the way to the top, without touching the grass. Look." He crouched at the bottom of the line, wiggled his tail, and sprang.

He was a very good jumper. Bobcats are. He landed on the first stone, and the second, and the third, light as a leaf, and then on the fourth his back paw slipped on the moss, and he went sideways into the long grass with a soft whump.

"Mostly a racetrack," said Beau, from the grass.

Sadie Belle laughed. She tried not to, but Beau looked so surprised, lying on his back with a dandelion on his nose, that she could not help it. And Beau, hearing her laugh, began laughing too, which made his black ear tufts wobble.

"I don't think it's a racetrack," said Sadie Belle, when they had both stopped. "The stones are too carefully set. And too old. Look how deep the moss is. They've been here a long, long time. Longer than anybody racing would bother with."

Beau sat up and looked again, more slowly this time. He was athletic and brave and warm-hearted, and he did not always think of things first, but when he did look properly, he looked with his whole self.

"They're all white," he said. "Every one. And there aren't any white stones in the creek. I've been in the creek loads. I'd know."

"So somebody brought them here," said Sadie Belle. "From somewhere else."

"Carried them," said Beau slowly. "Seven heavy stones. That's a lot of carrying." His nose wrinkled. "Why would anybody carry seven heavy stones all the way here and put them in a line and then just leave them?"

They looked at each other.

"I don't know," said Sadie Belle. "But I'd like to find out."

"Me too," said Beau. "Can it be both of ours? The finding out?"

Sadie Belle felt herself go a little pink under her headband, and was glad of the mist.

"Yes," she said. "Both of ours."

So they made a plan. Or rather, Sadie Belle made a plan, and Beau agreed with every part of it very enthusiastically. They would not dig the stones up. That would spoil whatever they were for. They would look carefully, and think, and ask, and come back every morning until they understood.

"Every morning?" said Beau. "That's a lot of mornings."

"It might be," said Sadie Belle. "Some secrets take a long time."

Beau thought about this.

"I'm not very good at long times," he said honestly. "I'm better at short ones. I'm brilliant at very short ones."

"Then I'll be the patient one," said Sadie Belle, "and you can be the brilliant one."

Beau beamed so hard that his whiskers stood straight out.


Chapter Three — Asking About

Over the next few days, they asked everybody.

They asked Maggie Mae, who had lived by Fern Meadow all her life and knew where every flower grew. She came and looked at the stones with her emerald eyes narrowed and her fluffy tail swishing thoughtfully.

"Well, I declare," she said. "All my life I've walked past this bend and never once noticed. They're very elegant, whatever they are. Somebody had an eye."

But she did not know what they were for.

They asked Benny, who knew about building. He knelt and examined the bottom stone with his measuring stick for a long time.

"Set in clay," he said. "Packed round with small pebbles so they wouldn't shift. And they're exactly the same distance apart, every one. Five feet and a hand." He stood up, frowning. "Somebody measured these. Carefully. Whoever did it knew what they were doing."

But he did not know what they were for.

They asked Rusty, who knew every trail in the Hollow. He trotted up and down the line twice, sniffing, with his white-tipped tail held high.

"There's no trail here," he said at last. "Nothing goes anywhere. The line starts at the water and stops at that rock. It doesn't lead to anything." He looked puzzled. "It's the first path I ever saw that doesn't go somewhere."

Charlie thought they might be giant eggs. Barney thought they might be the teeth of a buried giant, which Benny said was ridiculous, and which made Charlie hide behind Maggie Mae for a moment until Barney said he was only joking and they were definitely stones. Reba wondered whether they might be very old, very hard bread rolls, and tapped one hopefully, and was disappointed.

Maverick flew up high over the bend and looked down.

"It's a line," he reported, landing. "A very straight line. It points at the ridges. That's all. I don't think it's anything important." He fluffed his feathers. "If it were important, I'd know about it."

Beau, sitting on the flat rock at the top of the line, swung his legs and looked where Maverick had pointed. He looked down the line of white stones, over the creek, across the wide misty valley, all the way to the long blue shape of Sunrise Ridge on the far side.

There, on the top of the ridge, right where the line of stones seemed to point, there was a little notch: a dip between two rounded hills, like the gap where a tooth had come out.

"Huh," said Beau.

Nobody heard him except Sadie Belle.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," said Beau. "Probably nothing. Just, if you sit here, the stones point at that gap in the ridge. Look. Right at it." He squinted. "It's probably a coincidence."

Sadie Belle came and sat on the flat rock beside him, and looked down the line of stones, over the creek, across the valley, to the notch in Sunrise Ridge.

He was right. The line pointed exactly at it.

"Beau," she said slowly. "I don't think it's a coincidence."


Chapter Four — The Notch in the Ridge

They went to see Great-Grandpappy Buck.

He was sitting on his porch in the afternoon sun, in his old hickory rocking chair, whittling a little wooden bird. He listened to all of it, the white stones, the straight line, Benny's careful measuring, Rusty's path that went nowhere, Beau's notch in Sunrise Ridge. He listened with his head on one side and his spectacles on the end of his nose, and he did not stop whittling.

"White stones," he said, when they had finished. "In a line. By the bend below the mill."

"Do you know what they are?" asked Sadie Belle.

Buck's knife went still for a moment. He looked out over the creek, at nothing in particular, with the look of somebody trying to catch hold of a memory that keeps slipping away.

"I've a feeling," he said slowly, "that my grandmother told me something about that bend, once. When I was very small. Something about the sun." He shook his head. "But it's gone, I'm afraid. It's been a long time since I was very small."

Beau's face fell.

"But I'll tell you what I'd do, if I were trying to find out," said Buck, and he went back to his whittling. "I wouldn't ask an old beaver who can't remember. I'd ask the stones. They've been there a good long while. They know what they're for. You've only got to wait till they tell you."

"How do stones tell you anything?" said Beau.

Buck's whiskers twitched.

"Well," he said. "What does that line point at?"

"The notch in Sunrise Ridge," said Sadie Belle.

"And what comes up over Sunrise Ridge?"

Sadie Belle looked at Beau. Beau looked at Sadie Belle.

"The sun," they said together.

Buck said nothing at all. He only blew a curl of wood-shaving off the little bird's wing and turned it in the light.

Sadie Belle's heart had begun to beat faster.

"So if somebody sat on the flat rock," she said, "and looked down the line of stones at sunrise..."

"They'd see the sun come up," said Beau. "Over the ridge. Right in the notch?"

"Maybe," said Sadie Belle. "Maybe on one special morning. If that's what it's for."

"Then we have to go and watch," said Beau, jumping up. "At sunrise. Tomorrow!"

"You'll tell your families first," said Buck mildly, without looking up. "And you'll take a grown-up. Sunrise is an early hour for a creek bank."

"Will you come?" asked Sadie Belle.

Buck chuckled.

"These old bones are fond of their bed till the sun's properly up," he said. "But I've a feeling your mother might enjoy a sunrise, Sadie Belle. She always used to."


Chapter Five — Not This Morning

Sadie Belle's mother did enjoy a sunrise. She said so, at the kitchen table that evening, with a smile that made Sadie Belle think she had been hoping somebody would ask. And Beau's family said he could go too, as long as he was home for breakfast and did not fall in the creek.

So, the next morning, in the cold grey dark before dawn, three shapes and one slightly late fourth shape made their way down the bank to the bend below the Old Mill.

The late shape was Beau. He had overslept, and run, and taken a shortcut that Rusty had once told him about, which turned out to go through a bramble patch.

"I'm here!" he whispered, arriving with a bramble stuck to his tail. "Did I miss it?"

"Not yet," whispered Sadie Belle.

Her mother had brought a blanket and a small flask of warm berry-cordial. She settled at the top of the bank a little way off, so as not to be in the way, and the two youngsters sat side by side on the flat grey rock with the hollow in the middle and looked down the line of seven white stones, glimmering faintly in the dark, towards the long black shape of Sunrise Ridge.

The sky was very slowly turning from black to deep blue. The stars were going out, one by one. The creek talked to itself in the dark. Somewhere a wren woke up and tried a few notes.

They waited.

The blue turned paler. A thin line of gold appeared along the top of the ridge. It spread, and grew brighter, and the mist on the creek turned pink.

"Here it comes," breathed Beau.

The sun came up.

It came up over Sunrise Ridge in a great slow blaze of gold, so bright that they had to squint. It was beautiful. It made the whole valley glow.

But it did not come up in the notch.

It came up a little way to the left of it, over the shoulder of the rounded hill, nowhere near the gap.

Beau's ears went flat.

"Oh," he said. "It missed."

Sadie Belle looked at the sun, and then at the notch, and then at the line of white stones. Her heart sank a little. But only a little.

"Maybe it didn't miss," she said slowly. "Maybe it's just not the right morning."

"What do you mean?"

"The sun doesn't come up in the same place every day," said Sadie Belle. "Mama told me. In summer it comes up way over there," she pointed, "and in winter, right over there. It moves along the ridge a little bit every morning. So maybe there's only one morning when it comes up in the notch. And this isn't it."

"Then which morning is it?" said Beau.

Sadie Belle did not know.

Behind them, her mother had come quietly down the bank, with the blanket round her shoulders.

"Well," she said softly. "There's only one way I know of to find out which morning something happens."

"What's that?" asked Beau.

Sadie Belle's mother smiled.

"Come back tomorrow," she said.


Chapter Six — Every Morning

So they came back the next day.

The sun came up a tiny bit to the right of where it had been the day before. Still not in the notch. Closer, though. Beau was certain it was closer. Sadie Belle thought so too, but she was not certain enough to say so.

They came back the day after that.

It was closer again. They could both see it now. Every morning, the sun was creeping along the top of Sunrise Ridge towards the notch, a little bit at a time, like somebody walking very slowly along a fence.

"It's coming," said Sadie Belle. "It really is coming."

"How many more mornings?" said Beau.

"I don't know," said Sadie Belle. "We'll just have to keep watching."

And here was the hard part.

For Beau, it was very hard indeed.

Getting up in the cold dark was hard. Sitting still on a cold rock was hard. Watching the sun come up in nearly, but not quite, the right place, morning after morning, was the hardest of all. On the fourth morning he arrived yawning so widely that his whole face disappeared. On the fifth morning he arrived with his fur on end and said, quite crossly, "What if it never comes? What if the stones are just wrong? What if we're getting up every morning for nothing?"

Sadie Belle did not answer straight away. She looked at the sun, which was very nearly at the edge of the notch now. Then she looked at Beau.

"I don't think we're getting up for nothing," she said. "Look."

She pointed down at the creek.

The mist was lifting off the water in long, slow, golden curls. Every spider's web in the long grass along the bank was strung with drops of dew, and the low sun was shining through them, so that the whole slope glittered as if somebody had scattered it with tiny lamps. A heron was standing in the shallows on one leg, perfectly still, gold all down one side. And as they watched, a family of ducks came paddling round the bend, one big and six small, leaving little silver ripples behind them.

"We'd never have seen any of this," said Sadie Belle, "if we'd stayed in bed."

Beau looked at it all for a long moment.

"Oh," he said quietly.

He did not grumble on the sixth morning. He arrived on time, and sat on the rock, and watched the mist, and the heron, and the light coming through the dew. And when the sun came up, just touching the edge of the notch now, just catching the side of it, he did not say anything at all. He only reached out, very gently, and touched Sadie Belle's hoof with his paw, to make sure she had seen.

She had.

That evening, Sadie Belle's mother looked at the sky, and at the little marks Sadie Belle had been scratching on a piece of bark every morning, one for each day, and nodded slowly.

"One more morning," she said. "Maybe two. I'd tell your friends, if I were you."


Chapter Seven — Telling Everybody

They thought about it for a long time, sitting on the flat rock that afternoon.

"We don't have to tell," said Beau. "We could keep it. Just ours. Like you said. The finding out was both of ours."

Sadie Belle thought about the quiet mornings, just the two of them and her mother and the heron. They had been some of the best mornings of her whole life. She did not want to give them away.

But then she thought about Benny, kneeling with his measuring stick, saying somebody measured these carefully. She thought about Rusty, puzzled by a path that went nowhere. She thought about Great-Grandpappy Buck, trying so hard to remember his grandmother's story.

"Somebody made these for everybody," she said slowly. "Not for one or two. Seven stones is a lot of carrying. You don't carry seven heavy stones all the way to the creek for just yourself."

Beau thought about that.

"You're right," he said. "It's an everybody sort of thing." Then his face lit up. "And I'm brilliant at telling people things! That's a short sort of job!"

So Beau told everybody.

He ran up and down the whole valley that afternoon, from the beaver ponds to the Old Mill to Rose Arch Cottage, telling everybody to come to the bend below the mill in the morning, before dawn, because something was going to happen. He did not say what. Sadie Belle had asked him not to spoil it.

"What's going to happen?" asked Charlie.

"Something," said Beau mysteriously.

"Something good?"

"Something brilliant," said Beau.

At Reba's den, Reba wanted to know whether the something involved food.

"It might," said Beau, who had not thought about it. "If you bring some."

"Then I'm coming," said Reba, and went straight to her pantry to plan.

At the beaver ponds, Benny wanted to know exactly what time, to the minute, and Beau said "before dawn", and Benny said that was not a time, it was a feeling, and Barney said he liked feelings better than times anyway, and they were still arguing about it when Beau left.

Maverick was the hardest.

"Before dawn?" he said, from the top of his hickory, with deep suspicion. "Eagles don't really do before dawn. We do after breakfast. Mid-morning, at a push."

"You'll miss it," said Beau.

"Miss what?"

"I can't say," said Beau. "It's a surprise. But it's the best thing I've ever seen. Or it will be. If it works."

Maverick looked down at him for a long time.

"If it's the best thing you've ever seen," he said at last, "then I suppose I'd better see it too. In case it's nearly as good as me landing."

Back at the bend below the Old Mill, Sadie Belle sat alone on the flat rock in the late afternoon sun and looked down the line of white stones, and felt a small, cold flutter of worry.

What if it did not work? What if the sun came up just a little beside the notch again, with the whole Hollow sitting there in the cold, watching? What if they had all got up in the dark for nothing, because of her?

She sat with that worry for a while. Then she looked at the seven stones, and thought about whoever had carried them, so long ago, and set them so carefully, five feet and a hand apart.

They had been sure, she thought. Sure enough to carry seven heavy stones.

She stood up and went home to bed early.

He even bounded up the steps of Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, three at a time, to tell him.

"Tomorrow morning," he panted. "Before dawn. You have to come. Even if your bones like their bed."

Buck looked at him over his spectacles.

"Is it the notch?" he said quietly.

Beau nodded so hard that his ear tufts flapped.

Buck looked out at the creek for a long moment. Then he set down his whittling.

"Well now," he said. "I suppose these old bones will have to manage."


Chapter Eight — The Morning the Sun Came Home

They came in the dark.

All of them. Charlie came wrapped in Maggie Mae's shawl, too sleepy to talk, which for Charlie was very unusual. Benny and Barney came yawning, side by side, Barney still wearing one slipper. Brody came with a blanket big enough for three, and shared it. Reba came with a basket of warm cornbread, because she said nobody could be expected to watch a sunrise on an empty stomach. Rusty came by the proper path. Maverick came gliding down out of the dark and landed on the bank with only a very small wobble, looking as though he had not had nearly enough sleep and did not think much of mornings. The rabbit family came, and the miller and his wife, and several families from along the creek who had heard Beau's shouting and come to see what all the fuss was about.

And Great-Grandpappy Buck came last, slowly, with his walking-stick in one paw and Sadie Belle's mother's arm to lean on.

Sadie Belle and Beau sat on the flat rock at the top of the line. Everybody else gathered on the grass behind and beside them, in a soft, rustling, whispering crowd, looking down the line of seven white stones towards the black shape of Sunrise Ridge.

"What are we looking at?" whispered Barney.

"Shh," whispered Benny. "Just look."

The sky turned from black to deep blue. The stars went out. The gold line came along the top of the ridge, brighter and brighter, and the mist on the creek turned pink, and the whole crowd went still.

And then the sun came up.

It came up exactly in the notch. Right in the middle of the gap between the two rounded hills, as if the ridge had opened a door for it. A great slow blaze of gold, rising in the gap like a lamp being lifted into a window.

And the light came straight down the valley, across the creek, and down the line of seven white stones, one after another, like a path being lit, until all seven were glowing gold in the dew, and the light reached the flat rock at the top and fell across Sadie Belle and Beau sitting in it, and lit them up as well.

Nobody said a word.

Then, from somewhere behind them, there came a very small sound. It was Great-Grandpappy Buck. He was standing very still, leaning on his stick, with the gold light on his old grey face, and his eyes were wet.

"Even Day," he whispered. "That's what she called it. My grandmother. I remember now." He shook his head slowly. "Even Day. When the day and the night are the same length, and the sun comes up in the notch, and the whole Hollow comes down to the white stones at dawn to give thanks for the harvest coming in. I came here once, in her arms. I was so small. I'd forgotten all of it."

"Why did everybody stop coming?" asked Sadie Belle softly.

Buck thought about it.

"I don't suppose anybody decided to stop," he said. "I expect folk just got busy one year, and missed it. And then they missed it the next year. And the moss grew. And the ones who remembered got old." He looked at the line of glowing stones. "That's how most good things get lost, I think. Not all at once. Just a little bit at a time, when nobody's looking."


Chapter Nine — Even Day

When the sun had risen clear of the notch, and the gold light had spread across the whole valley, something happened that nobody had planned.

Brody, without saying anything, unpacked the cornbread from Reba's basket and began handing it round. Maggie Mae poured the cordial from Sadie Belle's mother's flask into everybody's cups, and when that ran out, from her own. The rabbit family sat down in a row on the grass. The miller's wife started humming an old tune, and the miller joined in, and then Buck, in his deep, cracked old voice, and then, slowly, others who half knew it.

And then, one by one, without anybody asking them to, the folk of Hickory Hollow began to say what they were thankful for.

The miller was thankful for a good dry harvest. One of the little rabbits was thankful for her new brother. Benny was thankful that the bridge over Pebble Run had held all autumn. Barney was thankful for Benny, though he said it very fast and then pretended he had said something else. Reba was thankful for cornbread. Charlie, waking up enough to speak, was thankful for Maggie Mae. Maverick, after a long pause, said he was thankful for mornings, actually, now that he had seen one properly.

When it came round to Beau, he went very quiet.

"I'm thankful," he said, and his voice wobbled a little, "that Sadie Belle let me help. Even though I'm not very good at waiting. She did the patient bit, and I did the... the noticing-the-notch bit. And it took both."

Sadie Belle went so pink under her headband that she was glad, once again, that there was still a little mist.

Then it was her turn.

She looked at the seven white stones, glowing in the low gold light. She looked at Buck, wiping his eyes with a big spotted handkerchief. She looked at the whole of Hickory Hollow, sitting together on a cold creek bank at sunrise, sharing cornbread, for the first time in longer than anyone could remember.

"I'm thankful," she said, "that somebody carried seven heavy stones down here a long time ago. Just so that one morning, we'd all be sitting here together."

Nobody spoke for a while after that. The creek talked. The heron flapped slowly away downstream. The sun climbed.

"We should come back next year," said Charlie.

"And the year after," said Barney.

"And every year," said Maggie Mae. "Before dawn. On Even Day."

"Who'll remember when it is?" asked Brody.

Everybody looked at Sadie Belle and Beau.

"We will," said Beau. "Won't we, Sadie Belle?"

"We will," said Sadie Belle. "We'll start watching the sun a week before. Every morning. And when it's nearly in the notch, we'll tell everybody."

"I'll do the telling," said Beau. "I'm brilliant at telling."


Chapter Ten — Time the Mountains Have Plenty Of

That evening, Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch was quiet and gold and full of sleepy friends. They had all been up since before dawn, and it showed.

Buck settled into his old hickory rocking chair. It gave its first long, slow creak, and the porch went quiet. Bramble's quilt went over the smallest knees. The lantern by the door was lit, though the sky was still pale over Weather Ridge, because everybody was so tired that it felt later than it was.

Beau lay along the top step with his chin on his paws and his eyes half shut. Sadie Belle sat beside him, her long legs folded under her. They had both said, several times, that they were not tired at all.

Buck took his banjo from its corner, and played a few soft notes, and then a few more. It was an old tune, slow and gentle, and nobody on the porch had heard it before.

"That one's new," murmured Brody, from the boards.

"That one's very old," said Buck. "It's the tune my grandmother hummed, that Even Day morning, when I was in her arms. I'd forgotten it along with everything else." He smiled. "It came back this morning, with the sun. Funny how things come back when you go and stand in the right place."

He played it through again, softer.

"You know," he said, when he had finished, "I've lived a long time in this Hollow, and I'd have told you I knew every corner of it. And all those years, those seven stones were sitting down there in the moss, waiting. Waiting for somebody to come along who'd look properly. And then sit still. And then come back. And come back again." He looked at Sadie Belle and Beau. "Not everybody would have kept coming back."

Beau opened one eye.

"I nearly didn't," he admitted. "On the fifth morning."

"But you did," said Buck.

"Sadie Belle showed me the heron," said Beau.

Buck rocked slowly. The chair creaked.

"Most of the wonders worth finding take a while, young'uns," he said. "Seeds. Sunrises. Friends. Give it time, little one." He looked out at the darkening ridge, where the notch was just a soft dip in the last of the light. "Time's the one thing the mountains have plenty of."

He began to play again.

Down at the bend below the Old Mill, in the blue dusk, the seven white stones lay in their straight line in the grass, with the moss cleared away from them now, so that they glimmered faintly in the dark like a row of small moons, waiting for the sun to come home again next year.

Beau fell asleep first, right in the middle of saying he was not tired. Sadie Belle stayed awake a little longer, watching the first star come out exactly above the notch in Sunrise Ridge.

Then she closed her eyes too.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
