Chapter One — Seven White Stones

It was early November, and the Hollow had gone quiet in the way it does when the harvest is in and the year is turning towards winter.

The leaves were mostly down now. The hickories stood bare and grey along Silver Creek, and the creek itself ran clear and cold over a bed of fallen gold. The mornings came late and frosty. In the low places, the mist lay on the water until the sun was well up, and the whole valley smelled of woodsmoke and wet leaves and the sharp, clean smell of the first cold.

Sadie Belle liked these mornings best of all.

She was a young deer, gentle and quiet, with green eyes, a pink floral headband and long, careful legs. Most mornings, while her mother gathered the last of the watercress from the shallows at the slow bend below the Old Mill, Sadie Belle wandered along the grassy bank nearby, looking for one small new thing. Other friends rushed. Sadie Belle noticed.

On this particular morning, in the grass a little way back from the water, the small new thing was a stone.

It was not like the other stones. The creek stones were grey and brown and every sort of shape. This one was white, a creamy, glowing white, like a lump of sugar, and round and smooth as an egg. It had been set into the earth so neatly that only its top showed, and moss had grown over half of it.

Sadie Belle bent her head and looked at it for a long time.

Then she looked up, and a little way further up the bank, in a straight line, she saw another.

And beyond that, another.

She walked slowly up the slope, counting. There were seven of them, seven round white stones, each one half-hidden in moss and grass, each one set about a deer's stride from the next, in a line as straight as a stretched string. The line began at the edge of the creek, ran up the gentle bank, and stopped at a big flat grey rock at the top, with a hollow worn in the middle of it, just the size for sitting in.

Somebody had put them there on purpose. A long time ago.

"Mama," she called softly. "Come and look."

Her mother came up the bank with her basket of watercress, and looked, and her eyebrows went up.

"Well, I never," she said. "All these years I've gathered cress at this bend, and I never once saw those."

"Who put them here?" said Sadie Belle. "And why?"

Her mother shook her head slowly.

"I don't know, my love," she said. "But it looks to me like a question somebody wanted asked."

There was a crashing in the holly bushes behind them, a shower of leaves, a loud "Whoa!", and a young bobcat burst out of the undergrowth, turned a somersault he had clearly not intended, and landed sitting up in the grass with leaves in his black ear tufts and his blue eyes very wide.

"Good morning!" said Beau. "I wasn't sneaking up. I was practising sneaking. Grandma said I could, as long as I stayed where she could see me." He waved a paw back towards the path, where an elderly bobcat with spectacles was sitting on a log, knitting. "Did you hear me coming?"

"Not until the holly bush," said Sadie Belle kindly.

"The holly bush was a mistake," Beau admitted. He looked about. "What are you looking at?"

Sadie Belle hesitated. It was her secret. She had only just found it.

But it was Beau.

"Come and see," she said.


Chapter Two — Not a Racetrack

Beau looked at the seven white stones with tremendous interest. He walked all the way down the line to the creek, and all the way back up. He sniffed the top one. He tapped the bottom one.

"I know what it is," he announced.

"You do?"

"It's a racetrack. For hopping. You start at the creek and you hop on every stone to the top without touching the grass. Watch."

He crouched at the bottom of the line, wiggled his tail, and sprang. He was a very good jumper. He landed on the first stone, and the second, and the third, light as a leaf, and then on the fourth his back paw slipped on the moss, and he went sideways into the long grass with a soft whump.

"Mostly a racetrack," he said, from the grass.

Sadie Belle tried very hard not to laugh, and did not manage it. Beau looked so surprised, lying on his back with a dandelion clock on his nose, that her laugh came out all at once. And Beau, hearing her, started laughing too, so hard that his ear tufts wobbled.

"I don't think it's a racetrack," said Sadie Belle, when they had both stopped. "Look how deep the moss is. They've been here a very long time. Longer than anybody would bother keeping a racetrack."

Beau sat up and looked again, more slowly. He did not always think of things first, but when he looked properly, he looked with his whole self.

"They're all white," he said. "Every one. And there aren't any white stones in the creek. I've been in the creek loads. I'd know."

"So somebody brought them from somewhere else," said Sadie Belle.

"Carried them," said Beau slowly. "Seven heavy stones. That's a lot of carrying." His nose wrinkled. "Why would anybody carry seven stones all the way here, put them in a line, and then just leave them?"

They looked at each other.

"Can we find out?" said Beau. "Together? The finding out?"

Sadie Belle felt herself go a little pink under her headband, and was glad of the mist.

"Together," she said.

They made a plan, sitting on the flat rock at the top of the line. They would not dig the stones up, because that might spoil whatever they were for. They would look, and think, and ask, and come back as often as their families let them, until they understood.

"That might take a while," said Sadie Belle.

"I'm not very good at a while," said Beau honestly. "I'm better at quick. I'm brilliant at very quick."

"Then I'll be the patient one," said Sadie Belle, "and you can be the quick one."

Beau beamed so hard that his whiskers stood straight out.


Chapter Three — Asking About

They began by asking their friends.

Benny came, with his measuring stick, and knelt by the bottom stone and examined it for a long time.

"Set in clay," he said. "Packed round with small pebbles so they can't shift. And they're exactly the same distance apart, every single one." He measured again, to be sure. "Somebody measured these. Very carefully."

Rusty came and trotted up and down the line twice with his nose to the ground. Then he stood very still, with his white-tipped tail held up, thinking.

"These aren't creek stones," he said. "They're the white rock from the top of High Ridge. There's a whole cliff of it, up past the Sugar Grove. I found it once, chasing a butterfly. It's half a day's walk up and half a day back." He looked at the line. "Somebody carried every one of these down from the top of the ridge."

"Seven times?" said Beau.

"Or seven somebodies," said Rusty.

That made everybody quiet for a moment. It was a long, long way to carry a stone.

Maggie Mae came with Charlie and a little trowel and two soft brushes. "If they're that important," she said, "they deserve to be seen." So the two of them knelt in the grass and brushed and scraped the moss away from all seven stones, gently, so as not to scratch them, until they gleamed white in the grass, every one. It took the whole morning. Charlie got moss in her whiskers and said it was the best job she'd ever had.

And Maverick came.

"It's just a line," he said, landing on the flat rock with a flourish. "Seven stones in a line. What's to find out?"

"Where does it point?" asked Sadie Belle. "Exactly? We can't tell from down here. The slope's in the way."

Maverick puffed out his chest. Exactly was the sort of thing an eagle was for. He flew straight up, high over the bend, so high that he was only a speck, and hung there on the cold air for a long time, looking down with his sharp blue eyes. Then he came gliding back.

"It points across the creek," he said, "and across the valley, and straight at Sunrise Ridge. Straight as an arrow. And right where it hits the ridge, there's a notch. A dip in the top, between two round hills, like the gap where a tooth came out." He frowned. "You can't see it from down here, because of the trees. But you can see it from the flat rock, if you sit in the hollow."

Sadie Belle and Beau looked at each other.

Then they both climbed into the hollow in the flat rock together, which was a bit of a squeeze, and looked down the line of white stones, over the creek, across the valley, to Sunrise Ridge.

Maverick was right. From exactly there, and nowhere else, the line of stones pointed straight at a small dip in the top of the ridge. A notch.

"Huh," said Beau.

"Sunrise Ridge," said Sadie Belle slowly. "It's called Sunrise Ridge because that's where the sun comes up."

Beau went very still.

"Sadie Belle," he said. "What if the sun comes up in the notch?"


Chapter Four — Watching

They went to Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch that afternoon, all of them, to ask if he knew anything about the white stones.

He was in his rocking chair, whittling a little wooden wren, and he listened to the whole of it without stopping his knife. When they had finished, he was quiet for a long while.

"I had a grandmother who used to talk about that bend," he said at last. "When I was very small. But I'm sorry, young'uns. Whatever she said, it's gone. It's been a long time since I was very small." He blew a curl of wood off the wren's wing. "It sounds to me like you know a great deal more about those stones than I do."

So they went back to the flat rock, Sadie Belle and Beau, with the others drifting along behind, and sat in the hollow and thought.

"If the sun came up in the notch," said Sadie Belle slowly, "it would shine straight down the line. Right down the stones. Like a path."

"Then let's watch it come up," said Beau. "Tomorrow!"

So they did. Sadie Belle's mother came, with a blanket and a flask, and settled at the top of the bank. And Beau's grandmother brought him down the path in the dark, with a lantern, still knitting.

"Remember, now," said Sadie Belle's mother, before the sky had even begun to lighten. "Never look straight at the sun once it's properly up. Look at the edge of the ridge, and the light on the stones. Not the sun itself. It's too bright for eyes."

They promised.

And they watched.

The sky turned from black to deep blue, and from deep blue to pale gold. And the sun came up over Sunrise Ridge.

But not in the notch. It came up over the shoulder of the round hill to the left of it.

Beau's ears went flat.

"It missed," he said.

Sadie Belle looked at the place where the sun had come up, and then at the notch, and then at the white stones. Something was turning over slowly in her mind.

"It doesn't come up in the same place every morning," she said. "Does it? In summer it comes up way over there," she pointed far to the left, "and in winter it comes up way over there," far to the right. "It must move along the ridge a little bit every day. Getting from one end to the other."

"So maybe there's just one morning," said Beau slowly, "when it's exactly in the notch."

"And maybe," said Sadie Belle, "that's what the stones are for."

Benny had come too, yawning, wrapped in a blanket. Now he sat up straight.

"Then we need to know which morning," he said. "So we need to measure where it comes up. Every single day. And see how fast it's moving."


Chapter Five — Benny's Pegs

Benny's idea was a very good one, and it took them all the next week.

The miller cut him a long, flat board of hickory at the Old Mill and drilled a neat row of holes along its edge, while Benny measured exactly where every hole should go. Benny laid it across the flat rock, right in front of the hollow, so that anybody sitting in the hollow could look along the top of it at Sunrise Ridge. And every morning, at the exact moment the sun first showed over the ridge, Sadie Belle, sitting in the hollow, would point. And Beau would put a little wooden peg into the board exactly where she pointed, in the row of holes along its edge.

"Then we can see how far it moves each day," said Benny. "And count how many more days till it reaches the notch."

The first peg went in well to the left of the notch.

The second peg went in a little to the right of the first. Not much. About the width of Beau's claw.

The third peg went in a little to the right of that.

"It's coming," said Sadie Belle. "It really is. A claw's width every morning."

"How many claws to the notch?" asked Beau.

Benny counted carefully along the board with his pencil.

"Four," he said. "Four more claws. Four more mornings."

"Four," said Beau, and his face fell. "That's ages."

Barney, who had come along on the third morning out of curiosity and stayed because of the cornbread, had an idea about that.

"Give the pegs names," he said. "Then it's not waiting. It's meeting people."

So the pegs got names. The first was Sir Earlybird. The second was Mrs Frostypaws. The third was called Benny, which Benny pretended to be cross about, and secretly was not. Every morning there was a new peg to name, and Barney named them all, and it did make the waiting a tiny bit shorter. Though not, as Beau pointed out, very much.

And here was the hard part.

For Beau, it was very hard indeed.

Getting up in the cold dark was hard. Sitting still on a cold rock was hard. Watching the sun come up in nearly, but not quite, the right place, morning after morning, was the hardest of all. Sadie Belle's mother came every single morning, with her blanket and her flask. Beau's grandmother came every single morning, with her knitting. Benny came most mornings. The others came and went.

On the fourth morning, Beau arrived yawning so widely that his whole face disappeared.

On the fifth morning, he arrived with his fur on end, and sat down hard on the rock, and said, quite crossly, "What if Benny's sums are wrong? What if it never gets there? What if we're getting up every single morning in the cold for nothing?"

Sadie Belle did not answer straight away.

She looked down at the creek.


Chapter Six — What They Would Have Missed

"Look," said Sadie Belle softly.

The mist was lifting off the water. It was not doing it all at once. It was coming up slowly, in soft white shapes, like sheep getting up one by one from a field. And as the first light came across the valley, it touched every spider's web in the long grass along the bank, and every web was strung with drops of dew, so that the whole slope suddenly glittered as if somebody had scattered it with tiny lamps.

A heron was standing in the shallows on one leg, perfectly still, turned gold down one side by the low light.

Then a family of ducks came paddling round the bend, all grown up now and nearly the same size, one after another in a neat row, leaving little silver ripples behind them. The last one stopped, and shook its head, and shook its tail, and went on.

Beau watched all of it.

"We'd never have seen any of that," said Sadie Belle, "if we'd stayed in bed."

Beau did not say anything for a long moment.

"Oh," he said quietly.

He did not grumble on the sixth morning. He arrived on time, and sat on the rock, and watched the mist and the heron and the lamps of dew. And when the sun came up, Sadie Belle pointed, and Beau put the peg in, and the peg went in just one claw's width from the notch.

That afternoon, Benny sat on the flat rock with his pencil and counted along the board three times, to be sure.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Or the day after. But I think tomorrow."

Sadie Belle and Beau looked at each other.

"We could keep it," said Beau. "Just ours. Like we said. The finding out was ours."

Sadie Belle thought about the quiet mornings, with the heron and the mist and her mother's flask. They had been some of the best mornings of her life. She did not want to give them away.

But then she thought about Rusty, saying somebody carried every one of these down from the top of the ridge. Half a day up and half a day down. Seven times.

"Nobody carries seven stones all that way just for themselves," she said. "They carried them for everybody."

Beau thought about it.

"You're right," he said. Then his face lit up. "And I'm brilliant at telling everybody things! That's a quick sort of job!"


Chapter Seven — Telling Everybody

So Beau told everybody.

He ran the whole length of the valley that afternoon, with his grandmother walking behind at her own pace, telling every family he met to come to the slow bend below the Old Mill in the morning, before dawn, because something was going to happen. He would not say what.

"Is it something good?" asked Charlie.

"Something brilliant," said Beau.

"Does it involve food?" asked Reba.

"It might," said Beau, who had not thought about it. "If you bring some."

"Then I'm coming," said Reba, and went straight to her pantry.

Maverick was the hardest.

"Before dawn?" he said from the top of his hickory, with deep suspicion. "Eagles don't do before dawn. We do after breakfast."

"You'll miss it," said Beau. "And you helped. You found the notch."

Maverick looked down at him for a long time.

"I did find the notch," he said at last. "All right. I suppose I'd better see what it's for."

Great-Grandpappy Buck listened from his porch with his wren half-whittled in his paw.

"Before dawn?" he said. "These old bones are very fond of their bed till the sun's well up."

"Please," said Beau.

Buck looked out at the creek for a long moment.

"Well now," he said. "I suppose they'll have to manage."

Back at the bend, in the late afternoon sun, Sadie Belle sat alone in the hollow of the flat rock and looked down the line of white stones, and felt a small, cold flutter of worry.

What if Benny's count was out by a day? What if the sun came up a claw's width beside the notch, with the whole Hollow sitting there in the cold, watching? What if they had all got up in the dark for nothing, because of her?

She sat with the worry for a while. Then she looked at the seven white stones, gleaming in the grass, and thought about whoever had carried them down from the cliffs at the top of High Ridge, so long ago, and set them so carefully, exactly the same distance apart.

They had been sure, she thought. Sure enough to carry seven stones all that way.

She went home to bed early. But she did not sleep.

After a while, her mother came and sat on the edge of her bed in the dark, the way she used to when Sadie Belle was very small.

"You're worrying," said her mother. "I can hear you worrying from the kitchen."

"What if it doesn't come up in the notch?" whispered Sadie Belle. "With everybody there? What if I made them all get up in the cold for nothing?"

Her mother stroked the soft fur between her ears for a while.

"Then everybody will have seen a sunrise," she said. "Over the creek, with the mist coming up, and the heron, and their friends beside them, and cornbread. I don't think anybody in this Hollow will ever call that nothing." She smiled in the dark. "And besides. You've watched it every morning for a week. You and Beau and Benny. You know what you know."

Sadie Belle thought about the row of pegs on Benny's board, each one a claw's width from the last, marching steadily towards the notch.

"We do know," she said slowly.

"Then go to sleep," said her mother. "You've an early start."


Chapter Eight — Halfway Morning

They came in the dark.

All of them. Charlie came wrapped in Maggie Mae's shawl, too sleepy to talk, which for Charlie was very unusual. Benny and Barney came yawning side by side, Barney still wearing one slipper. Brody came with a blanket big enough for three, and shared it. Reba came with a basket of warm cornbread. Rusty came by the proper path, for once. Maverick came gliding down out of the dark and landed with only a very small wobble. The rabbit family came, and the miller and his wife, and families from all along the creek who had heard Beau and come to see what the fuss was about.

And Great-Grandpappy Buck came last, slowly, with his walking-stick, leaning on Brody's arm.

Sadie Belle and Beau sat in the hollow of the flat rock, with Benny's peg-board in front of them. Everybody else gathered on the grass behind and beside them, in a soft, rustling, whispering crowd.

"Remember," said Sadie Belle's mother, to everybody. "Look at the ridge and the stones. Never straight at the sun."

The sky turned from black to deep blue. The stars went out, one at a time. A thin line of gold came creeping along the top of Sunrise Ridge, brighter and brighter.

And then the sun came up.

It came up exactly in the notch. Right in the middle of the gap between the two round hills, as if the ridge had opened a door for it.

And the light came straight down the valley. It crossed the creek. It touched the bottom stone, and the bottom stone lit up gold. Then the next, and the next, one after another up the bank, like lamps being lit along a path, until all seven white stones were glowing in the dew, and the light reached the flat rock at the top and fell across Sadie Belle and Beau sitting in the hollow, and lit them too.

Nobody said a single word.

Then, from behind them, there came a small sound. It was Great-Grandpappy Buck, standing very still, leaning on Brody, with the gold light on his old face.

"Halfway Morning," he whispered. "That's what she called it. My grandmother. I remember now."

Everybody turned to look at him.

"Halfway between the end of harvest and the Longest Night," said Buck slowly, as if he were reading the words off the inside of his own memory. "The morning the sun comes up in the notch. And the whole Hollow comes down to the white stones before dawn, and stands quiet till the sun's clear of the ridge. And then everybody tells one small wonder they've noticed this year. Just one. Something nobody else saw." He shook his head. "I came here once, in her arms. I'd forgotten all of it. Every bit."

"Why did everybody stop coming?" asked Sadie Belle softly.

Buck thought about it.

"I don't suppose anybody decided to stop," he said. "I expect folk got busy one year, and missed it. And then the next. And the moss grew. And the ones who remembered got old, like me." He looked at the seven glowing stones. "It wasn't lost all at once. Just a little at a time, when nobody was looking."


Chapter Nine — One Small Wonder

When the sun had climbed clear of the notch and the gold light had spread across the whole valley, the crowd stood quiet for a while longer, just looking.

Then Charlie, who had woken up properly at last, tugged at Maggie Mae's shawl.

"Can we do the wonders?" she whispered. "Like Grandpappy said?"

So they did.

Nobody had ever done it before, or not for so long that it was the same as never. But it turned out to be the easiest thing in the world. They stood in a loose ring round the flat rock in the early sunlight, passing Reba's cornbread from paw to paw, and one by one, they each told one small wonder that nobody else had seen.

The miller had seen a spider spin a web between the spokes of the mill wheel, and the wheel turn all the way round, and the web come back up the other side, unbroken.

The littlest rabbit had seen a snail with a stripy shell, like a sweet.

Benny had seen the sun move one claw's width every morning for a week, and said that was the most surprising thing he had ever measured.

Rusty had found a cliff of white stone at the top of High Ridge, chasing a butterfly, and now he knew what it was for.

Maverick, after a long pause, said that from very high up at dawn, he had seen the shadow of the whole Hollow lying across the valley, every tree and every roof, and then shrinking as the sun rose. He said it in a very quiet voice, not showing off at all.

Maggie Mae had seen moss come off a stone in a single piece, like a little green coat.

Brody had heard, one evening by the creek, a frog singing exactly the note his banjo could never quite reach, and had sat down and listened until it stopped.

Reba had found, in the very back of her pantry, a jar of blackberry jam from two summers ago that she had somehow never eaten, which everybody agreed was the greatest wonder of all, and which she had brought, and shared.

Barney had seen his brother laugh at a peg called Benny, and said that was rarer than any sunrise, and Benny threw a crust at him.

When it came to Beau, he went quiet for a moment.

"I saw a heron," he said. "Standing on one leg. Gold all down one side. On the fifth morning, when I was cross and wanted to go home." He looked at Sadie Belle. "Somebody showed it to me. Otherwise I'd have missed it."

Sadie Belle looked down at her hooves, and smiled, and did not say anything for a moment.

Then it was her turn.

She looked at the seven white stones, glowing in the grass. She looked at Great-Grandpappy Buck, wiping his eyes with a big spotted handkerchief. She looked at all of them, the whole of Hickory Hollow, standing together on a cold creek bank at sunrise.

"I saw a white stone in the grass," she said. "Just one. And I wondered why it was there."

Nobody said anything after that. The creek talked. The heron lifted off and flapped slowly away downstream. The sun climbed.

"Next year," said Charlie. "We have to come back next year."

"And every year," said Maggie Mae.

"Who'll know when it is?" asked Brody.

Everybody looked at Sadie Belle and Beau, and then at Benny and his board.

"We will," said Beau. "We'll start the pegs a week before. And then I'll tell everybody."

"You're brilliant at telling," said Sadie Belle.


Chapter Ten — The Dark Before

That evening, Great-Grandpappy Buck did not sit in his rocking chair.

He sat instead on the top step of his porch, with his walking-stick across his knees, because he said that after a morning like that, an old beaver wanted to be a little nearer the ground. The others came drifting up the creek path in the early dusk, in ones and twos, all of them yawning, all of them saying they were not the least bit tired.

Sadie Belle and Beau came last, with her mother and his grandmother walking behind. They sat down on the step below Buck's.

Buck had finished his little wooden wren. He turned it over in his old paws for a while. Then he reached down and set it on the step between Sadie Belle and Beau.

"For the two who kept coming back," he said. "You'll have to share it."

Beau picked it up very carefully, as if it might fly away, and looked at it, and then handed it to Sadie Belle. Sadie Belle looked at it and handed it back. In the end they set it on the step between them, where they could both see it.

The sky over Sunrise Ridge was going dark now, a deep, clear, cold blue. You could just make out the notch, a soft dip in the black line of the ridge.

"Grandpappy," said Charlie sleepily, from Brody's lap. "Were you cross? Getting up so early?"

Buck chuckled.

"I was, a bit," he admitted. "In the cold and the dark, I'll confess I was. I thought, what's an old beaver doing sitting on a wet creek bank in the dark?" He looked out at the ridge. "And then the sun came up in the notch."

He was quiet for a moment.

"You can't rush a sunrise, young'uns," he said softly. "So you might as well enjoy the dark before it."

Nobody answered. The last light faded from behind Weather Ridge. One by one, the first stars came out above the Hollow.

Beau was asleep before the third star. He fell asleep sitting up, with his chin slowly sinking towards his chest and his blue eyes closing, and his grandmother came and put her shawl round him and did not wake him.

Sadie Belle stayed awake a little longer. She sat on Buck's step with the little wooden wren beside her, looking out across the dark valley towards Sunrise Ridge, where the notch was waiting for next year.

Down at the slow bend below the Old Mill, in the frosty dark, seven white stones lay in their straight line in the grass, clean of moss now, faintly glimmering under the stars like a path that somebody had remembered.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
