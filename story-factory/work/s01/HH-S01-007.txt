Chapter One — Reba Volunteers

There is a day every autumn in Hickory Hollow that Reba the raccoon loved more than any other, and it was not a festival, or a party, or anybody's birthday.

It was Nut Day.

On Nut Day, when the hickory nuts had all come down and been gathered and dried, the whole Hollow brought its baskets to the old stone nut shed behind the Old Mill. And there the nuts were sorted, and counted, and shared out fairly, so that every family in the valley went home with a full basket for the winter, whether they had gathered a lot or a little.

The sorting was the important part. Because not every hickory nut is a good one.

There are the shagbark nuts, from the big shaggy-barked trees along the creek, sweet and rich and the best thing in the world in a cake. There are the bitternuts, from the smooth grey trees up on the slope, which look very nearly the same from the outside but taste so bitter that one bite will make you pull a face for a week. And there are the empties, the nuts that look perfectly fine but have nothing inside but dust, because a weevil got there first.

Every year, the miller's wife ran the sorting. She did it slowly and carefully, the old way: a test nut cracked from every pile, every basket labelled with a ribbon in that family's colour, the bitternuts kept in a separate bin for the woods, and the empties found by dropping every nut in a tub of water and seeing which ones floated.

But this year, the miller's wife had a bad back, and could not bend over the baskets.

"I'll do it!" said Reba, before anybody else could open their mouth. "Me! I'll organise the whole thing!"

Everybody in the nut shed turned to look at her.

Reba was a young raccoon with soft grey-brown fur, a black mask across her bright, curious eyes, a magnificent ringed tail, a pink bow above one ear and a red-and-pink flowered neckerchief. She was the most curious creature in the Hollow, and she had very clever paws, and she loved food more than almost anything.

"You?" said the miller's wife, from her chair.

"I know nuts better than anybody," said Reba. "I've eaten more of them than anybody. Probably more than everybody put together."

That was very possibly true.

The miller's wife looked at Reba for a long moment over her spectacles. Then she smiled.

"Well," she said. "Somebody has to. And you've certainly got the paws for it. Mind you do it properly, now. Test nut from every pile. A ribbon on every basket. And the float tub, for every single nut."

"Every single nut," said Reba solemnly. "Leave it to me."

Barney, who had come in with his brother carrying the beavers' baskets, poked his head round the door.

"Can I help?" he said. "I'm brilliant at sorting. I'll turn it into a game. We could have a nut race! Or nut skittles! Or..."

"No thank you, Barney," said Reba quickly. "This is serious work. I've got a system."

She did not have a system yet. But she was sure she would think of one.


Chapter Two — Reba's System

The nut shed was a cool, dim, stone building with a beaten earth floor, and by the middle of the morning it was full of nuts.

There were baskets of them along every wall. There were sacks of them in the corners. There was a great heap of them in the middle of the floor, brown and knobbly and smelling of autumn. And there was Reba, standing in the middle of it all with her paws on her hips, looking at the heap, and the baskets, and the ribbons, and the big wooden float tub in the corner.

It was a great deal of nuts.

"Test nut from every pile," she said to herself. "Ribbon on every basket. Float tub for every single nut." She looked at the heap again. "Every single nut. That's going to take all week."

She picked up a nut and turned it over in her clever paws.

"But I know nuts," she said. "I don't need to crack one from every pile. I can tell by sniffing."

She sniffed it. It smelled like a hickory nut.

"Sweet," she decided, and dropped it in a basket.

She picked up another one and sniffed it. It also smelled like a hickory nut.

"Also sweet," she said, a little less certainly.

Then she had a better idea. A much better idea. If you wanted to know whether a nut was sweet or bitter, the quickest way to find out was not to crack one and look at it. It was to crack one and eat it.

So she did. It was sweet. It was delicious.

She cracked another, from the next pile. That was sweet too. Even more delicious.

She cracked one from the pile after that, and ate it, and then another from the same pile, just to be sure, and then a third, because the second had been so nice.

"This is a brilliant system," said Reba happily, with her mouth full.

After a while, she noticed that she had stopped sorting and was mostly just eating. So she put the nuts she had sorted into baskets. She was nearly sure she remembered which pile each one had come from. Mostly.

Then there were the ribbons. Every family had a colour: blue for the beavers, green for the rabbits, yellow for Maggie Mae, red for Brody, and so on, a whole rainbow of ribbons on a hook by the door. Tying a ribbon on every basket would take ages.

"I'll remember," said Reba confidently. "I've got a very good memory. The beavers' basket is the one by the door. The rabbits' is the one next to it. Maggie Mae's is the big round one. I'll just remember."

And then there was the float tub.

Reba looked at it. It was very big, and the water in it was very cold, and dropping every single nut in it one by one and fishing them out again was going to take the rest of the day at the very least.

"Most of them will sink anyway," she said to herself. "Nearly all of them. I'll just do a few."

So she did a few.

Then she did one more, to be fair.

Then she sat down on an upturned basket in the middle of the nut shed, cracked a shagbark nut, ate it, and admired her work.

There was a pattering at the door, and Rusty the fox put his head round it, with his white-tipped tail swishing behind him.

"Hello, Reba," he said. He looked at the baskets with no ribbons, and the float tub with hardly a ripple in it, and the little heap of cracked shells round Reba's feet. His amber eyes narrowed thoughtfully. "That was quick. Taking a shortcut, are you?"

"No," said Reba, rather too fast. "It's a system."

Rusty looked at her for a moment longer. He knew a great deal about shortcuts. He knew that some of them got you home early, and some of them got you home with brambles in your tail, and that you could not always tell which was which until you were halfway along.

"All right," he said. "Just, the miller's wife's way takes all week for a reason, I think."

"My way's better," said Reba.

"Maybe," said Rusty, and went off to help his family carry their basket, and Reba told herself that she had not felt a small, prickly flutter of doubt at all.


Chapter Three — Barney Watches

Barney did not go away.

He had been told this was serious work, so he sat very seriously on the wall outside the nut shed door and watched through the gap. He was not much good at sitting still, but he was very good at watching, when he wanted to be, and he watched everything Reba did.

He watched her sniff the first nut and put it in a basket. He watched her start eating instead of sorting. He watched her look at the ribbons, and decide not to tie them. He watched her look at the float tub for a long time and do only a few.

And he watched, near the end of the afternoon, while Reba was busy cracking a particularly fine shagbark nut, as a whole basket of bitternuts from the slope trees got knocked over by her tail and rolled across the floor, and mixed itself right into the big heap of sweet ones.

Reba did not notice. She was too busy eating.

Barney opened his mouth to shout. Then he shut it again.

She had said no thank you. She had said she had a system. And Barney, who had been told "no thank you, Barney" quite often in his life, knew how it felt to be told you were in the way.

So he did not say anything.

But he did not stop watching, either.

By evening, the nut shed was full of baskets, all of them heaped to the brim and looking very handsome. Reba stood in the doorway with her ringed tail swishing and felt extremely proud.

"Done!" she announced to the miller's wife, who had come hobbling over to look. "Every single nut!"

The miller's wife looked round at the baskets. Her eyebrows went up a little.

"No ribbons?" she said.

"I've got them all in my head," said Reba, tapping it. "It's quicker."

"And you tested every pile?"

"Every pile," said Reba. Which was nearly true, if you counted eating as testing. "And did the float tub."

That part was less true. She did not say how many she had floated.

The miller's wife looked at her for a moment longer. Then she nodded slowly.

"Well," she said. "You've worked hard, I can see that. Hand them out in the morning, then."

Reba skipped all the way home.

Barney walked home much more slowly, beside his brother, with his paws in his pockets.

"You're quiet," said Benny. "You're never quiet."

"I'm thinking," said Barney.

"About what?"

Barney did not answer. He was thinking about a basket of bitternuts rolling across a floor.


Chapter Four — Nut Day Goes Wrong

The next morning, Reba handed out the nuts.

She stood at the door of the nut shed in her best bow, and as each family came up the path, she gave them a basket. She remembered most of them. She was nearly certain about the rest.

By lunchtime, it had all begun to go wrong.

Maggie Mae came first. She had taken her big round basket home and baked her famous hickory-nut cake for the Nut Day supper, the way she did every year. She came back up the path holding a slice of it on a plate, and her face was very polite and very pale.

"Reba, sugar," she said carefully. "I wonder. Could you taste this?"

Reba took a bite.

Her whole face folded up like a paper bag. Her eyes watered. Her tongue tried to climb out of her mouth and escape. It was the most bitter thing she had ever tasted in her life. It was bitternut, through and through.

"Is it supposed to taste like that?" asked Maggie Mae.

"Mmmfff," said Reba, because she could not make her mouth work.

Then came Brody, carrying the red-ribbon family's basket, which had turned out to be almost entirely empties. Every nut he cracked had nothing inside but dust.

"I don't understand it," said Brody, puzzled, holding up a hollow shell. "Last year our basket was full of good ones."

Then came the rabbit family, all nine of them, with a basket so small that it would barely have fed one rabbit, let alone nine. And behind them came the miller himself, carrying a basket so enormous he could hardly see round it.

"I think you've given me the rabbits' share," he said, "and the rabbits mine."

Reba looked at the rabbits' tiny basket. She looked at the miller's huge one. She tried very hard to remember which basket had been by the door, and which next to it, and which one was big and round.

She could not remember at all.

"Oh," she said in a small voice.

By the middle of the afternoon, half the Hollow had come back up the path with a problem. Too many nuts, or too few. Bitter ones in the sweet baskets. Empties where there ought to have been good ones. Somebody had got two baskets and somebody else had got none.

Everybody was very kind about it. Nobody shouted.

But they all looked at Reba, and they all asked the same thing.

"What happened?"


Chapter Five — It Must Be the Weather

Reba stood in the doorway of the nut shed with half the Hollow looking at her, and felt hot all over, from the tip of her nose to the end of her ringed tail.

She knew what had happened. She had sniffed instead of testing. She had eaten instead of sorting. She had not tied the ribbons. She had not done the float tub. She had been in a hurry, and she had been greedy, and she had been much too sure of herself.

But all those faces were looking at her. And she could not make the words come out.

So different words came out instead.

"It must be the weather," said Reba.

There was a pause.

"The weather?" said Maggie Mae.

"Yes," said Reba, and now that she had started, the words came faster. "It was a very funny summer. All that rain and then all that sun. I expect the trees got muddled. I expect some of them grew bitter nuts instead of sweet ones this year, and some of them grew empty ones. It happens sometimes. I've heard."

She had not heard. She had made it up that very moment.

"Well, I never," said the miller. "A muddled year. That would explain it."

"Poor trees," said the littlest rabbit.

And everybody nodded, and looked worried about the trees instead, and went away down the path with their wrong baskets, talking about the funny summer.

Reba stood alone in the doorway.

She had thought she would feel relieved. Instead, she felt as though she had swallowed a bitternut whole and it had got stuck somewhere in the middle of her.

She did not notice Barney, standing quite still beside the wall of the nut shed, where he had been all along.

He did not say anything. He only looked at her. And then he turned and walked away down the path, very slowly, with his paws in his pockets.

That evening, Reba did not eat any supper. She told her mother she was not hungry. Her mother was so surprised that she felt her forehead to see if she was poorly.

Later, when the lamp was out, her mother came and sat on the edge of her bed in the dark, the way she used to when Reba was very small.

"Something's the matter," said her mother quietly. "You didn't touch your supper. You always touch your supper. You usually touch everybody's supper."

Reba very nearly told her. The words were right there, just behind her teeth. But they were stuck fast, the way the bitternut taste had stuck.

"I'm just tired," she said. "It was a long day."

Her mother stroked the fur between her ears for a while, the way she had always done, and did not ask anything more.

"Well," she said at last. "If it turns out to be something more than tired, you know where I am."

Then she went out, and Reba lay in bed and stared at the ceiling.

It must be the weather, she had said.

The words went round and round in her head like a nut rolling across a floor.


Chapter Six — The Fib Grows

The trouble with a fib is that it does not stay where you put it.

By the next morning, the whole Hollow had heard that the hickory trees were sick.

The rabbits had told the otters, and the otters had told the families along the creek, and by the time the story reached Fern Meadow, it had grown. The trees were not just muddled. They were poorly. They had caught something from the funny summer. Somebody said it might be catching, from one tree to another. Somebody else said there might be no good nuts at all next year.

Charlie came running up to Reba on the bridge with her blue eyes huge.

"Reba! Did you hear? The hickory trees are poorly! All of them! What if they never get better? What if there are no more nuts, ever? What will the squirrels do? What will I do?"

Reba opened her mouth and nothing came out.

Then she saw a little procession coming along the creek path. It was Great-Grandpappy Buck, walking slowly with his walking-stick, and behind him Benny with a ladder over his shoulder, and Brody carrying a basket of tools.

"Where are you going?" called Charlie.

"Up the slope, to the bitternut trees," said Buck. "And then along the creek to the shagbarks. I hear they're poorly. I've been tending those trees for a very long time, and if they've caught something, I'd like to know what. Benny's going to help me look at the bark. And Brody's going to carry. We'll likely be at it all day."

All day. Great-Grandpappy Buck, who was so old, and walked so slowly, climbing up the steep slope to look at trees that were perfectly well. All day, because of something Reba had made up in a single moment so that she would not have to say I did it.

Reba looked down at the bridge.

Barney was sitting at the far end of it, dangling his feet over the water. He was not splashing. He was not laughing. He was just sitting.

Reba walked over and sat down beside him. For a long time, neither of them said anything.

"You saw," said Reba at last. "In the nut shed. Didn't you."

Barney nodded.

"The bitternuts got knocked over," he said. "By your tail. They rolled into the good ones. And you didn't do the ribbons. Or the float tub."

"Why didn't you tell anybody?"

Barney thought about it.

"Because it's yours to tell," he said. "Not mine." He kicked his feet. "And I kept hoping you would."

Reba looked at the water running under the bridge, and then up at the slope, where Great-Grandpappy Buck was slowly starting to climb.

The bitternut feeling in the middle of her was so heavy now that she could hardly breathe.

"I have to tell," she said. "Don't I."

"I think so," said Barney. "But I'll come with you. If you want."


Chapter Seven — Reba Tells

They caught up with Great-Grandpappy Buck at the bottom of the slope.

It was a long way for an old beaver, and he had stopped to lean on his walking-stick and get his breath, with Benny and Brody waiting beside him. Charlie had come running after Reba and Barney, and Maggie Mae after Charlie, and by the time Reba reached the bottom of the slope there were rather more creatures there than she had wanted.

She very nearly turned round and ran.

But Barney was standing beside her, and he did not move. So she did not either.

"Grandpappy Buck," said Reba. "Please don't go up the slope. The trees aren't poorly."

Everybody looked at her.

"It wasn't the weather," said Reba. Her voice was wobbling, so she said it faster, to get it out before it could stop. "It was me. I didn't test the nuts properly. I ate them instead. I didn't tie the ribbons, because I thought I could remember, and I couldn't. I didn't do the float tub, because it was slow. And I knocked over the bitternuts with my tail and didn't notice, and they got mixed in with the good ones." She took a breath. "And then when everybody asked what happened, I was too ashamed to say, so I said it was the weather. I made it up. And now everybody's worried about the trees, and you were going to climb all the way up there for nothing. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

It was very quiet at the bottom of the slope.

Charlie's mouth had fallen open. Maggie Mae's eyebrows had gone up. Brody looked at the basket of tools in his arms, and then put it down very gently on the grass.

Great-Grandpappy Buck did not say anything for a long moment. He looked at Reba over his spectacles, and then up at the slope, and then back at Reba.

"Well now," he said at last. "That was a hard thing to say, wasn't it?"

Reba nodded. She could not speak.

"Harder than the fib?"

Reba thought about it.

"No," she said slowly. "The fib was harder. I've been carrying it since yesterday. It was the heaviest thing I ever carried."

Buck nodded, as if that was exactly the answer he had expected.

"I'll admit," he said, "I'm glad not to be climbing that slope." His whiskers twitched. "These old knees were not looking forward to it."

Then Charlie, who had been very quiet, said in a small, wondering voice:

"So the trees aren't poorly at all?"

"Not one bit," said Reba. "They're perfectly well. It was only me."

"Oh, thank goodness," said Charlie, and threw her arms round Reba's middle. "I was so worried about the squirrels."

Reba hugged her back, and found that the bitternut feeling had gone, all at once, as if somebody had lifted it out of her.

"But the nuts are still all wrong," said Brody, worried. "Everybody's still got the wrong baskets."

Reba wiped her nose. She stood up straight. She straightened her pink bow.

"Then I'll fix it," she said. "All of it. Properly, this time. Every single nut."

"On your own?" said Maggie Mae.

Reba hesitated. She looked at Barney.

"No," she said. "Not on my own."


Chapter Eight — The Great Nut Boat Race

So the next morning, the whole Hollow brought its baskets back to the nut shed. And this time, Reba did it properly.

It was a great deal of work. There was no getting round that. Every nut had to be sorted again from the very beginning. But it turned out to be much less work than Reba had feared, because of Barney.

"The float tub!" he cried, the moment he walked in. "We'll make it a race!"

"It's not a game, Barney," began Reba, from habit. Then she stopped herself. "Actually. Go on. What sort of race?"

Barney bounced so hard that the float tub sloshed.

"The Great Nut Boat Race," he announced. "Every nut gets launched into the tub. The good ones are heavy and full, so they sink straight to the bottom. They lose the race. The empty ones are light, so they float. They win the race, and they get scooped out and put in the empties bin, for the woods. Last nut floating is the champion!"

So they did it. Charlie and the littlest rabbits launched the nuts, one by one, plop, plop, plop, into the cold water, and cheered every time one sank. Barney stood at the edge with a little net and scooped out the floaters, and gave each one a name and a short speech before it went in the empties bin. "Farewell, brave Captain Hollow-Nut. You floated well." The empties turned out to be a great many more than anybody had thought, and the race went on all morning, and the shed was full of plopping and splashing and laughing.

Reba did the hard part. She sat at the long table with a pile of sinkers and her clever paws, and sorted the sweet shagbarks from the bitternuts.

It is not easy to tell them apart. They are nearly the same size and nearly the same colour. But Reba had the cleverest paws in the Hollow, and she found that if she closed her eyes and felt each nut properly, the shagbarks were rougher and heavier, with thick, knobbly shells, and the bitternuts were smoother and thinner and lighter, as if they did not have as much inside them to be proud of.

"How do you know?" asked Brody, watching her.

"I don't know how I know," said Reba. "I just feel it. If I go slowly."

"You couldn't feel it last time," said Brody, without meaning anything unkind.

"Last time I wasn't going slowly," said Reba. "Last time I was eating."

And she cracked one test nut from every single pile and tasted it to be sure. Just one. And did not eat a single nut that was not a test nut, all morning, though her tummy rumbled so loudly that Barney named it the Thunder of the Nut Shed.

Then she tied the ribbons. Every one. Blue for the beavers. Green for the rabbits. Yellow for Maggie Mae. Red for Brody. A whole rainbow, on every single basket, in neat little bows. It took a long time, and her paws ached, and she did not mind at all.


Chapter Nine — The Right Baskets

By the middle of the afternoon, it was done.

Every nut had been floated. Every pile had been tested. Every basket had its ribbon. The bitternuts were in their own bin by the door, to go back to the woods for whoever liked them. The empties were in another bin, with a little sign Barney had painted that said BRAVE FLOATERS. And along the walls of the nut shed stood a row of full, fair, properly-sorted baskets, one for every family in the Hollow, each with its ribbon.

The miller's wife came hobbling in on her stick to look.

She walked slowly along the row of baskets, looking at every ribbon. She picked up a nut from the beavers' basket and cracked it and looked at the sweet kernel inside. She peered into the empties bin and smiled at the sign. Then she turned to Reba.

"That," she said, "is the best-sorted Nut Day I've seen in thirty years."

Reba went pink under her mask.

"It's the second one," she said. "The first one was the worst."

"Most things are better the second time," said the miller's wife. "That's what second times are for."

Then the families came, one by one, and Reba handed each of them their basket. And this time, at every one, she said the same thing.

"Here's your basket. It's the right one, I promise. And I'm sorry about the first one."

The rabbits got a basket big enough for nine. The miller got a basket the right size for a miller. Brody got a basket full of good, sweet nuts, not one empty among them, and cracked one on the spot to check, and beamed.

And Maggie Mae got her big round basket back, full of the best shagbarks in the whole shed, which Reba had picked out especially.

"I'll bake the cake again," said Maggie Mae. "Tonight. For the Nut Day supper, a day late." She looked at Reba with her emerald eyes twinkling. "I'll need a taster, though. Somebody who knows a bitter nut from a sweet one."

"Me?" said Reba.

"Who else?" said Maggie Mae. "Just a taste, mind."

"Just a taste," promised Reba.

As the last family went off down the path with their basket, Reba turned to Barney, who was sitting on the edge of the float tub, swinging his feet.

"Thank you," she said. "For not telling. And for the race. And for coming with me down the slope." She paused. "And I'm sorry I said no thank you, the first time. When you wanted to help."

Barney grinned so widely that his whiskers stood out sideways.

"That's all right," he said. "I'm used to it. But it's nicer when people say yes."

"Next year," said Reba, "you're in charge of the float tub. Officially."

"Officially?" breathed Barney. "Can I have a hat?"

"You can have a hat," said Reba.


Chapter Ten — Small Honesties

That evening there was a Nut Day supper on Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch, a day late, and nobody minded in the slightest.

Maggie Mae's hickory-nut cake sat in the middle of the long table, golden and rich and smelling of honey, and Reba had tasted it first, just a taste, and declared it perfect. There was cornbread too, and baked apples, and a big jug of hot berry-cordial. The lantern by the door was lit, and the creek ran quietly below, and the first stars were coming out over Sunrise Ridge.

Barney wore his hat. It was a paper one, folded out of the BRAVE FLOATERS sign, and it said FLOAT TUB OFFICER on the front in Benny's careful writing.

When the cake was all gone and the plates were scraped clean, Great-Grandpappy Buck settled back in his old hickory rocking chair. It gave its first slow creak, and the porch went quiet.

He did not reach for his banjo straight away. He sat looking out over the dark creek for a while, rocking slowly, with Bramble's quilt over his knees.

"Reba," he said at last. "That fib of yours. About the weather."

Reba's ears went down a little.

"How big was it, when you first told it?"

Reba thought.

"Tiny," she said. "Just a few words. It came out before I could stop it."

"And how big was it by the next morning?"

"Huge," said Reba. "The trees were poorly. It was catching. There'd be no nuts next year. You were going to climb the whole slope." She shook her head. "It got bigger every time somebody said it. And I couldn't get it back."

Buck nodded slowly.

"That's the way of them," he said. "A little honest thing, said straight away, stays little. It's a bit uncomfortable for a minute, and then it's done. But a little fib, you put it down and walk away, and when you come back it's grown." He rocked. "Small honesties keep the big troubles away, young'uns."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Buck reached for his banjo and began to play, a slow, easy, rolling tune, and the friends on the porch settled down to listen.

Charlie curled up between Maggie Mae and Brody with her tail over her nose. Benny and Barney sat side by side on the top step, Barney's paper hat slipping slowly down over one eye. Rusty, Sadie Belle, Beau and Maverick lay along the boards, listening, the cool night air coming up off the creek.

Reba sat at the very end of the porch, looking out at the hickory trees along the creek, black against the stars. They were perfectly well. They always had been.

Her tummy rumbled once, very softly.

She did not mind. It had been a very good supper, and she had eaten only her share.

Down behind the Old Mill, in the cool, dark nut shed, the baskets were gone and the floor was swept, and the two bins by the door sat side by side in the moonlight, one full of bitternuts for the woods, and one full of brave floaters, with their painted sign.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
