Chapter One — Reba Volunteers

There is a day every autumn in Hickory Hollow that Reba the raccoon loved more than any other, and it was not a festival, or a party, or anybody's birthday.

It was Nut Day.

On Nut Day, when the hickory nuts had all come down and been gathered and dried, the whole Hollow brought its baskets to the old stone nut shed behind the Old Mill. There the nuts were sorted and shared out fairly, so that every family went home with a full basket for the winter, whether they had gathered a lot or a little.

The sorting was the important part, because not every hickory nut is a good one.

There are shagbark nuts, from the big shaggy-barked trees along the creek: large, with thick, hard, four-ridged shells, and sweet, rich kernels that are the best thing in the world in a cake. There are bitternuts, from the smooth grey trees up on the slope: smaller and rounder, with thin, smooth shells, and kernels so bitter that one bite will make you pull a face for a week. Tipped together in a heap, in a hurry, in a dim shed, they can be very hard to tell apart.

And there are the empties: nuts that look perfectly fine but have nothing inside but dust, because a weevil got there first and left by a tiny round hole.

Every year, the miller's wife ran the sorting, the slow old way. A test nut cracked on the nut-stone from every pile. Every basket labelled with a ribbon in that family's colour. The bitternuts put in a separate bin for the woods. And every single nut dropped in the big float tub, because good nuts are heavy and sink, and empties are light and float.

But this year, the miller's wife had hurt her back, and could not bend over the baskets.

"I'll do it!" said Reba, before anybody else could open their mouth. "I'll organise the whole thing!"

Everybody in the nut shed turned to look at her.

Reba was a young raccoon with soft grey-brown fur, a black mask across her bright eyes, a magnificent ringed tail, a pink bow above one ear and a red-and-pink flowered neckerchief. She was the most curious creature in the Hollow, with the cleverest paws, and she loved food more than almost anything.

"You?" said the miller's wife, from her chair.

"I know nuts better than anybody," said Reba. "I've eaten more of them than anybody."

That was very possibly true.

The miller's wife looked at her over her spectacles. Then she smiled.

"Well. Somebody has to. You do it properly, now. A test nut from every pile. A ribbon on every basket. The float tub for every single nut. It's slow, I know. It takes me the best part of a week. But there's a reason for every bit of it."

"Every single nut," said Reba solemnly. "Leave it to me."

At that moment there was a clatter in the doorway, and Barney came in backwards, carrying one end of the beavers' basket while Benny carried the other.

"Did somebody say organise?" said Barney, setting his end down with a thump. "Can I help? I'm brilliant at organising. I'll make it a game. Nut skittles! Nut races! Nut..."

"No thank you, Barney," said Reba quickly. "This is serious work. I've got a system."

She did not have a system yet. But she was quite sure she would think of one.


Chapter Two — Reba's System

By the middle of the morning, the nut shed was full of nuts.

There were baskets along every wall and sacks in every corner, and in the middle of the floor sat a great brown knobbly heap that smelled of autumn. Reba stood in front of it with her paws on her hips.

It was a great deal of nuts.

"A test nut from every pile," she said to herself. "A ribbon on every basket. The float tub for every single nut." She looked at the heap. "That's going to take all week."

She picked up a nut and turned it over in her clever paws.

"But I know nuts," she decided. "I can tell by sniffing."

She sniffed it. It smelled of hickory nut.

"Sweet," she said, and dropped it in a basket.

She sniffed another. It also smelled of hickory nut.

"Also sweet," she said, a little less sure.

Then she had a better idea. If you wanted to know whether a nut was sweet or bitter, the quickest way was not to crack it and look. It was to crack it and eat it.

So she set a nut on the nut-stone, tapped it with the hammer-stone, and ate it. It was sweet. Delicious.

She cracked one from the next pile. Sweet again. Even more delicious.

She cracked one from the pile after that, and then another from the same pile, just to be sure, and then a third, because the second had been so very nice.

"This," said Reba, with her mouth full, "is a brilliant system."

She sorted for a while. Then she ate for a while. Then she noticed she had mostly stopped sorting and was mostly just eating, so she hurried to catch up, scooping nuts into baskets by the double pawful.

Then there were the ribbons, a whole rainbow of them on a hook by the door: blue for the beavers, green for the rabbits, yellow for Maggie Mae, red for Brody's family, and so on. Tying one on every basket would take ages.

"I'll remember," said Reba confidently. "The beavers' basket is the one by the door. The rabbits' is next to it. Maggie Mae's is the big round one. Easy."

And then there was the float tub.

She looked at it. It was very big, and the water in it was very cold.

"Most of them will sink anyway," she said. "I'll just do a few."

So she did a few. The ones that floated, she dropped into a spare basket in the corner, to deal with later.

She did not deal with them later. She forgot all about them.

Late in the afternoon, carrying a basket of bitternuts from the slope trees across to the bin by the door, she spotted a particularly fine fat shagbark on the floor, and bent to pick it up, and her magnificent ringed tail swept the whole basket of bitternuts off the edge of the table. They bounced across the floor and rolled straight into the bottom of the big heap of sweet ones.

Reba, who was busy cracking the fat shagbark, did not even look round.

Then she scooped the bottom of the heap, bitternuts and all, into the big round basket that was Maggie Mae's, because it was the biggest and there was a lot left over.

"Done!" said Reba at last, and sat down on an upturned crate to admire her work.


Chapter Three — Barney Watches

Barney had not gone away.

He had been told this was serious work, so he sat very seriously on the wall outside the nut shed and watched through the open door. He was not much good at sitting still. But he was very good at watching, when he wanted to be.

He saw the sniffing. He saw the eating. He saw the ribbons stay on their hook, and the float tub stay almost still, and the floaters go into the spare basket in the corner. And he saw Reba's tail sweep the bitternuts off the table and into the heap, and Reba not notice, and Reba scoop the heap into Maggie Mae's big round basket.

Barney opened his mouth to shout. Then he shut it again.

She had said no thank you. She had said she had a system. And Barney, who had been told "no thank you, Barney" a great many times in his life, knew exactly how that felt.

So he said nothing. But he went on watching.

Earlier that afternoon, before the bitternuts went over, Rusty had put his head round the door, with his white-tipped tail swishing behind him.

"That was quick," he said. He looked at the baskets with no ribbons and the still water in the tub, and his amber eyes narrowed thoughtfully. "Taking a shortcut, are you?"

"It's a system," said Reba.

Rusty knew a great deal about shortcuts. He knew some of them got you home early and some of them got you home with brambles in your tail, and that you could not always tell which was which until you were halfway along.

"The miller's wife's way takes all week for a reason, I reckon," he said.

"My way's better," said Reba.

"Maybe," said Rusty, and went off to help his family, and Reba did not feel even the smallest prickle of doubt. Very nearly.

By evening, every basket was heaped to the brim and looked very handsome. The miller's wife came hobbling over on her stick to look.

"No ribbons?" she said.

"I've got them all in my head," said Reba, tapping it. "It's quicker."

The miller's wife looked round slowly. She seemed about to ask something else. Then she rubbed her back, and winced, and decided against it.

"Well," she said. "You've worked hard, I can see that. Hand them out in the morning."

Reba skipped home.

Barney walked home slowly beside his brother, with his paws in his pockets.

"You're quiet," said Benny. "You're never quiet."

"I'm thinking," said Barney.

"About what?"

Barney did not answer. He was thinking about a spare basket in a corner, and a big round basket by the wall.


Chapter Four — Nut Day Goes Wrong

The next morning, Reba stood at the door of the nut shed in her best bow and handed out the baskets.

By lunchtime, it had all begun to go wrong. And it went wrong in the most spectacular way.

First came Brody, who had been given the spare basket from the corner. He stood in the middle of the path cracking nut after nut on a flat stone, with a growing heap of empty shells round his feet and a look of deep bewilderment on his kind face.

"Empty," he said. "Empty. Empty. Empty." He held one up to the light. "There's a little hole in this one. And this one. Reba, I think my whole basket's empty."

Then the rabbit family arrived, all nine of them, carrying between them a basket so small that the littlest rabbit was wearing it as a hat.

Then the miller arrived, with a basket so enormous that he could not see round it, and walked into the doorpost.

"I think," he said, from behind it, "you've given me the rabbits' share. And them mine."

Reba looked at the tiny basket on the littlest rabbit's head. She looked at the miller's enormous one. She tried very hard to remember which basket had been by the door, and which next to it.

She could not remember at all.

And then, coming up the path, slowly and carefully, very upright and very elegant, came Maggie Mae, carrying a plate with a slice of hickory-nut cake on it.

Maggie Mae had taken her big round basket home last night and stayed up late baking her famous cake for the Nut Day supper. Now she held out the plate with a very polite smile.

"Reba, sugar," she said. "Would you taste this, please?"

Reba took a bite.

Her whole face folded up like a paper bag. Her eyes watered. Her ears went flat. Her tongue tried to climb out of her mouth and escape down the path. It was the most bitter thing she had ever tasted in her life. It was bitternut, all the way through.

"Mmmmffff," said Reba.

"I thought so," said Maggie Mae faintly.

Beau, who had been passing, kindly took a big bite to see if it was really that bad, and then stood very still with his eyes watering and his black ear tufts quivering, and said in a strangled voice, "It's... got a lot of flavour," and went and sat down in the shade.

By the middle of the afternoon, half the Hollow had come back up the path with a problem. Too many nuts or too few. Bitter ones in the sweet baskets. Empties where there ought to have been good ones. Somebody had two baskets and somebody had none.

Nobody shouted. Everybody was very kind.

But they all looked at Reba, and they all asked the same thing.

"What happened?"


Chapter Five — The Funny Autumn

Reba stood in the doorway of the nut shed with half the Hollow looking at her, and went hot all over, from the tip of her nose to the end of her ringed tail.

She knew exactly what had happened. She had sniffed instead of testing. She had eaten instead of sorting. She had not tied the ribbons. She had not done the float tub. She had been in a hurry, and much too sure of herself.

But all those faces were looking at her. And the words would not come.

So other words came out instead.

"It must be the frost," said Reba.

There was a pause.

"The frost?" said Maggie Mae.

"That early hard frost," said Reba, and now that she had started, the words came faster. "Before Harvest Home. It came so suddenly. I expect it muddled the trees. I expect some of them grew bitter nuts this year instead of sweet, and some of them grew empty ones. It happens. I'm almost sure it happens."

She was not sure at all. She had made it up that very second.

"Well, I never," said the miller. "A muddled year. That would explain it."

"Poor trees," said the littlest rabbit, from under her basket.

And everybody nodded, and looked worried about the trees instead, and went away down the path talking about the frost.

Reba stood alone in the doorway.

She had expected to feel better. Instead she felt as though she had swallowed a bitternut whole and it had stuck somewhere in the middle of her.

She did not see Barney, standing quite still by the wall of the nut shed, where he had been all along. He looked at her. Then he turned and walked slowly away down the path, kicking at the leaves.

That evening, Reba did not eat any supper. Her mother was so surprised that she felt her forehead.

Later, when the lamp was out, her mother came and sat on the edge of her bed.

"Something's the matter," she said quietly. "You didn't touch your supper. You always touch your supper."

Reba very nearly told her. The words were right there behind her teeth.

"I can't say yet," she whispered instead. "Not yet. But I think I have to fix something."

Her mother stroked the fur between her ears for a while, and did not push.

"Well," she said. "When you can say, I'll be here. And if it needs fixing, you won't have to fix it on your own."


Chapter Six — The Fib Grows

By the next morning, Reba's fib had wandered off without her. The whole Hollow had heard that the hickory trees were poorly.

The rabbits had told the families along the creek, and by the time the story reached Fern Meadow, it had grown. The trees were not just muddled. The frost had hurt them. It might be catching, from tree to tree. There might be no good nuts at all next year.

Charlie came running onto the bridge with her blue eyes enormous.

"Reba! The hickory trees are poorly! All of them! What if they never get better? What will the squirrels do?"

Reba opened her mouth and nothing came out.

Then she saw a little procession coming slowly along the creek path. It was Great-Grandpappy Buck with his walking-stick, and Benny beside him with his measuring stick, and Brody carrying a basket of string and labels.

"Where are you going?" called Charlie.

"Up the slope to the bitternut trees," said Buck, "and then along the creek to the shagbarks. I hear they've been hurt by the frost. I've looked after those trees a long while, and I'd like to see for myself. Benny's going to help me look at the bark, and Brody's going to tie a label on any that look poorly." He leaned on his stick. "It'll take us most of the day, I expect."

All day. Great-Grandpappy Buck, so old and so slow, toiling up that steep slope to look at trees that were perfectly well, because of words Reba had made up in a single moment so that she would not have to say, "I did it."

At the far end of the bridge, Barney was sitting with his feet dangling over the water. He was not splashing. He was just sitting.

Reba went and sat down beside him.

"You saw," she said at last. "In the nut shed. Didn't you?"

Barney nodded.

"The bitternuts. Your tail. The floaters in the corner basket." He kicked his feet. "I nearly went to tell the miller's wife last night. I got as far as her gate."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because it was yours to tell first," said Barney. "I wanted to give you the chance. But I was going to go this morning, if you didn't." He looked at her. "Grown-ups need to know when things go wrong. Even nuts."

Reba looked at the water, and then up at the slope, where a small grey figure was beginning, very slowly, to climb.

"I have to tell," she said. "Don't I."

"I'll come with you," said Barney.


Chapter Seven — Reba Tells

They caught up with Great-Grandpappy Buck at the bottom of the slope, where he had stopped to lean on his stick and get his breath. Charlie had come running after them, and Maggie Mae after Charlie, and by the time Reba arrived there were rather more creatures there than she would have liked.

She very nearly turned and ran.

But Barney was standing beside her, and he did not move. So she did not either.

"Grandpappy Buck," said Reba. "Please don't go up the slope. The trees aren't poorly."

Everybody looked at her.

"It wasn't the frost," said Reba. Her voice wobbled, so she said it fast, to get it all out. "It was me. I didn't test the nuts properly. I ate them instead. I didn't tie the ribbons. I didn't do the float tub. I knocked the bitternuts into the good ones with my tail and didn't notice, and they went in Maggie Mae's basket, and I put all the floaters in a basket in the corner and forgot, and Brody got it. And when everybody asked what happened, I said it was the frost, because I was too ashamed to say it was me. I made it up. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

It was very quiet at the bottom of the slope.

Charlie's mouth had fallen open. Maggie Mae's eyebrows had gone up. Brody looked at the basket of labels in his arms, and set it down gently on the grass.

Great-Grandpappy Buck looked at Reba without a word. Then he looked up at the long, steep slope, and back at Reba.

"Well now," he said. "That was a hard thing to say."

Reba nodded. She could not speak.

"I'll admit," said Buck, and the corner of his mouth lifted, "I'm very glad not to be climbing that hill."

And somehow that made it all right. The bitternut feeling inside Reba came loose, all at once, and she found she could breathe again.

"So the trees are well?" said Charlie in a small voice.

"Perfectly well," said Reba. "It was only me."

"Oh, thank goodness," said Charlie, and flung her arms round Reba's middle.

"But everybody's still got the wrong baskets," said Brody. "And everybody still thinks the trees are poorly."

Reba straightened her pink bow.

"Then I'll fix it," she said. "All of it. Properly."

"On your own?" said Maggie Mae.

Reba looked at Barney. Then at Brody, and Maggie Mae, and Charlie, and Benny, and the long path back to the nut shed.

"No," she said. "Not on my own. It took me one day to make the muddle. It'll take the miller's wife's whole week to sort it properly." She took a breath. "Unless everybody helps."


Chapter Eight — Everybody Helps

So that afternoon, the whole Hollow brought its baskets back to the nut shed. The miller's wife sat in her chair right beside the float tub, with a cushion behind her back, to watch over it. And this time, Reba did it properly, with everybody.

First, the trees.

"Maverick," said Reba. "Could you tell everybody they're well? Everybody. Quickly."

Maverick did not need asking twice. He flew to the very top of the tallest hickory by the creek and announced it in his most enormous voice, so loudly that it echoed off Weather Ridge.

"ATTENTION, HICKORY HOLLOW! THE TREES ARE PERFECTLY WELL! THE FROST DID NOTHING! IT WAS REBA! SHE'S VERY SORRY! COME TO THE NUT SHED! BRING YOUR BASKETS!"

"Maybe not the bit about me," Reba called up.

"TOO LATE!" called Maverick cheerfully. "BUT SHE'S VERY SORRY!"

Reba put both paws over her face. Then, from behind them, she began to giggle, because it was perfectly true, and because it was a strange sort of relief to have the whole valley know at once.

Then the sorting.

Barney had been waiting for this moment all his life.

"The Great Nut Boat Race!" he cried. Every nut went into the float tub, plop, plop, plop, launched by Charlie and the little rabbits, and the empties bobbed to the top and were scooped out with a little net, and Barney gave each one a name and a short farewell speech before it went in the empties bin. "Goodbye, brave Captain Nothing-Inside. You floated beautifully."

Charlie was the first to spot the holes. "Look!" she squeaked, holding up a floater. "There's a tiny hole! Every empty one's got a tiny hole!" After that, she checked every nut before it went in the tub, and caught a whole pile of empties without even getting them wet.

Sadie Belle spread clean cloths out in the afternoon sun on the grass outside, and laid all the good wet nuts out in rows to dry before they went back in the baskets. "So they don't go mouldy in the winter," she explained.

Then the bitternuts. That was the hard part, and it was Reba's.

She sat at the long table with a heap of sinkers and made herself go slowly. And going slowly, she began to see what she had missed. The shagbarks were bigger. Their shells were thick and hard, with four ridges, like a little box. The bitternuts were smaller, and rounder, and their shells were thin and smooth.

"Smaller and rounder," she said out loud. "That's how. Every time."

Benny, beside her, sat up very straight.

"Smaller," he said. "Then we don't have to pick them out one by one."

He found a thin plank and a tool from the mill, and, with the miller drilling while he measured, made a row of round holes in it, all exactly the same size: just big enough for a bitternut to drop through, and just too small for a shagbark. They propped it over a basket, and Beau and Brody tipped the nuts onto it and shook it gently from each end, and the little bitternuts went pattering down through the holes into the basket below, and the big shagbarks stayed on top. Then Reba went over every nut left on top of the board with her clever paws, feeling for the four hard ridges of a shagbark, and picked out the few big bitternuts that had been too large to fall through.

"A nut sieve!" said Barney. "Benny, you're a genius!"

"I measured it," said Benny, going pink.

And Reba tested one nut from every pile on the nut-stone. Just one. And did not eat a single nut that was not a test nut, though her tummy rumbled so loudly that Barney named it the Thunder of the Nut Shed.

Then she tied every ribbon. Blue for the beavers. Green for the rabbits. Yellow for Maggie Mae. Red for Brody. A whole rainbow, on every basket.

And Rusty, who knew every path and every door in the Hollow, ran to every family who had gone home and told them their right basket would be waiting at the shed by evening, going the quickest way, along the trails nobody else knew, and was back before anybody had missed him.


Chapter Nine — Right This Time

By evening, it was done.

Every nut had been floated or checked for holes. Every good nut had been dried. Every pile had been tested. The bitternuts sat in their own bin by the door, for the woods. The empties sat in another, under a sign Barney had painted that said BRAVE FLOATERS. And along the walls stood a row of full, fair baskets, one for every family, each with its ribbon.

The miller's wife walked slowly along the row with her stick, looking at every ribbon. She cracked a nut from the beavers' basket and looked at the sweet kernel. She peered into the empties bin and smiled at the sign. She looked for a long time at Benny's nut sieve.

Then she turned to Reba.

"A whole week's work," she said, "in one afternoon."

"Everybody helped," said Reba.

"So they did," said the miller's wife, and patted Benny's nut sieve as if it were an old friend.

Then Reba handed out the baskets again. And this time, at every one, she said the same thing.

"Here's your basket. It's the right one, I promise. And I'm sorry about the first one."

The rabbits got a basket big enough for nine, and the littlest rabbit took her hat off. The miller got a basket the right size for a miller, and could see where he was going. Brody got a basket full of good, sweet, heavy nuts, not one empty among them. He cracked one on the spot to check, and beamed.

And Maggie Mae got her big round basket back, full of the very best shagbarks in the shed, which Reba had chosen one by one.

"I'll bake again tonight," said Maggie Mae. "And we'll have the Nut Day supper tomorrow, two days late. Nobody minds a late supper. Though I'll need a taster." Her emerald eyes twinkled. "Somebody who knows a bitter nut from a sweet one now."

"Just a taste," promised Reba.

When the last basket had gone, Reba turned to Barney, who was sitting on the edge of the float tub, swinging his feet, wearing the BRAVE FLOATERS sign folded into a hat, because he had painted a second one for the bin.

"Thank you," she said, "for waiting for me to be brave. And for coming with me down the slope. And for going to tell, if I hadn't."

"That's all right," said Barney.

"And I'm sorry I said no thank you, the first time. When you wanted to help."

Barney grinned so widely that his hat slipped over one eye.

"Next year," said Reba, "you're Float Tub Officer. Officially."

"Officially?" breathed Barney. "Can I keep the hat?"

"You can keep the hat," said Reba.


Chapter Ten — Small Honesties

The Nut Day supper was held the next evening, two days late, in the big warm room of the Old Mill itself, because the miller's wife said her back would not manage the walk to anybody's porch, and because the miller said it was high time the mill had a party.

It was the best Nut Day supper anybody could remember. Maggie Mae's cake sat in the middle of the long table, golden and rich, and Reba had tasted it first, just a taste, and declared it perfect. The great millwheel turned slowly outside the window, rumbling deep and steady like somebody humming. The miller played his spoons. Great-Grandpappy Buck played his banjo. Barney wore his hat all evening and would not take it off, even to eat.

Late in the evening, when the cake was gone, the miller's wife leaned over to Great-Grandpappy Buck.

"That young raccoon," she said quietly, nodding at Reba, who was helping to clear the plates without eating a single crumb off any of them. "She'll do the sorting next year, I think. If she wants it."

Buck watched Reba for a while.

"I think she'll do it very well," he said. "She knows now why it's slow."

Then Buck set his banjo down, and the room went quiet, because the miller's wife had asked him for a few words, the way she did every Nut Day.

He did not make a speech. He only looked round the warm, crowded mill room, at the full baskets stacked by the door with their rainbow ribbons, at Reba with a stack of plates, at Barney in his hat.

"Small honesties keep the big troubles away," he said. "That's all I've got to say this year. Now, who'd like another tune?"

It was dark when the supper ended, and the families walked home together along the creek path with their lanterns, in twos and threes, calling goodnight.

Reba walked home with her mother.

They went slowly, because Reba had something to say, and she wanted to say it properly, all the way through.

"Mama," she said. "You know when I said I couldn't say yet?"

"I remember," said her mother.

"I can say now."

And as they walked home together along Silver Creek, under the bare hickories, in the swinging light of her mother's lantern, Reba told her the whole of it. The sniffing and the eating. The ribbons and the tub. The tail and the bitternuts. The frost that never was. Barney at the gate. The bottom of the slope. The nut sieve and the Brave Floaters and the hat.

Her mother listened to every word, and did not interrupt once.

When Reba had finished, they had reached their own front door. Her mother stopped, and set down the lantern, and gave her a long, warm hug on the doorstep.

"Thank you for telling me," she said. "All of it."

"I should have told you the first night," said Reba.

"You told me now," said her mother. "That's what counts." She opened the door. "Now. Bed. And Reba?"

"Yes, Mama?"

"Did you eat anything at that supper?"

Reba thought about it.

"Only my share," she said.

Her mother laughed, and the door closed, and the lamp went out in the window. And all along the creek, up on the slope and down by the water, the hickory trees stood bare and black and perfectly well, under the stars.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
