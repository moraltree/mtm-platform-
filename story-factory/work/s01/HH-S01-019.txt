Chapter One — The Most Beautiful Garden

In the middle of May, Brody decided to make a butterfly garden.

He had been thinking about it all spring. Ever since an April afternoon in Fern Meadow, when he had been lying on his back in the grass, as he liked to, and a butterfly had landed on his nose. A big yellow one, with black stripes like a tiger and long tails on its back wings. It had sat on the end of his nose for the count of five, slowly opening and closing its wings, and Brody had gone cross-eyed trying to look at it, and kept as still as a stone. Then it had flown away, over the meadow, and Brody had lain there a good while afterwards, feeling as if something wonderful had happened to him personally.

So he wanted more butterflies. He wanted lots of butterflies. He wanted a whole garden full of them, so that every day he could sit in the middle of it and have butterflies land on him.

He chose a sunny patch of ground at the edge of the meadow, behind his family's den, where the grass grew long and scrubby and there were brambles at one end and an old untidy bush at the other. His father said he could have it, as long as he looked after it properly.

"I will," said Brody. "I'll make it the most beautiful garden in the Hollow."

And he set to work.

Brody was the biggest youngster in Hickory Hollow, and the strongest, and once he had decided to do something, he did it with his whole heart and both of his enormous paws. He dug up the scrubby grass. He pulled out the brambles. He pulled out every single weed he could find, the dandelions and the clover and the little purple violets that grew in clumps along the bottom of the old bush. He thought about pulling out the old untidy bush, too, but it was very deeply rooted, and in the end his father trimmed it for him, very hard, with the big shears, until it was a neat round shape, like a pudding.

Then he raked the earth until it was smooth and brown and perfect.

Then he went to find flowers.

He wanted the most beautiful ones. The biggest, the brightest, the most colourful, the most frilly. He went to every garden in the Hollow and asked very politely, and folk were very generous, because everybody liked Brody. He came home with a wheelbarrow full: big round pom-pom flowers, pink and white, with so many petals they looked like balls of tissue paper. Tall frilly flowers in yellow and orange, double and triple, every petal crinkled at the edges. Huge, heavy, blowsy roses from a cottage by the Old Mill, so full of petals that they drooped on their stems. And a pot of glossy red flowers from the miller's wife, shaped like little trumpets, with no smell at all.

He planted them in neat rows. Tallest at the back, smallest at the front, with the colours all arranged in stripes, like a picture he had seen once.

Then he stood back, and looked at it.

It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful garden in the Hollow. It looked like a cake. It looked like a party. It looked like a picture.

Brody went and fetched a little stool from home, and set it down right in the middle of the garden, and sat on it, very still, with his big face turned up to the sky, and his paws in his lap.

And waited for the butterflies to come.


Chapter Two — Waiting

The butterflies did not come.

Brody sat on his stool all that first afternoon, in the middle of his beautiful garden. The sun shone. The flowers glowed. Bees hummed in the meadow, and birds sang in the woods, and Brody sat very still and waited.

Not one butterfly came.

"They don't know it's here yet," he said, when Barney came by on his way home. "They'll find it tomorrow."

The next day, Brody sat on his stool all morning. A small white butterfly came fluttering along the edge of the meadow, and Brody sat stiller than still, and it flew right past the end of his garden without even slowing down, and went off towards the brambles at the far side of the meadow, and disappeared.

"It didn't see," said Brody.

The day after that, he made a sign. He painted it on a piece of board, in big careful letters, and stuck it in the ground at the front of the garden, facing the meadow, so that any butterfly flying past could read it.

BUTTERFLYS WELCOM.

Which was butterflies with a Y where the I and E should go, and welcome without its E on the end.

Benny came by, and looked at the sign, and looked at Brody, and opened his mouth to say something about spelling, and then looked at Brody's hopeful face again, and closed it.

No butterflies came.

The day after that, Barney had an idea, which was to build a butterfly house. So he and Brody built one, out of sticks and bark, at the side of the garden. It was the size of a small hutch. It had a little door, and a little window, and a little chimney, and Barney painted flowers on it, and Charlie put a tiny cushion inside, in case a butterfly wanted to lie down.

No butterflies came to the house. A beetle went in, and looked round, and came out again. That was all.

The day after that, Barney had another idea, which was that the butterflies simply did not know the way, and needed showing. So he tied Maggie Mae's brightest scarves, which she had lent him on the strict understanding that they came back clean, to the ends of two long sticks, and ran up and down the edge of Fern Meadow waving them over his head, very slowly and gracefully, the way he imagined a butterfly would fly, calling, "This way! Over here! Lovely flowers!"

No butterflies followed him.

But a great many bees did, because Maggie Mae's scarves smelled faintly of her rose soap. Barney looked round and saw a small, interested cloud of bees following him across the meadow, and stopped waving, and started running instead, and ran three times round Brody's garden with the bees bumbling after the scarves, and then, as he went past the pom-poms, tripped over the butterfly house, and sat down in the middle of the frilly yellow doubles with a scarf over his head.

The bees, who were not at all cross, only curious, circled him once, decided he was not a flower after all, and went back to the clover.

"Well," said Barney, from under the scarf. "That's bees sorted, anyway."

On the sixth day, Brody sat on his stool in the middle of his beautiful garden, with his chin in his paws, and did not wait for anything at all. He just sat.

That was where Reba found him.

Reba had been passing on her way back from the Old Mill, with a small paper bag of something that smelled of cinnamon, and she stopped at the edge of the garden, and looked at it, and then at Brody.

"Oh, Brody," she said. "It's gorgeous. It looks like a cake."

"It looks like a cake," agreed Brody gloomily. "And nobody wants it."

"Who doesn't?"

"The butterflies," said Brody. "I made it for butterflies. The most beautiful flowers in the Hollow. A house. A sign. And they won't come. Not even one. They fly straight past." He looked at his big paws. "Maybe they just don't like me."

Reba sat down on the edge of the garden, and looked at the rows of frilly, blowsy, brilliant flowers, and thought.

Reba was the most curious creature in the Hollow. She could not see a puzzle without wanting to know the answer. She did not know very much about gardens. She did not know very much about butterflies, either.

But she knew how to find things out.

"Brody," she said. "Where do they go?"

"What?"

"The butterflies," said Reba. "When they fly past. You said they fly straight past your garden. So they must be going somewhere else." She stood up, and brushed the earth off her tail. "Where?"


Chapter Three — Watching

So Reba and Brody went to find out.

They followed the next butterfly that came past. It was a small one, pale blue, flittering low over the long grass. They followed it across the meadow, Brody walking very slowly and very carefully so as not to frighten it, and Reba creeping along beside him with her nose in the air. It went past the end of Brody's garden. It went past the butterfly house. It flittered across a patch of rough grass, and over a ditch, and came down at last on the far side of the meadow, at the bottom of Blackberry Bank, by the edge of the woods, where nobody had done any gardening at all.

It landed on a weed.

A small, scruffy, ordinary weed, with clusters of tiny white flowers, growing in a tangle among the brambles. The little blue butterfly sat on it, and put out a long, curly tongue, as thin as a thread, and dipped it into one of the tiny flowers, and then the next, and the next.

"It's eating," whispered Reba.

"It's eating a weed," whispered Brody, astonished.

They watched it until it flew. And then they looked round.

The bottom of Blackberry Bank, the bit by the woods that nobody had ever tidied, was alive.

There were bees on the blackberry blossom, big furry ones, rolling about in the white flowers as if they were in bed. There were tiny flies, and little beetles. There was a brown butterfly with orange spots on the clover, and two more of the little pale-blue ones on the weed with white flowers, and a big black one with blue on its back wings drifting along the edge of the trees. On a tall plain purple flower, the kind that grows by every path and that nobody ever bothers to pick, there were so many bees that the whole flower was humming.

Brody stood in the long grass and stared.

"There's more butterflies here," he said slowly, "in this messy bit, than in the whole of my garden."

"There's more butterflies here than anywhere," said Reba. And she put her nose right into a wild rose, to see what it smelled like to a bee. She sniffed so hard that she came out with her whole nose dusted bright yellow with pollen, and sneezed, and the sneeze blew a very surprised bumblebee clean off the next rose along. It buzzed round her head twice, crossly, and went back.

"Sorry," said Reba to the bee, with a yellow nose.

Then she took her paper bag of cinnamon things out of her pocket, tipped them carefully into her little green rucksack, flattened the bag out, and found a stub of pencil in her other pocket, where she always kept one in case of recipes. Then she began to write.

"What are you doing?" said Brody.

"Finding out," said Reba. "I'm going to watch. Properly. Which flowers they go to, and which they don't. Every day. And write it down." She looked up at him. "Want to help?"

Brody looked at his beautiful garden, far away across the meadow, glowing in the sun like a cake that nobody had eaten.

Then he sat down in the long grass beside Reba, very carefully, so as not to squash anything.

"Yes," he said.


Chapter Four — What the Bees Knew

They watched for three days.

They sat at the bottom of Blackberry Bank, and in Brody's garden, and in Maggie Mae's garden at Rose Arch, and in the miller's wife's window boxes, and along the edge of the creek path. Reba wrote everything down on her paper bag, and then on another paper bag, and then on the back of a recipe for blackberry pie, which she said was all right because she knew it off by heart anyway.

And slowly, they began to notice things.

The bees and the butterflies went to the plain flowers. The flat ones, and the open ones, and the little ones in clusters. Blackberry blossom, and clover, and the scruffy weed with the tiny white flowers, and the tall plain purple ones by the path, and the open pink wild roses in the hedge at the bottom of Fern Meadow, the simple kind with only five petals, that nobody had ever thought very much of.

They did not go to Brody's flowers.

Not to the pom-poms, or the frilly doubles, or the blowsy roses. Not even once.

"Why?" said Brody, frowning at Reba's paper bag. "They're the most beautiful ones. They've got the most petals."

"Maybe that's it," said Reba slowly.

She went and knelt down by one of Brody's big pink pom-pom flowers, and looked at it. Then she gently, very gently, parted the petals with her clever little paws, and looked inside. And then she went and did the same with one of the plain wild roses in the hedge.

She came back with her nose wrinkled, thinking.

"The wild rose," she said, "has five petals, and then right in the middle there's a big round yellow bit, all dusty, and the bees can just land on it and walk about and get at everything. Easy." She pointed at the pom-pom. "Yours has got so many petals that there isn't a middle. Or if there is, it's buried, right at the bottom, under all the frills. A bee would have to dig." She thought about it. "It's like a pie," she said. "A really beautiful pie, with the most beautiful crust, all decorated. But when you cut it open, there's no filling."

Brody looked at his pom-pom flower.

"No filling," he said.

"And smell," said Reba. "They like a smell. Remember Barney and Maggie Mae's scarves? The bees chased him all round the meadow, and it wasn't the colour, it was her rose soap. So they go by smell. Partly." She frowned. "But your red trumpets haven't got any smell at all, and the bees never go near them. Only one thing ever came to them, all three days. A tiny green bird, no bigger than my paw, that hung in the air in front of them, buzzing like a bee, and put its long beak right inside."

"A hummingbird," said Brody.

"So somebody wants the red ones," said Reba. "Just not the bees."

Brody sat down heavily on the grass, and looked across at his garden.

"Pies with no filling," he said.

"Well," she said, "now we know. So we can do it again." She looked at her paper bag. "With filling."


Chapter Five — The Violets

On the tenth morning, they went to see Maggie Mae, because Maggie Mae was the best gardener in the Hollow, and because she would know which plain flowers grew well, and where to get some.

Maggie Mae was very interested in Reba's paper bags. She read every one of them, all the way through, including the blackberry pie recipe, and then she sat back in her garden chair and looked at Brody.

"Reba's right," she said. "The bees and the butterflies need flowers they can get into. Open ones, plain ones, ones with plenty of nectar and pollen. Most of the wild flowers in the Hollow are like that. They grew up together, the flowers and the butterflies, all those years, and they suit each other." She tapped the paper bag. "But there's something else you need. Something nobody ever thinks of. Where did you pull your weeds out, Brody?"

"Everywhere," said Brody. "I pulled out everything."

"What sort of weeds?"

Brody thought. "Dandelions. Clover. Some grass. And those little purple flowers, along the bottom of the old bush. Violets. Lots of those. I did the last of them only yesterday, round the edges."

Maggie Mae looked at him with an odd expression.

"Show me," she said.

So they went back to Brody's garden, and Brody showed her the heap of pulled weeds from the day before, wilting at the edge of the meadow. Maggie Mae knelt down beside it, and very carefully began to look through the heap, turning over the limp violet leaves one by one.

On the fourth leaf she turned over, she stopped.

"Look," she said softly.

Clinging to the underside of the wilted violet leaf, there was a caterpillar. A dark, spiky caterpillar, nearly as long as Reba's finger, with little orange-and-black spines all over it. It was not moving very much. It looked hungry.

"A caterpillar," said Reba. "On a violet."

"A fritillary caterpillar," said Maggie Mae. "Do you remember the big orange-and-brown butterflies with the spots, that you see all over the meadow at the start of summer? That's what this will be, in a few weeks. And it can only eat one thing. Violets. Nothing else in the whole world. Its mother lays her eggs near the violets, so that when the babies hatch, there's food right there." She looked at the heap of pulled-up violets, wilting in the sun. "There's probably a lot more of them in this heap."

Brody looked at the caterpillar. Then at the heap. Then at his neat, smooth, weeded garden, with not a single violet left in it.

"I pulled up their dinner," he said, in a very small voice for such a big bear. "I pulled up all their dinner and threw it away."

Maggie Mae did not say anything. She only put her paw on his arm.

He knelt down beside the heap, very slowly, and looked at the hungry little spiky caterpillar on its dying leaf.

"Can we put them back?" he said.

Maggie Mae looked at the heap, and at the wilted violet roots, and at the bare brown earth along the bottom of the old trimmed bush.

"We can try," she said.


Chapter Six — Putting Things Back

They tried.

All that evening, until the light went, Brody and Reba and Maggie Mae and Charlie, who came running when she heard, knelt in the garden and sorted through the heap of pulled-up weeds. Every violet that still had a good root, they set aside. Every caterpillar they found, they lifted very gently on its leaf, and set aside too, in a shady box that Maggie Mae found, lined with fresh violet leaves from her own garden, which had plenty.

Then they planted the violets back, and Maggie Mae brought spare clumps from her own garden to fill the gaps where the oldest ones, pulled on the very first day, were too far gone. All along the bottom of the old bush, where they had been before, and round the edges of the garden, and in little clumps between the frilly flowers. Brody dug each hole with one careful claw, and set each violet in, and patted the earth round it, and Charlie watered them, at the roots, as Maggie Mae showed her.

Some of the violets were too far gone. Brody put those on the compost heap himself, very sorrowfully.

But most of them, by the next morning, had perked up. And the caterpillars, set back down on them leaf and all, as Maggie Mae showed them, without anybody's paws touching them, began to eat.

Brody watched them eat.

"How many did we find?" he said.

"Eleven," said Reba, consulting her paper bag. "Eleven caterpillars."

"Eleven," said Brody. "That's eleven butterflies. That would have been none." He looked down at the little spiky caterpillars, munching on their violets, and then he looked at the rest of his garden. The pom-poms. The frilly doubles. The blowsy roses. The red trumpets, which only a hummingbird wanted.

"What about the old bush?" said Reba. "The one you made into a pudding?"

They went and looked at it.

It was not a very interesting bush, now that it had been trimmed so hard. Just a round green lump. But Maggie Mae, peering at it, said, "Oh," and scratched off a tiny bit of bark from one of the twigs with her claw, and held it out for them to smell.

It smelled spicy. Warm and lemony and peppery, like something from Reba's mother's spice drawer.

"Spicebush," said Maggie Mae. "There's a butterfly that lays its eggs on spicebush, or sassafras, and nothing much else. A big black one, with blue on its back wings."

"I saw one!" said Reba. "At the bottom of Blackberry Bank! Drifting along the edge of the trees!"

"That's the one," said Maggie Mae. "If you let this grow out again, and don't trim it, she might come."

Brody looked at the old pudding-shaped bush, which he had very nearly pulled out on the very first day.

"I nearly pulled it out," he said.

"But you didn't," said Reba.

"Only because it was too stuck," said Brody honestly.

And despite everything, they all laughed.


Chapter Seven — The Plain Garden

Over the next week, Brody rebuilt his butterfly garden.

He did not pull anything out this time. Not straight away. First he watched, with Reba, and wrote down what came. Then, slowly, he began to change things.

He gave the pom-poms and the frilly doubles and the blowsy roses back to the folk who had given them to him, with a polite thank you, and explained why, and most of them were very interested and asked to see Reba's paper bags. The red trumpets he kept, for the hummingbird, in a pot by the garden gate.

In their place, he planted plain flowers. Wild ones, and simple ones, the sort with an open face and a middle you could get at. Maggie Mae divided pieces off her own big clumps of bee balm and coneflowers, with plenty of root on every piece, so that they would flower that very summer. The miller's wife gave him cuttings. And Sadie Belle, who knew every path in the Hollow, showed him where the wild flowers had seeded themselves so thickly into the middle of the Mill track that the miller was going to have to dig them up anyway, so Brody rescued them, and carried them home in a basket, and planted them where nobody would tread on them. Some of them were already flowering. Most of them were not. The little milkweed, Maggie Mae said, might take till next year to flower properly.

Brody was not good at waiting for things to grow, as it turned out. That week he went out to the little milkweed every morning with Benny's ruler, and wrote down how much it had grown, and every morning it was the same as the morning before, give or take a whisker. On the third morning he got down on his tummy, and put out a claw to dig, just a little, just to see if the roots were doing anything.

Then he stopped, with his claw in the air.

He was thinking about a hickory nut, a long time ago, and a whole spring of nothing happening, and how much he had wanted to dig that up too.

He took his claw away, and patted the earth flat again, and went in for breakfast.

Brody looked at the little green plants, all leaves and no flowers. They did not look very beautiful. They did not look like a party, or a picture. They looked like weeds.

But he did not mind as much as he would have, a week ago.

He let the clover grow back at the edges. He let the violets spread. He left a corner at the far end, by the brambles, completely untidy, and wrote MESSY ON PURPOSE on a little board and stuck it there, so nobody would tidy it by mistake. He let the old spicebush grow, and did not trim it, and every morning he went and looked at its leaves.

The butterfly house, he left where it was, because Charlie had put the cushion in it, and it seemed unkind to take it down. A family of beetles moved in, and seemed very happy.

And the sign at the front of the garden, Benny quietly took away one evening and brought back the next morning, repainted, with all the letters in the right places.

BUTTERFLIES WELCOME.

And underneath, in smaller letters, which Brody had asked for specially:

AND BEES. AND CATERPILLARS. AND BEETLES. AND ANYBODY ELSE.


Chapter Eight — The First Visitor

The first visitor came on a warm, still morning in the first week of June.

It was the middle of the morning, and the sun was full and hot on the garden, and Brody had come out with Reba to look at the fritillary caterpillars. They were very big now, and very fat, and some of them had stopped eating altogether, and were hanging upside down from the undersides of the violet leaves in a curious way, as if they were thinking about something.

Brody was crouched beside them, watching, when something drifted over his shoulder.

It was big. It was black. It had a cloud of soft blue dusted across its back wings, and a row of pale spots along the edges, and it flew slowly, lazily, low over the garden, as if it were looking for something.

It went past the butterfly house. It went past the sign. It went past the clover and the violets and the little green milkweed.

And it landed on the spicebush.

Brody did not move. Reba did not move. They crouched among the violets without moving a whisker, and watched, as the big black butterfly walked slowly along a spicebush twig, touching each leaf with its feet as if it were tasting them. Then it curled the end of its body round, under one of the leaves, and stayed there a moment. Then it fluttered to another twig, quite a long way off, and did the same thing again, once. And then it opened its wings, and flew away, over the meadow, towards the woods.

Brody got up, very slowly, and went to the spicebush, and found the leaf the butterfly had curled itself under, and turned it over, with one gentle claw.

On the underside of the leaf, there was a tiny round egg. Pale and greenish, no bigger than a grain of sand. Just one.

"One here," whispered Reba, at the other twig. "Just one. She doesn't put them all together."

"So they've each got a whole branch to themselves," said Brody slowly.

He sat down in the grass, all at once, with a soft thump, and did not get up for quite a while.

Then they both ran. Not very fast, because Brody never ran very fast, but as fast as they could. All the way across Fern Meadow to Rose Arch Cottage, where they knocked on the door so hard that Maggie Mae came out with her apron on inside out, thinking something must be wrong.

"She came," said Brody, out of breath. "The spicebush one. She came, and she laid eggs. On my bush. That I nearly pulled out."

Maggie Mae looked at him. Then, without even stopping to put her apron the right way round, she ran back with them across the meadow, to look.


Chapter Nine — Buck's Visit

Great-Grandpappy Buck came to see the garden that afternoon, because everybody in the Hollow had heard about the eggs by lunchtime, and he said he wanted to see a spicebush egg for himself before his eyes got any older.

He came slowly, along the edge of the meadow, with his walking-stick, and stopped at the front of the garden, and read the sign. Both parts of it. His whiskers twitched.

Then he looked at the garden.

It was not beautiful, as it had been on the first day. Not like a cake, or a party, or a picture. It was mostly green. There were violets along the bottom of the old spicebush, and clover round the edges, and rows of leafy plants with their first tight buds just showing, and a messy corner at the far end with a board in it. There was a beetle house with a cushion in it. There was a pot of red trumpets by the gate. And on a few of the plain early flowers that were already open, there were bees, busy and buzzing, and one small pale-blue butterfly, sitting on a clover head with its tongue out, drinking.

Brody showed him the spicebush eggs, and Buck bent down, with a creak of old knees, and peered at them through his spectacles, and said, "Well, I never." Then Brody showed him the fritillary caterpillars, hanging upside down from the violets, and Reba showed him the paper bags, all of them, including the one with the blackberry pie recipe on the back.

Buck read every one, right down to the hummingbird. "A hummingbird," he said. "I haven't seen one close up in years. I'd dearly like to, once more." Then he looked at Brody.

"How did you find all this out?" he said.

Brody looked at Reba.

"We watched," he said. "Reba asked where the butterflies went, and we followed them. And we watched what they did. Every day. For days. And Reba wrote it all down on paper bags."

Buck nodded slowly.

He looked out across the garden, and the meadow beyond it, and Blackberry Bank by the woods where the bees were humming on the last of the blossom, and the long green line of the Hickory Woods beyond that, with the afternoon sun on the leaves and a wood thrush starting to sing somewhere deep inside.

Then he said, in his slow, warm voice, "Listen to the woods and they'll tell you everything worth knowing."

He stayed a while longer, sitting on Brody's little stool in the middle of the garden, with the bees going about their business round his ears. And just before he left, the small pale-blue butterfly lifted off its clover head, and flittered across, and landed for a moment on the peak of his cap. Then it flew away again.

Buck did not move until it had gone. Then he chuckled to himself, and stood up, and went home.


Chapter Ten — The Red Trumpets

That evening, Brody carried the pot of red trumpets across the meadow and over Grandpappy's Bridge to Great-Grandpappy Buck's porch.

He had thought about it all afternoon. The red trumpets had no smell, and no bee had ever looked at them. But they had filling, deep inside, at the bottom of each long red tube, for somebody with a beak long enough to reach it. And Buck sat on his porch every evening, in his rocking chair, and Buck had said, that very afternoon, that he would dearly like to see a hummingbird close up once more.

So Brody set the pot down on the porch rail, right beside the rocking chair.

"For the hummingbird," he said. "So it can come to you."

Buck looked at the red trumpets, and then at Brody, and his whiskers twitched.

The others came, as they always did on a fine evening, and sat about on the porch boards and the steps. Reba came with a bag of cinnamon buns, and shared them. Maggie Mae came, and Charlie. Benny came, and asked how tall the milkweed was now, and Brody told him, to the nearest whisker. Barney came with Maggie Mae's scarves, washed and folded, which he had been meaning to give back for a week.

Nobody waited for anything. They just sat, and ate cinnamon buns, and talked, while the evening came on warm and soft, and the swifts went chittering high over the creek, and the light went long and low across the porch boards.

And then, while Barney was in the middle of a story about bees, something buzzed past his ear.

It was tiny. It was green, the shining green of a new leaf, and it had a throat that flashed red when it turned. It hung in the air in front of the red trumpets on the porch rail, so close to Buck's rocking chair that he could have reached out and touched it, with its wings going so fast that they were only a blur and a hum. And it put its long, thin beak right down inside the first red trumpet, and then the next, and then the next.

Nobody on the porch moved. Charlie had a cinnamon bun halfway to her mouth, and it stayed there.

The hummingbird visited every trumpet in the pot. Then it hung in the air for a moment, right in front of Buck's spectacles, as if it were looking at him. And then it was gone, so fast that nobody saw which way it went.

Buck sat back in his rocking chair.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, now."

Barney finished his story about bees. Charlie finished her cinnamon bun. And when it began to get dark, the grown-ups came along the creek path with their lanterns, Reba's father and Brody's father and Maggie Mae's neighbours and the brothers' mother, and the porch emptied, a few at a time, with goodnights all round, until only Buck was left.

Great-Grandpappy Buck was still sitting in his rocking chair in the blue dusk, with his paws folded, not rocking, watching the pot of red trumpets on the rail beside him, just in case.

Goodnight, Hickory Hollow.
