## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.1 / 10 — NOT YET PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This is one of the most tender and best-written drafts of the season, at the level of individual lines. "Quiet… That's the worst thing I'm good at" (l.119) is a real laugh. "It was the most enormous small sound she had ever heard" (l.170) and "a little striped stone" (l.231) are the kind of lines parents remember. Brody's "The gaps are the best bit. That's where the tune breathes" (l.358) shows the theme instead of saying it. The best idea in the story is Buck's admission that he had forgotten the crackle (l.420), where the child gives the elder something back. The flag line about chipmunk winter sleep (l.3–9) is light, accurate and funny, which is exactly what the slate asked for.

Six problems hold the score down:
- **The theme is spelled out three times before Buck speaks** (house rule 1). The last time is Charlie herself, one line before the saying, so the Mountain Wisdom arrives as a repeat.
- **It retreads the season.** This is the third story in which Sadie Belle's slowness is the answer (006, 012). Keeping still so that birds come has already been done with Rusty in 004 and with a chickadee on Reba in 011. Snowflakes on a dark surface was 009's whole payoff. In 012, Beau's flaw was hurrying and he asked Sadie Belle to teach him to be slow; here he is suddenly already a master of stillness.
- **The close is assembled from series stock** (house rule 5): the lantern procession of families come to fetch their youngsters, close to word for word from 005; a carried walk home; stars coming out twice; "Charlie did not say anything"; and a single tiny sound as the last image.
- **A predator-and-prey line** (l.252) and a cat crouched watching birds (l.221–231) break §15.
- **"Too much Charlie" is resolved by Charlie becoming less Charlie.** Her mother's "what have you done with my Charlie?" and "She did not chatter… the way she usually did" frame her natural energy as the fault. That cuts against Canon 4 ("difference is always a distinction, never a deficit").
- **The ruler payoff, Charlie's one piece of problem-solving, does not hold together physically** (see weakness 6).

### Strengths

- **The Ch1 opening and flag line (l.3–15).** "Chipmunks are not very good at winter. That is not a rude thing to say" sets the voice at once, makes the torpor biology accurate (waking every few days to eat from the store) and turns Charlie's wakefulness into character rather than plot convenience. The half-asleep "Mmm" from her mother (l.39) is funny, though see Lock Three S1.
- **The Ch2–3 comedy escalates on stage** (house rule 4): fidgeting, chase, the lost ruler, the hide-and-seek nobody joined, the soup, Maverick dozing off, the faster tune waking Beau, and finally the milk spill. Each mishap is concrete and slightly bigger than the last, and nobody is cruel.
- **The guilt section is short and true (l.105–123).** "I'm too much Charlie for a snowed-in day" is a genuine childhood feeling, said in a child's words. Everybody's response is kind and specific (the rug "had seen worse").
- **Sadie Belle's method is a child's method, not an adult's.** "Long legs are very bossy… Just let them want to run, and don't let them" (l.150) is excellent: practical self-regulation without a lecture. It suits her v1.1 gentle, encouraging canon.
- **The turning point in Ch5 lands.** The chickadee on the paw is earned by the counting and the aching arm, and "A bird stood on me" (l.213), repeated to her mother in Ch9, is a good refrain.
- **Ensemble moments that fit character.** Reba passes seeds through the door without a word, a nice reversal for the food-lover (l.199). Rusty is the scout who finds the snow fleas and generously gives the credit away (l.309). Maggie Mae and Charlie point at the same frost fern from either side of the glass (l.270), a lovely staging of the mentor bond. Benny's "I wouldn't go that far" (l.346) is a good comic button.
- **The Ch8 ruler idea, a still object doing the job better than the busy measurer,** is a clever, child-found echo of the theme, in principle (see weakness 6).
- **Buck's close (l.408–424).** Staging it at the top of the buried steps, with Buck standing rather than rocking, is a genuine variation on the porch close. "Hear what, little one?" lets the child lead. The wisdom fits the brief's appreciation half closely.
- **The last line (l.444),** "for the first time all winter, she fell asleep straight away", is a perfect bedtime cue that closes the loop opened in Ch1.

(l.383) and "She did not chatter all the way home, the way she usually did" (l.432) both treat the quiet Charlie as the improved one. The brief is patience and appreciation, not "be less yourself", and Canon 4 says difference is never a deficit. One beat must value her energy: her quick eye spotting the ruler, a happy bound into the trodden snow at the right moment, or Maggie Mae answering "too much" directly with something like "exactly enough Charlie". The answer should show that stillness is a new thing she can do, not a replacement for who she is.

### "Sherlock", natural history and physics

- **S-1. Eastern chipmunk winter torpor (l.5): ACCURATE.** *Tamias striatus* hibernates in burrows from late autumn. It enters torpor bouts of several days and rouses periodically to feed from its underground cache, rather than living on fat. It occasionally surfaces on mild winter days. "Every few days they wake up, have a little something to eat from the store" is a fair child-level summary. Real adult chipmunks usually overwinter alone, one to a burrow; the family heap is acceptable in-world licence. Charlie being up and restless is a light, honest flag answer.
- **S-2. Chickadees in the southern Appalachians (l.189–209): ACCURATE, keep the name generic.** Carolina chickadees occupy the lower and middle elevations of the southern Appalachians. Black-capped chickadees occur only at high elevations (roughly above 1,200–1,500 m in the Smokies), with hybrids in between. The description (grey, black cap and bib, white cheeks) and the "chick-a-dee-dee-dee" call fit both species. Calling them simply "chickadees" is correct, and nothing needs changing for a valley setting. Both species readily take sunflower seeds from a still, outstretched hand where feeding habituates them. They take one seed at a time, fly to a perch, hold it under the feet and hammer it open, so l.195 and l.209 are accurate.
  - **Minor:** "It weighed less than a leaf" (l.209) is not true. A chickadee weighs about 10–12 g; a dry leaf weighs a gram or two. Use "It weighed almost nothing".
  - **Scale note for illustration:** a chickadee is about chipmunk length ("no bigger than Charlie herself", l.189), so it perching on a chipmunk's paw needs careful art direction. The text can stand.
- **S-3. Northern cardinal (l.242–254): ACCURATE.** Resident year-round, the male bright red, often sheltering in holly and dense evergreens in snow. This matches Bible v1.0 l.597 ("the cardinal, a flash of scarlet in the winter snow"). "red against the white holly" means snow-covered holly, which is fine.
- **S-4. Snow muffling sound (l.162–170, l.406, l.416): BROADLY ACCURATE.** Fresh, porous snow absorbs sound, so a snowy landscape is unusually quiet. Falling snow is very nearly silent; the text hedges well ("not quite a sound… the edge of a sound"), which keeps it honest. Hearing a creek running under snow on a still, cold evening, and trees crackling or creaking in hard cold or under new snow load, are both plausible.
- **S-5. Frost ferns (l.264–268): ACCURATE, with one wording note.** Fern frost forms on the inside face of cold single-pane glass, where warm, moist indoor air (here, a fire and steaming soup) meets glass chilled by the outside air. It begins as a mist of condensation and then grows dendritically, often visibly over minutes. "Cold enough outside, and warm enough inside" is right. Because the ferns are on the inside face, Charlie sees them through the glass and Maggie Mae is on the same side as them, which still works as staged. Sadie Belle's "they're just ice. Growing" is fine.
- **S-6. Springtails, "snow fleas" (l.289–297): MOSTLY ACCURATE, three fixes required.**
  - Accurate: springtails (Collembola, e.g. *Hypogastrura nivicola*) are about 1–2 mm long, dark blue-grey, and look like pepper on snow. They live in leaf litter and migrate up to the snow surface. They jump by releasing the furcula, a forked spring folded under the abdomen and held by a catch (the tenaculum). "They've got a little spring underneath… and pop" is lovely and correct, and "not really fleas" is correct.
  - **Fix 1, conditions.** They surface on mild winter days, typically near or above freezing and often sunny, frequently in late winter. This draft has the afternoon "grew colder" enough for frost ferns (l.264) and the evening cold enough to make noses tingle (l.406). Make the late-afternoon break mild ("the air had softened", gold light) before the clear, colder dusk.
  - **Fix 2, place.** They come up through the snowpack from leaf litter, so they cluster around tree trunks, stems and edges. Brand-new snow lying on a wooden porch step is the least likely place, and freshly fallen deep snow usually takes time to be colonised. Move them to the snow around the base of the hickory or a porch post, with Rusty pointing from the porch edge.
  - **Fix 3, "the tiniest little creatures there are"** (l.297) is false (mites and many others are smaller). Use "some of the tiniest creatures in the whole Hollow".
- **S-7. Icicle bubbles (l.260): ACCURATE.** Icicles commonly trap air bubbles and have cloudy cores, especially where they freeze fast. Icicles need meltwater, from roof warmth or sun, refreezing. They can reasonably date from 012's snows, so no change is needed.
- **S-8. Willow snowshoes (l.369): PLAUSIBLE, and a good choice.** Traditional snowshoes are bent wood frames, usually ash, but willow and other pliable woods were also used, especially for improvised shoes. Lacing was usually rawhide, so "woven willow" neatly avoids animal products in a no-predation world. Keep it.
- **S-9. Chickadee calling after dark (l.438–440): IMPLAUSIBLE.** Chickadees roost in cavities or dense cover and are essentially silent once settled at night. A sleepy "chick-a-dee-dee" from the dark beech is poetic licence that a nature-literate parent will notice. If the close keeps a final sound (Lock One revision 3 suggests replacing it), use a soft rustle of a bird shifting in its roost, or move the moment earlier, to the last of the light.
- **S-10. Minor checks: no issues.** A moth on a window indoors in winter (overwintering moths do this), rabbit tracks in snow, a squirrel sheltering in its drey in a storm, and breath clouds in cold air are all accurate.

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding, bedtime suitability and narration readiness

(Role-based AI assessment, not clinical or professional safeguarding advice.)

**The stove incident (l.97).** Charlie stirs a pot on the stove hard enough that soup "slopped… onto the stove with a hiss". Buck then lifts the pot off and says stoves are for grown-ups. The rule is correct and firmly kind. But the staging puts two children (Reba "helping Buck in the kitchen with the soup", l.74, and then Charlie) at a hot pot on the stove while Buck has to "come" from elsewhere. In other words, the hazard happened before the adult arrived, which is exactly what house rule 7 and founder default 5 are meant to prevent.
- **S1 (required).** Keep Buck at the stove throughout. Reba "helps" by tasting from a spoon he hands her. Charlie's over-enthusiasm jogs his elbow, or slops a cooled bowl at the table, so the mishap stays comic and no child is ever at the heat.

**Children snowed in (Ch2).** This is handled well. Buck is present all day, he alone puts logs on the fire (l.58), there is food and firewood, and the families come to collect them. "Your families know you're here" (l.56) is reassuring. Two points need tightening:
