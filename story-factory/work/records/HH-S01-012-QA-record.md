## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.0 / 10 — NOT YET PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This is the funniest draft of the winter run, and its comic engine is sound. The four events give a clean, escalating structure. Each failure is staged on the page and has its own shape (too hard, too soft, sideways; too far; backwards; in a circle). The comedy is Beau's own overreach, never cruelty from others. The turn in Ch7, where the boaster's failures become exactly the qualification a frightened little one needs, is a genuinely good idea. Charlie's "You threw one at the miller's hat" and "That was terrible" (l.394–396) is a lovely piece of repair.

Five problems hold the score down:
- **The moral is spelled out three or four times before Buck speaks** (house rule 1), and the chosen saying fits resilience better than the brief's humility and sportsmanship.
- **The turning point and ending lean on recycled series material.** The littlest rabbit sledging "eleven times" on a tea tray, squealing, is lifted from 009 l.403, which also contradicts "frightened of that hill all winter" (l.486). The "pretend we meant to" gag is Maverick's (004, 009), and here it is used three times, by Beau. Maverick's own fall into a drift repeats 009.
- **The close reuses elements on the banned or near-banned list:** stars coming out (twice), objects glowing in the dark as the final image, "neither of them said anything", the lantern bobbing away along the creek path, and the little one turning at the foot of the steps. The final image is also a Beau and Sadie Belle two-shot under the stars, which pushes against the crush canon.
- **Rusty, a named principal, vanishes from the story at its turning point.** He leaves in Ch6 and has no part in Ch7–8. A non-cast minor character (the littlest rabbit) supplies the catalyst, the judging, the honour and the closing gesture.
- **The sportsmanship half of the brief is mostly summarised, not staged.** "Beau cheered loudest of all" (l.427) and the Barney and Benny apologies (l.398) are reported in a clause. Several scoring and logic slips also undercut the competition (see weaknesses 7–8).

### Strengths

- **Ch1 premise and voice.** Beau's "I am the champion of winter" / "Of what?" / "Of everything" (l.9–15) is a crisp comic opening, and "Beau looked at him with great pity" (l.27) is very funny. The snowdrift slide "as if it had all been planned" sets up the pattern economically (though see weakness 3).
- **Buck's skating boundary (l.62–70)** is model safeguarding: "It doesn't matter what ice looks like. It only matters what it is." It meets the slate flag fully, and it is firm and loving without lecturing.
- **The adults choose a safe slope (l.58–60):** no trees, no rocks, no creek, a flat run-out, and one-at-a-time with a clear finish rule. This is good practice built into the story world without fuss.
- **Ch2 practice montage (l.82–88)** is staged comedy, and "Testing the bush" and "Warming up" are good character lines.
- **Ch3–5 failures escalate well and are on stage** (house rule 4). The plop over the hedge (l.127), Benny's "your feet are still in the air" (l.188), backwards with "an expression of great surprise" (l.225), following his own trail (l.241).
- **The winners each win in character.** Charlie by cleverness rather than strength, Benny by fair rules, Barney by clowning, Sadie Belle by patience. This is good Canon 4 material: size and slowness become distinctions, not deficits.
- **Ch6 Rusty scene.** He sits on the log and "did not say anything for a while", and "Go away" / "No" (l.272–274) is simple and true. The shortcut anecdote (l.286) uses his v1.0 trait well. "But that's not the bad bit" (l.294) is the right moral priority: the jokes didn't matter, the words did.
- **Ch7 turning point.** "I fell off three times today… That's not falling off, exactly. But it's close" (l.335–341). The boaster finds that his failures, not his talents, are what he has to give. This is the strongest idea in the draft.
- **Ch8 repair.** The apology to Charlie is specific ("you threw them up high, like rainbows, and that was clever, and I never thought of it"), and she answers with a joke rather than forgiveness speech. "I'm sorry I said I didn't need luck. I needed loads" (l.400) is charming.
- **Ch9.** "Nobody won more than one event. So nobody's the Champion" (l.433) is honest and pleasingly anticlimactic. "Sometimes," said Rusty (l.461) is the right note of friendly teasing.
- **The ice and pit rules, Benny's measuring and the timed run** give the world a real sense of fair play.

### Weaknesses

1. **The moral is stated before the saying (house rule 1).**
   - Rusty, l.290: "Being laughed at only hurts while you're the only one not laughing." This is a polished aphorism that does the saying's job in Ch6.
   - Beau, l.455: "Best Cheerer… That's better than Champion." Rusty, l.457: "Champions have to be good at things. Cheerers just have to be nice." Together these state the theme in the closing chapter's penultimate scene.
   - Buck's lead-in, l.476: "Learned more from that snowbank than from all the races I ever won." This paraphrases the saying immediately before it.
2. **The saying fits only half the brief.** #50 is about learning from failure (resilience). The brief's moral is humility and sportsmanship. The fit with the literal falling is neat, but it rewards the falls rather than the change in Beau. #2 ("Help a neighbour up the hill, and you'll find you climbed it too.") fits the Ch7 turn almost literally and lands the "friendship more rewarding than winning" half; #70 ("You can't learn a thing while your own voice is going.") fits the boast. Either needs founder agreement; #50 can stay if the founder prefers, but then cut the lead-in.
3. **Recycled series beats.**
   - The littlest rabbit "went down… eleven times… squealing" on a tea tray (l.376) repeats 009 l.403 almost word for word ("eleven times, squealing all the way"). In 009 it was "the best thing that had ever happened to her", which jars with "She's been frightened of that hill all winter" (l.486) and "I'm scared I'll fall off" (l.329). The 009 slope was tiny, so the contradiction can be reconciled, but the text has to do it.
   - The littlest rabbit as the small one who unlocks a bigger friend's lesson, and is then honoured, echoes 005 ("She was the hero today, I hear"). This is her fifth appearance in twelve stories and her biggest role yet, in a story that already has Charlie as the cast's smallest member.

### "Sherlock", facts and plausibility

- **F1. Hedgehogs are not native to North America (l.357, "like a hedgehog").** Hickory Hollow is Appalachian, and the Hollow's creatures are native species. Use a native image: a "roly-poly bug" (pill bug, the Appalachian children's name), or "like a sleeping fox".
- **F2. Snow that packs** (see the 009 contradiction above). Fresh, very cold, sparkling powder makes poor snowballs; the snow needs to be near freezing to pack.
- **F3. Charlie's throws.** Lobbing acorn-sized snowballs thirty of Benny's paces is a stretch: tiny, light snowballs lose speed to air resistance quickly, and they tend to crumble. A high arc is a good accuracy trick but not a range trick. Either shorten the line for everybody, give the smallest entrants a nearer mark set by Benny (which suits his fairness), or make her snowballs a little bigger.
- **F4. Bobcats and snow.** Beau's "Bobcats are made for snow" is factually wrong: bobcats have relatively small feet and struggle in deep snow compared with their cousin the lynx. Because it is Beau's boast, not the narrator's claim, this is not an error and could even be a quiet joke. It should not be endorsed by the narrator.
- **F5. Beavers sliding.** "Beavers were built for sliding, and it turned out he was right" (l.215) has the narrator endorse it. Otters are the famous sliders; beavers are not. Keep it as Barney's claim and make the result luck or technique (his flat tail as a rudder, for example).
- **F6. Sledging plausibility.** A sledge spinning on a bump is plausible (more so for a tray than a runner sledge). A runner sledge bogs down in deep fresh powder but runs fast on a slope packed by a morning of use, which fits the afternoon. The flat run-out slowing everyone naturally (l.58, l.225) is correct.
- **F7. Holly.** American holly is native to southern Appalachia, so it is accurate, but it is prickly, which breaks the "comic falls soft" flag (Lock Three S5).
- **F8. Safe-falling technique.** "Tuck your chin in and curl up round… and roll, so that you land soft" (l.357) matches general fall-safety guidance: protect the head, keep the arms in (outstretched arms cause wrist injuries), and roll to spread the impact. Standard sledging guidance for children says: sit up facing forward, feet first; never go head-first or backwards; if you cannot stop safely, roll off to the side; use a slope with a clear run-out; have an adult supervising; helmets are recommended for young children. The story's teaching on the flat first is sound. The deliberate falling-off game and the deliberate backwards run in Ch8 contradict that guidance (Lock Three S3).
- **F9. Natural history otherwise checks.** Chickadees overwinter and appear in v1.0's bird list. A deer carrying a mitten in her mouth is fine. Leaping distances of four and a half to five paces are modest for a young deer and fox. Sunset behind the west ridge is correct.

### Result: PASS WITH NOTES (conditional on C1–C5, F1, F2 and F5, the continuity fixes in revision 4, the crush placement in revision 9, and founder confirmation of the new details)

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding, bedtime suitability and narration readiness

*This is an AI role-based assessment, not clinical or professional safeguarding advice.*

**What works.**
- **Skating** is handled exactly as the slate requires: the adult says no, explains that ice cannot be judged by looking, and says grown-ups must check and mark a place (l.62–70).
- **Sledging supervision** in the race itself is good: the adults chose the slope, Buck is at the top, Benny at the bottom, one rider at a time, and a clear run-out.
- **Snowballs** are thrown at a target, not at each other. The stray that knocks off the miller's hat is clearly accidental, Beau apologises at once, and the adult laughs it off.
- **The comic falls** land in deep soft snow and nobody is hurt.
- **Beau's unkind outburst and its repair.** The outburst is brief, its effect is shown (Charlie's face crumples) without dwelling, a friend names the harm, and Beau makes specific, unprompted apologies. This models repair well for the age group.
- **The emotional arc is safe.** The low stretch (end of Ch5 to the middle of Ch7) is about 700 words and turns quickly.
- **Wind-down.** Ch9 lowers the energy with the rosettes at sunset, and Ch10 is slow, warm and quiet. Shouting in capitals is confined to Ch8, well before the close.
- **Read-aloud clarity** is generally good. Short sentences, good comic rhythm and clear attribution throughout. Unattributed lines ("So?", l.190; Beau's cheering, l.378) are clear from context.

**Required changes.**

- **S1. A small child at the slope without an adult (Ch7, l.319–371).** The littlest rabbit is alone at the bottom of the hill; her mother is "not looking just then". Beau, a child himself, takes her up the slope and sends her down repeatedly, eventually "from nearly halfway up", with no adult knowing, until Ch8. This is the one real safeguarding gap. Before he takes her up the hill, Beau should call or take her to her mother (or Buck) and ask; the mother then watches from the bottom, smiling. That costs two lines, keeps the scene's warmth, and models asking a grown-up. (Rusty fetching the mother would also serve revision 5.)
- **S2. "I'll catch you if you go too fast" (l.365).** Children copy this. Standing in the path of a moving sledge to catch it is a common way children get hurt. Replace it with "I'll be right beside you" and keep her starting low.
- **S3. The Falling-Over Race (Ch8, l.406–418) teaches deliberate falling from moving sledges**, and Beau "went down backwards, on purpose" (l.414). A child who copies the game on a real slope risks injury, and backwards riding is something sledging guidance says never to do. Keep the joy and the judging, but change the event: a rolling contest on the flat run-out or the gentle foot of the slope with no sledges (a "roll-down", judged on the funniest roll), with Buck nodding it through and the one-at-a-time rule kept. Beau's accidental backwards ride in Ch5 is fine: it is a mishap, at the foot of a safe slope, and nobody copies it.
