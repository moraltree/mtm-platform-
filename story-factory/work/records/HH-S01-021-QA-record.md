## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 7.8 / 10. NOT PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

On the page, this is the funniest Season One story so far. The comedy escalates on stage and is properly built:
- the bow that grows from teacup to chair seat to floor, with Benny's "you'll be bowing underground";
- the "after you" queue at the bridge;
- the fly-filled Mill;
- the Maverick bow-off ending in a somersault and a topple;
- "offered to carry Reba instead".

The turn is genuine. Rusty's "stranger in a hat" (l.247) gives a hard truth from a friend. Beau "forgot all about bowing" (l.276) at Granny Opal's spill, which is the right, unforced pivot.

It is still nowhere near 8.5, because the draft breaks a cluster of rules and continuity points at once:
- **the season is wrong:** the story is called "summer" four times in a spring slot;
- **one of the two founder-named leads is decoration:** Sadie Belle appears in a single seven-line beat and never returns;
- **the theme is spelled out by characters** before the wisdom (Maggie Mae l.35, the miller's wife l.364–366), **and recapped after it** (l.440–448);
- **the close uses the "for the next time" construction** the reviewers asked S01 to drop, **and echoes 017's frog chorus;**
- **there are three hard continuity breaks:** the lesson days, where the hat is, and Granny Opal's Wednesday flour day with Maggie Mae;
- **there is a narrated white lie** in a story whose wisdom comes from the honesty block;
- **there are three "the way" constructions** against a limit of one.

Each item is fixable without touching the comic engine.

### Strengths

- **Escalation done properly (Ch2–4).** Each set piece costs more than the last: comic friction at Maggie Mae's, a jammed public crossing, a nuisance to the Mill, a mutual tumble with Maverick, and then a child in tears (l.204–208). The tears are the right first real cost, and they are kept short.
- **The bow as a running visual.** It measurably grows, with Benny's teacup, chair seat and floor sum at l.80. It is then pointedly absent: "without a single bow" (l.255), "did not bow to a single thing" (l.339), "He did not bow" (l.354). A child can track the arc through one object.
- **The pivot is earned and wordless.** "Beau forgot all about bowing … He just ran" (l.276–278). The best line in the draft is "Then he sat down in the dust beside her" (l.292). It is consideration shown, not told.
- **Granny Opal is handled with tenderness.** The detail that she sat still rather than be seen "in a muddle" (l.288) is true to a shy opossum who freezes when frightened (011). Beau's cure is to make *himself* the joke (l.303–319). This is consideration that costs him his dignity, which is the moral in action.
- **Lovely callbacks.** "Now there goes a true gentleman" (l.7) is answered by "Now there is a true gentleman" (l.360). Buck's "It came out. Tea always does" (l.411) works, and so does the neckerchief returned "smelling faintly of elderberry" (l.415).
- **Beau's locked visual is used as a plot instrument and restored.** The golden neckerchief becomes the flour bag (l.299) and is tied back on, "a bit crooked" (l.419).
- **Rusty earns his place.** He enjoys the "After YOU" game (l.131), and then gives the honest friend's verdict (l.243–247) and the reconciliation, "It suits you" (l.390).

### Weaknesses

1. **Season breach.** "Fine summer evening" (l.399), "the first truly warm evening of the summer" (l.430), "soft summer dusk" (l.460) and "the first frogs of summer" (l.468). S01 spring runs 016–022, and 022 must still read as spring. The 020 timeline (Gathering at the very end of May) puts this story in the first days of June, which is *late spring* for the series' purposes.
2. **Sadie Belle is not a lead.** She has one scene (l.86–92): she goes pink, drops her basket, and Beau does not notice. She is absent from the bridge, the tea party, the turning point, the reconciliation and the close. The brief names her as co-lead. The founder decision keeps the crush unnoticed *by Beau*, not unnoticed by the story.
   - The draft already has an empty slot that fits her exactly: the anonymous "family from along the creek" (l.288, l.341, l.348) who witnesses the flour scene and spreads the news.
   - The same slot is also a logic problem. Strangers watch an elderly neighbour sit in the road and do not help.
3. **The theme is said aloud before the wisdom.**
   - Maggie Mae at l.35: "You know everybody likes you already. Just as you are." That is "be yourself" stated in Ch1.
   - The miller's wife and Beau at l.364–366: "I just helped." / "That's right." That is "kindness beats manners" stated, and *validated by an adult*.
   - Both break house rule 1, and the second also weakens rule 2: the adult hands over the conclusion.
4. **The lesson is recapped after the wisdom (l.440–448).** Maggie Mae's "Do you remember lesson one? … Knowing when to stop", followed by Beau's mental replay ending "without a single bow anywhere" and "Oh", is a post-wisdom restatement. It also introduces a competing third moral (moderation, or "know when to stop"), alongside "be yourself" and "kindness over manners". The story would be cleaner with one.
5. **The close is not fresh enough.**
   - "For the next time … somebody needs something to take off to you" (l.464) is the "kept for next time" device the reviewers asked S01 closes to avoid.
   - The final line, "the first frogs … began to sing" (l.468), re-uses 017's close (the peeper chorus) only four stories later, and is also factually off (Sherlock S-1).
   - "Did not seem to mind them at all" (l.421) and "nobody minded at all" (l.460) echo 011's "did not hiss at all" and "nobody minded at all" (011 l.427).
   - The hat parade is the right comic idea for the porch, but it is the highest-energy beat of Ch10 and sits two paragraphs from the end. It needs a slower beat after it.
6. **Beau gives away a borrowed item (l.99, l.462).** The hat is "borrowed … from his father's cupboard". Gifting it to Granny Opal without a word about his father is a small lapse in *consideration*, in the one story whose moral is consideration.
7. **A narrated white lie (l.325).** "I'm going that way anyway." This was not true … But he said it anyway." The narrator approves a kind untruth told to a grown-up, and it is never owned. Rule 11 allows at most one untruth, and a lie plot must show telling a grown-up. Here the lie *is* told to the grown-up and is never corrected. The wisdom is drawn from the honesty block, so the line works against the close. It is also unnecessary: "I'll carry it home for you, if you'd like" is truthful and just as kind.
8. **The arc overlaps 012.** Beau wants public recognition, overreaches, upsets the littlest rabbit, and ends up honoured for kindness instead. That is 012's shape. The draft distinguishes itself well enough through the manners comedy and Granny Opal. The overlap is advisory for this story, but it is a flag for the season plan: Beau's next lead should not be a vanity arc.
9. **Repeated superlatives.**
   - "More refined than he had ever felt in his life" (l.84) and "more miserable than he had ever felt in his life" (l.264).
   - "The stiffest, most uncomfortable tea party that anybody … had ever been to" (l.231).
   - "The longest it's ever taken" (l.145).
   - One of the two "ever felt in his life" lines should go.
10. **Benny, Brody, Reba and Maverick are light.** Benny's sum (l.78–80) is funny but is commentary only. Brody is a prop (the small chair, then lying on the boards). Reba has one line (l.198). Maverick's bow-off is a pure set piece. Rule 3 is met by Charlie (she unjams the bridge), Rusty (the turning point) and Maggie Mae (the lessons), with Barney in a minor role (he fishes out the hat). It is not met *generously*.

### "Sherlock", facts and logic

**Verdict: NOT PASSED. Minor revise.** The natural history is mostly sound:
- swifts in early June;
- honeysuckle in late spring;
- garden peas ready in early June;
- elderberry coming into bloom;
- sparrows eating spilt flour;
- a chipmunk able to scamper along a beam;
- an opossum's freeze response, matching 011's fainting note.

**Required fixes**

S-1. **"The first frogs of summer began to sing" (l.468).** In an Appalachian valley, frogs have sung since March (spring peepers, and 017's close). Green frogs and bullfrogs do start in late May and June, but "first frogs" is false, and the line duplicates 017's close. Cut it or replace it (see R-10).

S-2. **The visitors' departure time (l.5).** A full day's walk means they would leave in the morning. See M-8.

S-3. **The bridge logic (l.105–125).** Rusty is already crossing after Beau's first "after you", so Beau's "No, no … I insist" contradicts his own invitation. That is fine if it is understood as Beau's cluelessness, but a listening child may simply be confused. A cleaner mechanism: both step on together, both step back, both say "after you" together, and repeat. That keeps the joke and makes the jam inevitable.

S-4. **Bridge structure (l.135).** "Underneath the planks, along the support beam" does not exist on a single-plank bridge with a mid-stream pier (002). See M-2 and Dr Noura D-2.

S-5. **The witness logic (l.288, l.341).** The family "coming up the lane behind" Opal must have stood at the bend for the whole scoop, walk and conversation. See M-9.

S-6. **Flour quantity (l.321).** "Nearly" a week's flour fitting into one neckerchief is plausible only for a small opossum's household. "Not quite a whole week's worth" already hedges this, so it is acceptable. No change is needed.

S-7. **The tea-party mechanics (l.233).** A tilted little finger making the paw wobble is fine. Hot tea is the issue; see Dr Noura.

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", child development, safety and emotional tone

**Verdict: PASS WITH REQUIRED FIXES.** The emotional modelling is very good:
- honest feedback between friends, given kindly (l.243–247);
- walking away to cool off rather than lashing out (l.255);
- repair through apology and action: to Rusty (l.382), to Buck (l.409), and to the littlest rabbit through play (l.372–374);
- an older creature's distress met with sitting *beside* her rather than rescuing *over* her (l.292). This is an excellent, imitable model of empathy;
- self-deprecating humour used to put someone else at ease.

The littlest rabbit's tears are brief and repaired the next morning. Nothing frightens. No humour is at anyone's expense except Beau's own, and he is in on it by Ch7.

Two tone notes:
- Rusty's dry "Was it?" (l.151) is the edge of sarcasm. It is acceptable because the tea party pays it off, but it could soften to "Was it?" said with a puzzled frown.
- The rabbit mother "very cross with Beau" (l.348) is fine.

**Required fixes**

D-1. **Walking alone at dusk (l.84–86).** Beau walks home "because it was getting dark" (bowing to trees "by mistake"), and Sadie Belle is out alone on the path. House rule 7 says nobody walks alone in the dark. Either make it "the long gold light of evening" (keeping the joke as bowing to a gatepost and a scarecrow-like stump in the low sun), or have Sadie Belle's mother just behind her on the path. The second fits Sadie Belle's canon (her mother is often present, as in 006 and 016).

D-2. **Charlie climbs under the bridge (l.135).** A small child clambering beneath a bridge over running water is imitable and unsafe, and adults are standing right there. Replace it with a safe, clever solution that is just as funny. For example, Charlie walks *between* the two queues, asks each creature where they are going, and points out that everybody on Beau's side wants to go one way and everybody on Rusty's side the other. Or she simply takes Rusty's paw and Beau's paw and walks them across *together*, side by side, one on the plank and one on the slatted slope. Or she rings the bridge bell and calls "Everybody on this side, go!", which uses the 002 bell. Any of these keeps her as the solver.
