## LOCK ONE: "Piers Morgan" (story quality)

**Score: 8.1 / 10. FAIL (pass mark 8.5)**

### Strengths
- **A fair-play mystery built on real science.** The clues are planted honestly and in order:
  - ordinary fireflies blink "each one in its own good time" (l.13), so the synchrony is the wonder, as the slate's canon flag required;
  - the failed nights (Ch3–4) show light and trampling as the problems;
  - "Look down" (l.176) reveals the answering lights in the grass;
  - the daytime beetle (Ch5) explains what the lights are;
  - the moon clue (l.292) explains when to watch.
  A child can follow the reasoning and get there a beat before the characters.
- **The children solve it, and the work is well shared out (Rules 2 and 3).** Each brief beat is present and in character:
  - Sadie Belle links Maverick's beetles to fireflies (l.211–225, l.239);
  - Benny reasons that fireflies need the dark to see each other's flashes (l.250);
  - Beau's "a candle at midday" (l.254) is goofy and correct;
  - Rusty reasons that the grass lights are answering, and owns his shortcut (l.256);
  - Maggie Mae turns it into a list (l.260);
  - Charlie runs the stillness practice (l.274);
  - Brody gives the key line, "You stop waiting for it to be over" (l.286);
  - Maverick provides the moon clue (l.292);
  - Barney is the comic engine, with the clever water, the ghosts and the drum.
  This is one of the strongest ensembles in S01.
- **The comedy escalates on stage (Rule 4).** Ch2 has the talk-comedy theories ("Very clever water", l.56; "a dance without ghosts", l.74). Ch3 has the noisy expedition, with lantern flickering, muddled whip-poor-will and owl whistles, and Beau's full-body signal. Ch4 has the bigger lantern and the BOOM. "Everything Goes Wrong in a Different Way" is a good chapter title that keeps its promise.
- **Respect is shown, not told.** Three beats stand out:
  - Rusty stops with his paw in the air and does not pick up the sleeping beetle (l.233–235);
  - everybody keeps to the middle of the path, "and did not let so much as a toe touch the long grass" (l.186);

- **Ch7–8 is a superb, narratable set piece.** It moves from moonset (l.317–321) to the held stillness (l.325) to the build: "one small light… Then another… Then a dozen… Then a hundred" (l.329–338). - **Canon voice and habits are on point.** Rusty has the canon shortcut line, "Trust me… I know a shortcut" (l.146), birdsong whistling, white-tipped tail and amber eyes. ### Sherlock: verdict REVISE (minor)

**Required fixes**
- **S-1. Noise does not disrupt firefly displays.** Fireflies (Lampyridae) have no known hearing of airborne sound. Light is the established disruptor, which is why park guidance asks for no lights, or dim covered lights pointed at the ground. Quiet is visitor etiquette.
  - As written, the narration shows the display faltering at the moment of the BOOM, and Benny blames "both" (l.162–172). Sadie Belle's hypothesis "dark. And quiet." (l.248) is then never tested, because night 3 is both.
  - Fix as L1-4: the light is the visible cause on stage. Optional Benny line: "I don't think fireflies have ears. But they've got eyes."
- **S-2. The first sighting is too early (l.5).** "A little after the sun had gone down behind Weather Ridge" is twilight, and in a valley the sun goes behind the ridge even earlier. *P. carolinus* synchrony builds after full dark, typically an hour or more after sunset. Ordinary meadow fireflies (*Photinus pyralis*) are the dusk flyers, which l.13 already, correctly, describes.
  - Fix: "long after the sun had gone down behind Weather Ridge, when the last of the light had gone out of the sky". l.3 already says "dark evening".
  - The Ch3 failure (arriving at sundown with lanterns, l.107) then becomes doubly correct: they were too early, too bright and too loud.
- **S-3. "New moon" for a visible crescent (l.292, l.309).** Astronomically, the new moon is invisible. The thin crescent a few days later is the "young moon". Folk usage does call it "the new moon", but a child who later learns the term will find it confusing.
  - Fix: "A young one. Very thin." (l.292) and "there was the young moon" (l.309).
- **S-4. The moon-phase logic should acknowledge the waxing.**
  - Night 0, with no moon (l.54), means new moon around night 0, so night 3's crescent is about 3 days old. That is correct. A crescent of that age sets roughly two hours after the sun on a flat horizon, and sooner behind a western ridge, so "very soon after the sun" (l.292) is acceptable for children.
  - But nights 1 and 2 also had a thinner moon, low in the twilight. And the moon sets later and shines brighter each night, so the watching window gets worse as the week goes on.
  - Maverick's "Tonight there's a moon" slightly implies it is new information.
  - Fix (one clause): "The moon's back. Just a sliver, low in the west over Weather Ridge. It's a little bigger every night, and goes down a little later, so tonight's the best of it. It goes down soon after the sun." This is more accurate, more Maverick, and adds gentle urgency.

**Verified as sound (the brief's checklist)**
- **Flash pattern:** males flash about 5–8 times together, then go dark for about 6–9 seconds. The story has 5–7 (l.64), six flashes counted (l.346), "Six, mostly.
