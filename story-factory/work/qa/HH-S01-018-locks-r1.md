## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.0 / 10. NOT PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

The draft takes the VARIETY flag seriously and answers it well. This is a discovery story, not a mishap story, and its central device is excellent. Maggie Mae's terse blue card, seven lines long, is decoded one line at a time, and each line opens onto a piece of quiet, unseen kindness. "Me. Six." is a superb final reveal, and the blue-boat cup makes a lovely closing gift. It still falls short of the mark, for six reasons:
- **an Absolute Prohibition is broken:** Charlie tells a littlest *rabbit* a story about a "VERY HUNGRY" *fox* (l.238);
- **the moral is spelled out three times before Buck speaks;**
- **the comedy is flat in Ch2–4 and does not escalate.** Ch4 is not comic at all;
- **two helper beats use the same solution** ("fetch somebody tall");
- **there is a plot-logic hole:** Maggie Mae's note "last night" (l.130), about a surprise she only learned of this morning;
- **there are three safety slips involving heights.**

### Strengths

- **The device is the story.** The blue card (l.39–46) works as structure, comic engine and emotional ledger all at once. Charlie's "Seven words. Well, fourteen." against Maggie Mae's "Is it?" (l.50–54) is a perfect hook. The countdowns ("five lines to go", l.170; "two lines left", l.346; "one line left", l.415) are accurate and give a child a real sense of progress.
- **The VARIETY flag is honoured.** Unlike 003, nothing breaks and nobody is cross. Each chapter turns a wrong guess into a discovery (clock → winding, wool → company, exciting story → nap), which is exactly what the concept review asked for.
- **"Me. Six." (Ch8) is the best scene of the story.** Maggie Mae's sudden shyness (l.421–427), "It's the only line on the list that's for me", and Charlie's decision to give her the ten minutes *and leave* (l.455) form a real, character-driven turning point. Charlie, who "had never in her whole life been able to leave anybody alone", choosing silence is earned growth, and it lands.
- **The continuity is strong:**
  - Granny Opal's "I've plenty of wool. Folk have been very good" (l.199) quietly pays off 011's "Bring her spare wool";
  - Opal's shyness and small creaky laugh match 011;
  - the miller's wife's back "since last autumn" matches 009, 010 and 015;
  - Charlie's stillness, "learned that winter, on a snowy porch … as Sadie Belle had shown her" (l.260–262), is a true and touching callback to 013;
  - the rabbit family's seven little ones match 011;
  - Maggie Mae's garden chair matches 016.
- **The running gag is good.** Maggie Mae fails to relax: the book on the same page (l.183), drumming her fingers (l.183, 315, 431), the hat falling off (l.319), and "Sorry. … Day off. … Sorry." (l.362). It is gentle, character-true comedy, and it is the story's best comic thread.
- **The close is calm, warm and fresh in location.** The wisdom comes on a lane bank, not the porch, and the close is on Rose Arch's front step. "Nobody's ever stayed awake long enough to find out" (l.521) is a lovely bedtime joke. Buck under the straw hat (l.523) is a fine comic grace note.
- **The prose is clean and narratable,** and the British English is consistent.

### Weaknesses

1. **A predator-and-prey story is told to a rabbit (major; Bible §15).**
   - "There was a FOX, and he was VERY HUNGRY … and he met a BEAR, and the bear said GRRRRR" (l.238), followed by "a very loud owl" (l.240).
   - The story is told to the littlest rabbit, who ends up with "her ears flat" (l.242).
   - This is a hungry fox, plus an owl, frightening a rabbit. It is a direct predator-and-prey reference. It also sits badly beside canon Rusty ("never a stereotypically deceitful fox"; "a world where a fox and a chipmunk are friends").
   - The comic idea (too exciting for a nap) is right. The content must change.
2. **The moral is signposted three times before Buck (major; house rule 1).**
   - l.339: "she began to wonder … whether a day off was really what Maggie Mae had wanted."
   - l.433–439: "And all at once, Charlie understood something. Maggie Mae had not needed a whole day off … What Maggie Mae needed was ten minutes …" The narrator states the thesis outright, about 117 words.
   - l.476: "every single job, I got it wrong first. Because I didn't know what they needed. The clock didn't need the time …" This is the moral in Charlie's own words, said immediately before Buck's wisdom. It is a summarising lead-in in all but name.
   - l.474: a 97-word recap montage of the whole day, the same fault flagged in 017.
   - The scene already *shows* the realisation: Charlie looks at the step, then at the untouched chair, then walks silently into the kitchen (l.429–441). Trust it.
3. **The comedy in Ch2–4 is flat and does not escalate (house rule 4).**
   - Ch2 has one verbal misreading ("It's half past eight!") and a short tiptoe-on-the-chair beat.
   - Ch3 is a verbal "Line?" puzzle and a lumpy shirt.
   - Ch4 is poignant, not funny.
   - The real comic peak is Ch5 (which must be rewritten, W1), and Ch6's bird's-nest darn comes after the rule-4 window.
   - The brief asks for "comic confusion". It is there, but it is mild and it plateaus. The easiest fix is to escalate the Maggie Mae runner on stage: each time Charlie comes back, Maggie Mae has secretly done one more small thing, and is "caught" doing it. This keeps the comedy away from mishap (the VARIETY flag) and puts it in the scene.
4. **Two helper beats use the same solution, "fetch somebody tall" (l.99, l.150–152).**
   - "Benny's tall" (l.99) is also new and unsupported. Brody is the Hollow's tallest youngster (l.152). Benny's canon gift is careful, inventive planning, not height.
   - The washing-line scene even plants the answer and then ignores it: "a basket, and a prop, and a pegbag" (l.136). Taking the clothes prop out lowers a washing line. That is a real, child-sized, clever solution, and it is Charlie's own.
   - So the ensemble is two "be tall" jobs plus Rusty, who only reports a need. See house rule 3.
5. **There is a plot-logic hole (l.130).**
   - The miller's wife says Maggie Mae "popped a note under the door **last night** … She said you'd be coming instead."
   - But the surprise was sprung on Maggie Mae "before breakfast" (l.15–33), and she sat in the garden chair all day.
   - She could not have known the night before.
6. **Charlie's own detective work is thin.** Most needs are *told* to her: by Buck (l.85), the miller's wife (l.130, 146), Opal (l.199) and Rusty (l.301). This is thematically coherent with #76, since listening is the lesson. But it leaves Charlie with few deductions of her own before Ch8. The clothes-prop fix (W4) and one small deduction in Ch5 would help.
7. **The voice has tics and recycled phrasing.**
   - "very" appears 68 times, and "slowly" 17 times.
   - "face went soft" (l.31, l.276) also appears in 001 and 013. "something had come undone in her" (l.325) and "As if she had put something heavy down" (l.487) are stock.
   - The introduction of Maggie Mae (l.9: "best friend, and something like a big sister … black and white and elegant") is almost verbatim from 013's opening (013 l.27).
   - "in the last of the evening sun/light" appears twice (l.509, l.525) and also in 003, 009, 013 and 016.
   - "drawing herself up to her full height" (l.23) echoes 004.
8. **The closing coda follows a recent pattern.** "the ladybirds went on slowly about their work in the last of the evening light" (l.525) is the fourth "nature carries on while we rest" coda in a row: 015's wheels, 016's bank asleep, 017's leaves "as if they had all the time in the world". It is not a banned beat, but rule 5 asks for a fresh close every time. The last line, "nobody needed anything from anybody at all", is good and could close the story on its own, without the ladybird clause.

### "Sherlock", natural history and physics

**Verdict: PASS WITH NOTES. S-1 to S-4 required.**

**Confirmed accurate:**
- **Greenfly (aphids) on roses in May (l.374–376):** accurate. They are pale green, soft and clustered on undersides, stems and new buds, and they feed on sap. Heavy infestations make buds distort or fail to open, which is fair for a child's level, though "never open" slightly overstates it. Optional: "and the buds come out crooked, or never open at all".
- **Ladybirds eat aphids (l.380):** accurate. "Small and red and round and shiny, with black spots" fits the seven-spot.
- **Larvae as "strange little black-and-orange creatures that looked like tiny crocodiles" (l.409):** accurate. Seven-spot larvae are dark grey-black with orange spots, and "tiny alligators/crocodiles" is the standard gardener's description. Larvae appearing in mid-May is plausible in a warm spring.
- **Washing greenfly off gently with water (l.380):** accurate as a gentle control. "Or spray them" is fine, but cutting it improves timelessness (Canon 1).
- **Water the roots, not the leaves; wet leaves "give them spots" (l.362):** accurate. Black spot (*Diplocarpon rosae*) spreads by water splash and needs wet leaf surfaces.
- **Climbing roses "just coming into bud … the first few beginning to open" in mid-May (l.352):** accurate for a southern Appalachian setting, where the main flush is May to June.
- **Darning (l.329):** accurate. Unpick the failed attempt, lay a frame of parallel threads across the hole (the warp, "like the strings of a loom"), then weave over and under row by row. A slightly different shade and a little lumpiness are realistic.
- **Winding and restarting a pendulum clock (l.101):** accurate in sequence. Wind, counting the turns; set the hands; start the pendulum swinging.
- **Shelling peas in mid-May (l.124):** plausible for southern Appalachia, where English peas come in mid-May to June.

**Required fixes:**

- **S-1. "Runs for exactly one week, that clock, and then it wants winding … It always stops on a Wednesday morning" (l.85).**
  - Weekly-wound longcase clocks are *eight-day* clocks, built to run about eight days so that a weekly winding never lets them stop.
  - A clock wound every Wednesday morning that runs "exactly one week" would stop only if it was wound late.
  - Fix: "It's an eight-day clock, by rights, but it's old, and it never quite makes seven these days. Stops every Wednesday about dawn, and then it wants winding." This also explains the ten-past-four stop (l.79).
- **S-2. "the key goes in right at the top" (l.85).**
  - On a longcase clock the winding holes are in the *dial face*: you open the hood door and wind through the dial.
  - The dial is still well above a small animal's reach on a tall clock, so the plot is unaffected.
  - Change to "and the keyholes are up in the clock's face, there".
- **S-3. Sunset timing (l.411, l.509).**
  - In mid-May, sunset is about 8:30 pm. At about 5:30 to 6:20 pm the sun is not "going down towards Weather Ridge", and 6:20 is not "the last of the evening sun".
  - A western ridge would hide the sun earlier, but not by two and a half hours.
  - Fix: "the sun was sliding westward towards Weather Ridge, and the light was beginning to turn gold" (l.411), and "in the long gold evening sun" (l.509).
- **S-4. "a blackbird on the roof of Rose Arch" (l.478).**
  - The singing rooftop blackbird is the Eurasian *Turdus merula*, which does not occur in Appalachia. North American "blackbirds" (red-winged blackbirds, grackles) are marsh and field birds with harsh calls.
  - 017's report flagged the same issue.
  - Use "a robin on the roof of Rose Arch". The American robin sings at dusk from rooftops in May.

**Advisory:**
- **S-5.** "ate even more greenfly than the grown-up ladybirds" (l.409) is widely repeated but contestable: adults also eat large numbers. "ate greenfly all day long" is safer.
- **S-6.** The ladybird–greenfly relationship is unavoidably an "animals eating one another" reference under Bible §15's literal wording. Prior seasons used ladybirds without predation. The founder's own SHERLOCK list names "larvae eating aphids", which suggests the founder intends it, but this needs **an explicit founder ruling** (see Dr Noura N-5). If it is not approved, softer verbs work: "the ladybirds see to them" and "keep them down".

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding, bedtime suitability and narration readiness

**Verdict: PASS WITH NOTES. N-1 to N-4 required; founder ruling on N-5.**

**What is done well:**
- Flames and boiling water are explicitly adult-handled, with the rules stated plainly (l.443) ✔.
- Granny Opal's anxiety about busy places is handled with real tenderness. Charlie offers a paw, talks so Opal need not, and makes no fuss (l.207–213). This is excellent modelling of quiet support for a shy or anxious child.
- Sad moments are brief and resolved at once (l.246–252, l.313).
- Nobody is out alone after dark. Charlie's solo lane-side wait is in early evening daylight, with Buck arriving.
- The ending is an exemplary wind-down: shared quiet, slow tea, an old beaver humming under a hat, "nobody needed anything from anybody at all". It is calming and safe, and it models rest as something you can give to someone you love.

**Required fixes:**

- **N-1. Heights (rule 7).**
  - (a) Benny, a youngster, climbs the very chair Buck says he himself fell off (l.91, l.101). The fall line makes the danger explicit and then hands it to a child. Remove the fall (M-8), and use Benny's sturdy step-box with Buck steadying it (R-4), or have the hood lifted down for winding at table height.
  - (b) Charlie "stood on her tiptoes on the chair, and stretched" (l.95). Keep the comic failure, but add Buck's steadying paw on the chair-back ("Steady, little one"), or have her stand flat-footed and still fall short.
  - (c) "with Charlie standing on the garden wall for the high ones" (l.409), reaching porch and arch height among thorny stems. Change it to "with Maggie Mae doing the high ones and Charlie doing the low ones, which were the best ones for crocodiles".
- **N-2. Stove and hot tea (l.443).**
  - Maggie Mae lights the stove and "went back outside", leaving Charlie alone with a lit stove and a heating kettle. Change this so she stays in the kitchen doorway: "and leaned in the doorway, pretending not to watch".
  - Charlie then carries a freshly poured hot cup "with both paws". Either Maggie Mae carries the cup out and Charlie carries the milk jug and cushion, or the tea is poured "half full, so as not to slop".
  - Both changes can be made without adding words, by trimming the over-explained rules in the same sentence.
- **N-3. Rusty's grandmother (l.301).** "It's the only thing I've got of hers" brings implied bereavement to a lead character mid-story, then leaves it unacknowledged. This is new sorrow canon, and the Bible's sorrow allowance (Boone, Bramble) is narrow. Soften it (M-7), or obtain founder approval.
- **N-4. The predator story (l.238–240).** Required under Bible §15 (R-2). A hungry fox frightening a rabbit is exactly the image the Hollow forbids, and the listener is a frightened little one. The replacement should be loud and exciting, not scary: bumps, bouncing and silly voices.

**Founder ruling:**
- **N-5. Insect predation (l.380, l.409, l.525).** Ladybirds and their larvae eating greenfly is natural, gentle and gardening-true, and the founder's own SHERLOCK list names it. But it is literally "animals eating one another" under §15. Recommend founder approval with softened verbs ("see to", "keep down", "tidy up") and no "eat" in the closing image. l.525 currently keeps the larvae, which is fine as long as they are not eating.

**Bedtime suitability:** high once N-4 is fixed. The emotional arc goes from eager, to puzzled, to humbled, to giving, to quiet. That is ideal for sleep. Nothing in the second half raises arousal.

**Narration readiness:**
- **The list (l.39–46):** the narrator needs a note to read it as a card, with pauses. "Bridge, 11." must be voiced "Bridge, eleven". Consider spelling it out in the text for audio.
- **Duplicated lines:** each chapter title is immediately repeated as the chapter's first line (l.67, 118, 175, 226, 289, 344), and "Roses." is spoken three times in a row in Ch7 (l.342, 344, 350). In audio this sounds like a mistake. Drop the in-text repeats (R-9).
- **All-caps shouting (l.238):** these lines will be rewritten under R-2. Limit the replacement to one or two capitalised words, so the narrator does not have to shout into a child's bedtime.
- **Long sentences that need breath points:**
  - l.5 (70 words);
  - l.199 (Opal's 126-word speech, which has natural pauses and is fine with the ellipsis);
  - l.213 (92 words);
  - l.443 (127 words; split it while doing N-2);
  - l.474 (97 words; cut under R-1).
- **Dialogue attribution** is clear throughout. Unattributed lines (l.89, 250, 404) alternate between two speakers.
- **"Bless you" (l.278)** may be heard as a sneeze response in audio. Consider "Oh, thank you".

### Summary

"Charlie's Big-Sister Surprise" answers its VARIETY flag well. It is a discovery story built on a lovely device: Maggie Mae's seven-line blue card, decoded line by line into hidden kindnesses. It has a superb "Me. Six." turning point, and Mountain Wisdom #76, verified word for word and delivered bare. Its continuity with 009–016 is unusually careful.

It scores **8.0/10** and does not yet pass Lock One, because:
- a "VERY HUNGRY" fox story told to a rabbit breaks the §15 predator-and-prey prohibition;
- the moral is spelled out by the narrator and by Charlie just before Buck;
- the Ch2–4 comedy is mild and does not escalate;
- two helper beats are both "fetch somebody tall";
- Maggie Mae's note "last night" is impossible.
