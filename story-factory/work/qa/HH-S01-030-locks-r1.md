## LOCK ONE: "Piers Morgan" (story quality)

**Score: 8.0 / 10. FAIL (pass mark 8.5)**

### Strengths
- **The founder default is honoured cleanly, and with real elegance.**
  - The brothers see the river, want it, and choose to stay (l.185–205, l.318–322). There is no log, no River Rangers, no departure, and no Season Two dependency.
  - The gold leaf drifting downstream "towards… the far silver river" while the brothers stay on the bank (l.362–372) is a fine solution. It nods to the Canon 5 image of something carried down the creek to the wide world, yet nobody gets in, nobody follows, and the text says so ("They did not follow it", l.372). It is exactly a hint, not a launch.
- **The set pieces are among the best writing in the season.**
  - The reveal at the edge (l.126–134) builds well: the falls, then the valley, then the creek joining another, then "Something wide, and slow, and silver". "A river," breathed Benny is earned.
  - Chapter Seven, the cool wind, is outstanding. The wind moves through trees, then meadow, then bridge. Barney's "It's actually cold" (l.263), the miller's ears (l.265), Reba smelling "apples, and woodsmoke, and hickory nuts, and pumpkins" (l.265), and the honest correction "It was not really cold" (l.267) are all vivid and child-sized. The rain spots that "dried, and then did not dry, and then joined up with the next" (l.275) are a lovely piece of observation.
  - The leaf as a structural device works. It is planted in Ch6 (l.240–248), loosened by the wind of Ch7, and paid off in Ch10, and the tree is "every one of its leaves green again" afterwards (l.376).
- **Charlie's question is the true emotional engine.** "You looked at it like you wanted to go" (l.177) and "I don't want you to go" (l.193) are pitched perfectly for the audience. Her later promise "to be brave, when things changed" (l.312) gives the "courage to embrace change" strand to the youngest character, which is right.
- **Buck is handled with restraint.** He sets safety conditions (l.54) and otherwise lets the youngsters discover. His one memory, "My own grandpappy took me" (l.228), and his long look at Benny "as if he were seeing something a long way off" (l.234), are moving without explaining anything.
- **Callbacks are warm and earned.**
  - Barney's drum: "No," said everybody (l.70–76), paying off 027's drum put away.
  - The three-eyed kite (028).
  - Buck's Speed "in threes" (l.117) and Brody's "So it never gets lost again" (l.306), from 026.
  - Spring water from Rusty's spring and the miller's wife's cucumber sandwiches (029).
- **Clean on banned phrases.** None of the Rule 6 list appears. There are **zero** "the way X does" similes.
- **The Wisdom is verbatim** (l.337 matches §8 #79 word for word), has no summarising lead-in, and is unused in 001–029 (searched for "glad you came back" and "Home isn't").

### Weaknesses
1. **The Wisdom is telegraphed repeatedly before Buck says it (Rule 1).** #79 is the sum of two ideas: home is the folk, and the folk are glad you came back. The story states both, several times, before l.337:
   - l.185 Benny: "And everybody's here." This is "home is the folk", given as his reason to stay.
   - l.197 Benny: "We'd come back. We'd always come back."
   - **l.310 Granny Opal** promises "to be there when everybody came back, wherever they had been, and that she would want to hear all about it." This is the Wisdom acted out almost word for word ("folk who are glad you came back").
   - l.318 Benny: "Because this is home."
   - l.320 Barney: "But we'll come back. We'll always come back."
   - l.331 is a narrator reverie: "Some of them had gone over the ridges… And some of them had come back. And some of them had stayed…". It is the theme in narrator voice, followed by "He did not say any of that" (l.333). The device announces that a moral is being held back.
   - "Come back" or "came back" appears **11 times** in total. When Buck speaks, the line confirms what the audience has heard four times rather than landing as a new thought.
   - Smaller items: the narrator aphorism "because sometimes it helps to be carried a little way" (l.207), and the post-Wisdom narrator summary at l.372–374 ("They did not need to… who had decided, for now, to stay exactly where they were"). See also Rule 1 in the table.
2. **The close re-runs 029's close almost beat for beat (Rule 5).**
   - In 029 (l.352–372): "Benny and Barney sat on a little longer" by the creek at dusk, talk about where the water goes, then "Rusty's father called them from the path, holding up his lantern… The brothers stood up… and went up the bank to the path."
   - In 030 (l.346–380): the brothers sit on the creek bank at dusk, talk about where the water goes ("And on"), and Rusty's father waits at the top of the bank with a lantern. "The two brothers climbed the bank together, to where Rusty's father was waiting with the lantern."
   - Same characters, same place, same adult, same lantern, same climb, in consecutive stories. It also echoes 009 ("Buck held the lantern for them and waited, and did not hurry them at all") and the walk-home-by-lantern closes of 002, 010, 012, 017, 021 and 026.
   - l.376's "the Hollow was going home to bed, friends calling goodnight" is the shape of 023's "all over the Hollow, families were going home to bed".
   - The leaf (l.362–372) is fresh and is the right final image for the season. The lantern climb after it dilutes it.
3. **The "brothers linger" device is used three times in two stories.** It appears in 029's close, in 030 Ch4 (l.160, "All except Benny and Barney, who stood a little longer"), and in 030 Ch10. The Ch4 use is also a safety failure (see N-2).
4. **The dramatic question is answered twice, so the second half loses tension.**
   - Charlie asks in Ch5. Benny answers in full at once (l.197), and the promise is sealed (l.205).
   - Ch8 then repeats the same answer as a group ritual (l.318–322) with the same words.
   - Barney's longing and Benny's caution are never set against each other: they agree immediately every time. Barney is the one who wants to go (l.21, l.187). A single beat where he wavers, and Benny's answer costs him something, would give the promise weight.
   - The Ch8 promise list is charming but runs as a list: twelve promises in a row with little shaping. Rusty's father and the miller's wife can go, and two or three of the youngsters' promises could be folded together.
5. **The comedy in Ch2–4 is present but mostly summarised (Rule 4).**
   - The packing is the best of it: the drum's double "No", and Reba's three lunches escalating (l.78), paid off at lunch (l.115).
   - The three physical gags (Barney's slides, Beau's leap, Brody as a step) are packed into one narrated paragraph (l.93) with no dialogue and no build. Beau's leap "with ferns in his ears" deserves its own short staged beat.
   - Ch4 rightly drops comedy for awe. For a finale, the lighter comic load is fair. But Rule 4 asks for staged escalation, and at present only the packing is staged.
6. **Logic and wording slips.**
   - **l.13–15.** "I've been thinking about that all week" / "Ever since the evening the whole Hollow had sat by the creek". That evening was **last night** (029 Ch9–10, Friday), and in 029 Benny says "I've never thought about that before" (029 l.364). Make it "all night" or "since last night".
   - **l.148 / l.150.** Benny stands "with his notebook open in his paws", then "Then Benny opened his notebook again".
   - **l.148.** "Eyes as round as Benny's notebook": a notebook is not round. It is a confusing simile for a listener.
   - **l.87.** "Waved from her through-draught" makes sense only if the listener remembers 029's mechanism. Say "waved from her open door".
   - **l.142.** Maverick "I always turn back at the ridges. I never knew there was all this." This contradicts 020. See M-1.
   - **l.354.** "It'll be full again by morning." Not plausible after a few hours of soft rain on a drought-dry valley. See S-2.
7. **Ch9 steps backwards right before the Wisdom (narration).** After Buck's reverie (l.331–333) the narration drops into the pluperfect: "Maverick had gone home… The grown-ups had lit the lanterns" (l.335). Then the Wisdom (l.337). A listener cannot tell when Maverick left relative to the promises he made at l.312. Move Maverick's departure and the lantern-lighting to the end of Ch8, so that Ch9 runs straight from the rain to Buck to the Wisdom. This is the same issue as 028 r1 L1-6.
8. **Repetition.** "Come back/came back" ×11. "A long time/a long while" ×6, plus "Not for a long, long time" (l.185), "Not for years and years" (l.197) and "Not for a long, long while" (l.372). "Softly" ×5, "slowly" ×5, "quietly" ×4, "gently" ×4. "As if" ×4 is fine.

### Sherlock: verdict REVISE (minor)

**Required fixes**
- **S-1. Positioning of the falling leaf (l.362, l.376).** The leaf comes "down out of the dark above them" onto the water at their feet, yet the tree is "behind them, up on the bank" (l.376), and its gold leaf was on "one of its lowest branches, right over the path" (l.240). Add one clause so that the physics works: "the old hickory, whose long lowest branch reached out over the creek", or have the light breeze carry the leaf out to them.
- **S-2. The creek's recovery is overstated (l.281, l.348–354).**
  - A few hours of soft, light rain on a valley at the end of a summer-long dry spell and a heatwave mostly soaks into the parched ground. The first effect would be a visible but modest rise: darker stones, the thin ribbon widening, the trickle quickening. "A little trickle of water began to grow" (l.281) is fine.
  - "Now there was a creek again, small and quick and chattering" (l.350) is generous but storybook-acceptable.
  - **Benny's "It'll be full again by morning" (l.354) is not plausible.** Change it to something like: "It'll take a lot more rain than this to fill it," said Benny. "But it's started." That is truer and better in character for careful Benny.
  - "Rising a little with every hour of rain that had come down off the ridges" (l.350) is good, because runoff from the rocky ridges is the right mechanism.
- **S-3. The Beaver Ponds (l.87).** Beaver dams hold back water, so even in a severe drought a dammed pond shrinks and develops a mud margin, but rarely becomes "a few shallow pools" unless the dam fails. Combine this with M-2: the ponds are low, with a mud rim, and the lodges are higher out of the water.
- **S-4. The Mill wheel "still and dry" (l.87).** In 029 there was "still water in the race" on Wednesday (029 l.224). "Stood still for lack of water" is plausible, because there is not enough flow to turn it. "Dry" contradicts 029. Cut "and dry".

**Verified as sound**
- **Great blue lobelia (*Lobelia siphilitica*).** It flowers from about August to October, in wet, shady places: streambanks, seeps and swampy woods. It is native and widespread in the Southern Appalachians. A seep in "a damp shady hollow under a cliff" (l.101) staying wet through a drought is plausible. Minor colour note: the flowers are a clear, deep blue to blue-violet. "A blue so dark it was nearly purple" is a stretch but defensible. "Tall spikes" is right. "Nodding" is slightly off, since the spikes are erect, though individual flowers can tilt. Optional: "tall spikes of deep, bright blue, standing up in the shade".
- **A single early-turning hickory leaf in early September (l.240–248).** Plausible. Drought and heat stress commonly cause individual leaves or branches to yellow and drop weeks early, and hickories turn a clear butter-yellow to gold, which matches "bright, buttery gold". It is especially fitting after the 029 heatwave. One leaf alone on a huge tree is a storybook simplification. Buck's "Every year… just one" (l.248) is presented as his lore, not fact, so it is acceptable (list it as new canon). A hickory leaf is compound, so "from tip to stalk" (l.242) is fine. A stressed leaf letting go in the evening after a steady wind (l.364) is plausible.
- **The cool north wind ending the heat (Ch7).** A cold front with a wind shift to the north, a sharp drop in temperature, and then light, steady rain behind it is a classic end-of-heatwave pattern. Fronts in this region often bring thunder, but a quiet, stratiform rain behind the front is equally common, and the text is explicit that there was "no thunder, and no great dark cloud" (l.257). The rain "eased to almost nothing… a soft fine mist" by nightfall (l.344) is plausible. "Not really cold" (l.267) is honest. Approaching "over High Ridge… where nobody could see it coming" (l.255) is fine (see new canon 9 on orientation).
- **The creek leaving through a gap (Ch4).** A creek leaving a mountain-ringed valley through a notch, stepping down in small cascades (l.130), is geologically ordinary (a water gap). "Thin and silver in the low summer water" is correct for drought flow.
- **A river visible in the distance (l.130–132).** From a high ledge on clear afternoon air, a large river several miles off would show as a "wide, slow, silver" band catching the sun. "Curling away into the haze" fits the Blue Ridge's characteristic summer haze. The creek joining another creek and then a river is the Bible's own sequence (origin tale, bible l.3431–3433).
- **Timing.** They leave a little before nine o'clock, walk several hours with stops, eat lunch, reach the edge in the early afternoon, and are back at the bridge in the late afternoon (l.216). Sunset in early September is around 7.45 pm local time in the Southern Appalachians, so "back well before dark" holds.
- **A stone bottle keeping water "still nearly cold"** (l.115). Stoneware stays cool for hours in the shade. Fine.
- **Five youngsters holding paws round the trunk** (l.238). That gives roughly a 1 m diameter, which is large but real for an old open-grown hickory.
- **The hawk's feather (l.209).** Identified by an eagle, found on a path. Moulted feathers are common and neutral. There is no predation implied. (Real-world note only: in the US, collecting raptor feathers is legally restricted. The text does not have anyone keep it. The stone with a hole is what Charlie keeps, which is good.)

### New invented details for founder confirmation

The creek leaves the Hollow there in a series of small falls. It is reached by an overgrown old path beyond the last meadows and cottages, past a "big grey rock", a dry gully, a mossy bank and a fallen tree (l.87–126). **This must be reconciled with 020's low pass (M-1)** before confirmation.
2. **The country beyond the edge.** A wide land of hills, woods and fields. The creek joins another creek, and both join a wide, slow, silver river visible in the far distance (l.130–132). This is distinct from 020's smaller next valley with its quick brown river.

## LOCK THREE: "Dr Noura" (child safety, wellbeing, bedtime suitability) and narration readiness

### Dr Noura: verdict PASS WITH CONDITIONS

**Founder safeguarding defaults:** **met.** No young beaver goes on open water at any point. The only drifting object is a leaf, and the brothers explicitly "did not follow it" (l.372). That is good modelling of the exact imitation risk the slate flagged. The brothers stand in a *dry* creek bed in Ch1 (l.9).

**Expedition supervision:** largely good.
- Two grown-ups for ten youngsters, one of whom knows the old paths (l.64).
- Buck's conditions: not alone, with a grown-up, back well before dark (l.54).
- Water, hats and shade on a hot day (l.48, l.68).
- An adult stops the sliding (l.93). Grown-ups help everybody over the fallen tree "one at a time" (l.93).
- Nobody enters water. Paws in the trickle only (l.117).
- Back in the late afternoon, well before dark (l.216).
- Barney at the edge: Rusty's father's paw on his shoulder, and Barney stops (l.144–146). This is exactly right, and Barney's "I'm not going anywhere" models the right attitude.

**Conditions (required)**
- **N-1. Everyone standing "on the edge of the grey rock, in a row" (l.136).** Ten young listeners hear their heroes line up on the lip of a drop above falls. Have the adults place them first, for example: "They all stood in a row, a good long step back from the lip, where Sadie Belle's mother had told them to stand." Then Barney's step forward at l.144 visibly breaks that line, and the paw on his shoulder answers it.
- **N-2. The brothers left alone at the cliff edge (l.158–160).** "They turned, one by one, and went back into the trees. All except Benny and Barney, who stood a little longer on the edge." Both adults have left, and two youngsters are unsupervised at a drop in the wild, far from home. **Cut the lingering** (also L1-3). If a final look is wanted, have an adult wait beside them, for example "Rusty's father waited, a paw on each of their shoulders, until they were ready".
- **N-3. Beau's leap across the dry gully (l.93).** He "very nearly managed" it and lands in ferns, and no adult reacts, whereas the bottom-sliding is stopped. Unrewarded but unchallenged jumping of gaps on a wild path is a mild imitation risk. Have Rusty's father call the leaping off as well, or make the gully a narrow, shallow ditch ("no deeper than a puddle"). This combines naturally with L1-4.
- **N-4. Night at the edge of a rising creek (l.346–362).** Two youngsters sit at the water's edge in near-dark ("the water at their feet", l.362) while the creek is "rising a little with every hour", and the adult stands "at the top of the bank, a little way back". Put Rusty's father *beside* them on the bank with the lantern, or seat the brothers on the bank top. The water itself is small, but the posture (youngsters at moving water, at night, with the adult apart) is what children copy. This also helps L1-2, because the adult does not have to be climbed up to at the end.

**Advisory**
- **Sheltering under a tree in rain (l.286).** There is no thunder (l.257), the rain is soft, and the decision is the adults' and the whole community's, so it is factually safe. Given 024's clear "never under a tree in a thunderstorm" message, and 028 r1's N-1, one adult clause would remove any mixed message: Buck, "No thunder in it. If there were, we'd all be indoors." Optional.
- **Lanterns hung on the hickory's branches (l.335).** The grown-ups light and hang them, so Rule 7 is met, and the tree is wet. This is fine. Consider "on the lowest branches, well clear of the leaves", or on hooks on the porch posts.
- **Rusty's promise to find a "shortcut to the edge" (l.298).** It is balanced by Rusty's father's promise to take anyone next summer (l.310). Optionally make Rusty's "…and show your father first".
- **Charlie's tears (l.191–199).** These are brief, held kindly (Benny kneels to her height), and resolved with a promise and with being carried. Appropriate.
- **Buck's reverie, "some of them had come back" (l.331).** It implies that others did not. It is a faint shadow for a young audience at bedtime (§15, no unnecessary sadness), and it is recommended for cutting under L1-1.

**Wellbeing and tone**
- **The finale's bittersweet register is well judged.** Wonder at the edge, a pang at Charlie's question, comfort in the promises, then cool rain and calm. It matches the Bible's guidance for the brothers' arc ("wonder shot through with a gentle, bittersweet ache", bible l.3482) without any departure.
- **No predator/prey content.** The hawk's feather is a found object identified by Maverick, with no predation implied or suggested. The beetle is admired, not caught. There is no menace, sarcasm or put-down. The "No," said everybody joke about the drum is affectionate, and Barney takes it cheerfully.
- **The close is calm.** Soft mist, a chattering creek and a drifting leaf. There is no jolt. With N-4 and L1-2 it will be both safe and fresh.
