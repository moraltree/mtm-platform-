## Headline

This is a warm, generous, well-populated story. All eleven characters do real work, and the "telling the bees" custom is a lovely and authentic invention. The Ch9 round of news is the best ensemble set piece of the spring run. It still **does not pass round 1**, for these reasons:

1. **A family and age error that breaks locked canon.**
   - l.188 calls the miller the miller's wife's *son*, and has Buck say "Your grandmother, lad".
   - l.304 calls the trowel the miller's "mother's".
   - Both clash with l.322 ("lifted his wife") and with 021 l.348 ("The miller had told his wife").
   - l.334 has the miller's wife telling the bees about Buck "when you were small". That makes her older than Buck, but Canon 2 makes Buck "the oldest resident". It also cannot be squared with l.184 ("since I was smaller than Charlie") or l.60 (her mother died when she was a young wife).
2. **Safety breaches under rule 7.**
   - A youngster (Brody) carries a full water butt (l.126).
   - Sadie Belle is taught to prune, which implies using blades (l.225).
   - The children's speaking line sits on the hub paving, where the bees fly in and out (l.252).
   - Two youngsters walk home in the dark with no grown-up (l.338).
3. **Factual errors.**
   - Roses do not fold up at night (l.340).
   - Marrows cannot grow "almost as big as Brody" in ten days (l.219).
4. **Wrong credit for things that happened in earlier stories.**
   - The plain-flower insight belonged to Reba in 019, not Brody (l.219).
   - Beau saw the chestnut shoots in 017, not Rusty (l.263).
   - The woodchuck "stayed on after the Blossom Gathering", but 021 l.5 has the visitors from over Weather Ridge going home.
5. **Variety.** This is the third time Buck forgets something and then remembers it (006 l.356 "I remember now"; 017; 022 l.198 "I'd forgotten all about it. Until this minute."). The closing image of stones in starlight also echoes 006's close.

All of these can be fixed with light edits. The structure does not need to change.

### Weaknesses
- **Families and ages do not add up** (l.184, 188, 198, 304, 334). This is a story about generations, and the generations are wrong. It is the main reason the score is held below the pass mark (see Moneypenny).
- **There is little tension and no real obstacle.** After permission is given in Ch2, nothing pushes back. Ch4 and Ch7 are lists of separate scenes rather than escalation. The turning point (the gums in Ch5 and the custom in Ch6) is a reveal, not something the children achieve. That is acceptable for a gentle close to spring, but under rule 4 it is weak.
- **There is no comedy in Ch2, and the comedy in Ch3–4 does not build.** The vignettes sit side by side (the fountain and bucket, Reba testing, Beau's nettles) instead of building to one staged payoff. Ch2 is all tender sadness.
- **Sadness is dense.** Characters cry or wipe their eyes four times (l.58, 200, 202, 248). The mother's death ("passed on") is mentioned five times (l.33, 60, 202 twice, 246). The weeping apology to the bees (l.246) is a third tearful beat for the miller's wife. Rule 4 says guilt and sadness sections stay short, and these accumulate.
- **Buck forgets and remembers for the third time** (006, 017, 022). The miller's wife's "every year I said, next year" (l.60) repeats the guilt beat of Buck's "I never came back to see" in 017 and Granny Opal's "I meant to" in 011. The founder's variety flag (third forgotten-tradition story) is borne out. In fact this is the fourth, because 020's "great planting" was also a forgotten tradition revived.
- **Rule 1 is borderline.** Charlie's whisper "This is everybody's now." (l.289) comes directly before Buck's wisdom (l.297) and states the "shared place" moral in a character's mouth. The wisdom then reads as Buck's reply to it. The miller's wife's "Then it's yours as much as mine" (l.76) does the same more lightly.
- **A thread is left hanging.** At l.171 the miller says "I'm no beekeeper. But I know who was." It is never paid off. The sweet smell "Charlie did not know the name of" (l.11) is also never named.
- **The ending repeats familiar beats.** Families going home with lanterns, "one by one" (l.322), is the departure beat of 005, 017, 019 and 021. "The wheel of stepping stones showed pale in the starlight… forgotten years" (l.340) echoes 006's last image ("seven white stones… faintly glimmering under the stars like a path that somebody had remembered").

### "Sherlock" (natural history and facts)

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL FAIL. The core bee material is accurate. Three minor errors must be fixed.**

| Check | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Bee gums** | ✓ Accurate. These are hollow sections of black gum (*Nyssa sylvatica*) stood on end on flat stones, with a board laid on top as a lid and a notch cut at the base as the entrance. This is the traditional Southern Appalachian hive, used "before there were boxes" (l.159, 165). Calling them "tall" is fine at small-animal scale; real gums were about 2–3 ft. |
| **Swarming season** | ✓ The main swarming season is roughly April to June. A wild swarm taking over a long-empty cavity with old comb traces is classic scout behaviour. "This spring, I'd say. Or last." (l.165) is a plausible estimate. |
| **Telling the bees** | ✓ This is a real custom in Britain, New England and Appalachia. The hive is told of births, marriages and especially deaths, and if it is not told the bees may leave or die (l.192). The "old folk said" framing keeps it as folklore ✓. **Advisory:** at l.202 the miller's wife states as fact that when nobody told the bees, "they went". For small children, soften the cause: "Whether it was that, or just that nobody was minding them, they went." The regular Sunday-evening community telling is invented (N4). |
| **Bees fanning at night** | ✓ Fanning goes on through the night to evaporate water from nectar and to ventilate the hive. Saying some fan while "the rest sleep" (l.312) is a fair simplification; bee sleep is documented. A soft hum from inside the hive at night ✓. |
| **Bee behaviour at dusk** | ✓ Foragers come home before dark, heavily laden, and walk in at the entrance (l.236, 306). Lanterns set "well away from the bees" ✓, since lights near hives at night can draw bees out. Bees do not fly in the dark, so the evening crowd is low risk once they are in, **but the crowd at l.252 is gathered while foragers are still landing.** See S3. |
| **Protective veils** | ✓ A broad-brimmed hat with a fine net veil, long gloves, and slow, unhurried movement (l.147, 153). The gums are never opened and there is no honey-taking, so no smoker is needed ✓. |
| **Herbs and old roses in June** | ✓ Mint, lemon balm and chives all survive neglect and are in growth and flower in June ✓. Thyme surviving years under brambles is less likely, because it is a sun-loving subshrub that gets shaded out. Acceptable, but it could be "a few straggles of thyme" (advisory). Old once-flowering roses, such as a small, flat, frilly crimson gallica type, bloom in June ✓. A white rose still in bud is plausible ✓. Sweet peas being tied in June ✓. Buttercups and honeysuckle ✓. |
| **Frogspawn in June** | **Flag, but not strictly wrong.** In Appalachia, gray treefrogs, green frogs and bullfrogs breed from May into summer, and gray treefrogs readily lay in rain barrels. So spawn in a water butt in June is plausible. British readers, however, associate frogspawn with early spring, and by June **tadpoles** are more likely and less ambiguous. Tipping them into "the ditch" (l.126) is poor practice, because June ditches dry out. Fix: "tadpoles", carried by a grown-up to the marsh or the Beaver Ponds. This combines with S1. |
| **Roses "folded themselves up for the night"** (l.340) | **✗ Wrong.** Roses do not close at night. (The bloodroot and hepatica closing in 016 was correct; this borrows that beat wrongly.) Replace it. Honeysuckle smelling sweetest after dark is true, because it is moth-pollinated. |
| **Marrows "grew… almost as big as Brody"** (l.219) | **✗** It is impossible for marrows planted in mid-June to fruit within ten days. Change to "which would grow, Barney said, almost as big as Brody". |
| **Onions in June** (l.221) | **Minor ✗.** Onion sets go in in early spring; set in mid-June they would not bulb well. Change to **leeks**, which are transplanted in June and set deep in holes. The bath image can be kept: "just their green tops peeping out, like heads in a bath". |
| **Beans, lettuces, strawberry plants in June** | ✓ Acceptable. Lettuces may bolt in the heat, which does not matter here. |
| **Nettles** | ✓ Stinging nettle stings bare paws (l.140) ✓. Optional: adding a dock leaf would fit the Hollow's folk feel. |
| **Wheel or spoke garden layout seen from the air** | ✓ Plausible. Eight spokes make eight beds ✓, and the counts are consistent throughout. Five gums, four empty and one busy, is consistent at l.159–163, 202, 210, 236 and 342 ✓. |
| **Sunset behind Weather Ridge** (l.302) | ✓ Weather Ridge is to the west. |
| **"Midsummer had come early"** (l.223) | ✓ Mid-June is just before the solstice, and Midsummer Music Evening is a canon festival. This does not call the slot summer. |

## LOCK THREE — "Dr Noura" (child development and safeguarding) and narration readiness

**Verdict: CONDITIONAL FAIL. Four safety fixes and one emotional-load fix are required.**

**What is done well**
- **Permission and property** are model behaviour for 3–7-year-olds. They ask the owner first, they get her blessing, and they make an explicit promise about the bees (l.39, 66–78). The founder's moral and safeguarding flag is fully met.
- **Bees are presented with respect and without fear.** "Bees don't like being surprised. Any more than you do." (l.153) is excellent, developmentally apt guidance. Grown-ups handle the bees in protective gear, and the youngsters stay back at the gate (l.147). No sting is ever threatened.
- **Blades:** "nobody else was allowed to cut brambles, or to touch anything with a blade" (l.85) ✓.
- **Climbing the gate:** when Charlie climbs the gate bars, an older friend lifts her down at once and holds her paw (l.149) ✓. A gentle, realistic model.
- **Intergenerational warmth.** Granny Opal's "and that she was staying" (l.271) and Reba's grandmother sharing her recipe knowledge are lovely belonging beats (Canon 4).
- No lies, no meanness, and the humour is never at anyone's expense. Beau's nettles are self-inflicted and treated kindly.

**Required fixes**
- **S1. l.126: Brody carries the water butt and tips it out.** This breaks rule 7 (adults handle water tubs). A full butt is also far too heavy for a cub. Fix: Brody spots the tadpoles, and the miller or Brody's father carries the butt (or scoops the tadpoles into a bucket) down to the marsh or the Beaver Ponds while Brody walks alongside. Brody can keep a carrying job by moving the fallen wall stones *with* a grown-up.
- **S2. l.225: the woodchuck "showed Sadie Belle how to prune the old roses".** Pruning means blades, which contradicts l.85. Fix: "showed Sadie Belle which stems to cut, and did the snipping herself, while Sadie Belle gathered up the trimmings and tied the long stems back to the wall".
- **S3. l.236–252 and l.308: the children's line.** The line is drawn "on the stones" of the hub, and foragers are still landing at that moment (l.236). Small children are standing close to an active entrance and possibly in the flight path. Fix: say the line was drawn "on the path, well back from the gums and off to one side, out of the bees' way home" (l.252). At l.308 Charlie's "nearest stepping stone… as close as the rule allowed" should be "on the line, as close as the rule allowed". Optionally, hold the telling until "the last of the bees were nearly all in".
- **S4. l.338: Charlie walks home "with Maggie Mae, last of all"** with only Maggie Mae's lantern. Both are youngsters, and it is dark. Fix: add a grown-up, for example "with Maggie Mae and Charlie's mother", or Maggie Mae's neighbours from 019. Their walk "along the edge of the Hickory Woods" (l.322) then remains supervised.
- **E1. Emotional load** (see R3). The death of a parent is the backbone of the telling-the-bees custom. It is handled warmly, but at present it is *dwelt in*: four tearful beats, five "passed on", a weeping apology to the bees, and a stated cause-and-effect ("nobody told the bees. And they went.") that a sensitive child could turn into worry or guilt ("if I forget to tell, things leave"). Trim it as in R3, and soften l.202 as in Sherlock. Bible §15: sorrow "never dwelt in".

**Advisory**
- Reba eating plants from someone else's garden (l.130–134) is fine, because the owner has given her blessing and Maggie Mae checks her. It remains consistent with Reba's v1.1 arc of "learns to respect others' belongings".
- Optionally add that the youngsters wore gloves or kept to cleared ground near the nettles (l.118 already has adults clearing ahead of them ✓).

### Narration readiness
- **Overall: READY after the fixes above.** The voice is warm and even, with read-aloud rhythm in the Ch9 list of news. Dialogue is clearly attributed and names are easy to say. The comic timing works on the page: "Ow," quietly, at intervals; "Onion Twelve and Onion Thirteen"; "Softly," which was nearly as loud. The pace slows properly through Ch10 into a sleepy last line.
- **Split l.219** (about 240 words in a single paragraph) into two or three paragraphs: the families' beds, then Granny Opal. As written it is a breath-test for narrators.
- l.236 is also long; one break after "…all facing the middle" would help.
- Pronunciation and term notes for the narrator: "billhook"; "bee gums" (say it as one phrase); "lemon balm".
- l.188 "Old Mother..." trails off. Give the narrator a clear pause cue, or a name once M1 is resolved.
- No sound effects or songs that need direction.
