## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.1 / 10 — NOT YET PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This is a warm, well-made story with the best central image of the season so far: snowflakes carried on chilled black velvet to a woman who cannot go out to them. It matches the brief closely. The failure is honest and physical (a thaw, not a villain). Benny's role is exactly what the brief asks for: he notices, asks the right question and supplies the method. Barney's idea is his own, and it comes from a real turning point.

It is held below 8.5 by five things:
- **The theme is spelled out by the narrator and characters** several times before Buck speaks (house rule 1).
- **The surprise has a logic hole.** The palace is built in broad daylight, all afternoon, in the Hollow's "front parlour", with "everybody out in the snow anyway" (l.64). Yet "nobody else knows" and "Not one creature… had seen" it (l.168). Barney's original plan, to build it "tonight, while everybody's asleep" (l.37), is quietly dropped.
- **The sulk runs long** (end of Ch4 to the middle of Ch6, about 1,200 words). Ch7 is a list of job assignments rather than scenes.
- **Stock-phrase and simile slips** (house rule 6).
- **A handful of continuity and fact errors** (see Lock Two). The worst is "three winters", which contradicts S01-007.

### Strengths

- **Ch1 opening (l.3–31).** "Barney woke up because the light was wrong" is a superb first line. The hush, the steaming creek and the sugar-dry snow ground the listener in winter exactly as v1.0 §10 asks.
- **Ch2 comedy is on stage and escalates.** The snowball that shrinks to an acorn and vanishes (l.78–82), then Brody's exploding armful (l.92). This is real physical comedy, and it teaches the physics without a lecture. Charlie falling off Maggie Mae's head is a good topper.
- **Ch3's palace is glorious and comically imperfect.** "Leaned like a tired heron", the moat that is "mostly just a ditch", the staircase to nowhere, Maverick's "It looks like a cake from up here!" (l.127–133).
- **The Ch4 aftermath image** (statues melting at different speeds; snow Buck's head in snow Maggie Mae's lap) is funny rather than sad, so the disappointment is held lightly.
- **The turning point is earned and dramatised.** Barney looks at the miller's wife and her words come back to him (l.246–252). His first objection ("They'd melt") lets Benny contribute the method. This is the brief working exactly.
- **The ensemble does real work** (house rule 3): Maggie Mae's velvet boards, Charlie's no-breathing rule, Sadie Belle's open-meadow advice, Rusty's tested shortcut (a nice reversal of his shortcut gag), Beau's smooth carry, Brody's slip-proof path, Reba's warm drink, Maverick's snow lookout. Every job fits the character.
- **Ch8 snowflake scene.** It is quiet, precise and beautiful. The forms are named in child-friendly similes (feathers, fir trees, spoons, a window). The melting is gentle, and "Her cheeks had gone pink, and not from the cold" is a good line.
- **The palace heap becomes a sledging slope for the littlest ones (Ch9).** This is the moral shown with no words: the grand thing, once humbled, becomes a small kindness. It is the best idea in the second half.
- **Mountain Wisdom #12** ("The world gets a little warmer every time somebody chooses to be gentle") is quoted exactly with no summarising lead-in. It resonates with the operation itself, which is all about gentleness: no breathing, smooth carrying, quick hands. It has not been used in S01-001 to 008.
- **The close is staged freshly** at the mill step and on the walk home rather than on Buck's porch, which the founder default allows. The two lines of pawprints ("one neat and straight, one wandering", l.438) and "No palaces… But maybe a very small heap" are lovely brother beats.

### Weaknesses

1. **The theme is stated repeatedly before Buck (house rule 1).**
   - l.238: Benny's speech, "it was mostly amazing for us… said ooh, and gone home. And then it would have melted anyway", argues the lesson out loud.
   - l.274: the narrator: "It was not a palace. It was not big. It was not the biggest surprise… It was a few snowflakes on a bit of cloth."
   - l.289: "the brothers found that even a very small surprise needed a great many friends."
   - l.387–393: "She looks happier than anybody would have looked at the palace." / "And it was only snowflakes." / "Only snowflakes." This is the moral in dialogue, two beats before Buck.

   Benny's two questions ("Who was it for?" / "Did anybody need a palace?", l.225–229) are enough. The rest should go or be shown.
2. **The logic of the surprise (Ch1–Ch4).**
   - l.37: Barney plans to build alone at night while everybody sleeps. Benny agrees, and the plan then silently becomes a daytime group build. This is a story-logic problem and a safeguarding problem (see Lock Three).
   - l.70 / l.168: the palace is built all day in Fern Meadow in front of a valley that is "out in the snow anyway". "Nobody else knows" and "nobody saw it" are not credible unless the text gives a reason. For example, the grown-ups are asked not to look until morning, or the build is in a hidden corner of the meadow.
3. **The disappointment section runs long (house rule 4).** Barney's sulk spans the end of Ch4 (l.166–184), Ch5's silence (l.193–221) and Ch6's argument (l.236–244). Ch5 has no comic beat at all. The guilt and sadness should stay short.
4. **Ch7 is a catalogue** of nine one-paragraph job assignments (l.291–325), and it echoes Ch2's roll-call catalogue (l.66). Only Maverick's beat gets a real exchange. Dramatise at least two jobs, with a small complication or joke, rather than reporting them.
5. **Stock phrases and similes (house rule 6).**
   - "the way X does" similes: three, against a limit of one. l.258 "the way it did when an idea was arriving"; l.264 "the way he did when a problem was interesting"; l.416 "the way it had been on the first morning".
   - Near-variants of banned phrases: l.31 "his eyes shining" (banned: "eyes shone"); l.315 "Reba's face lit with joy" (close to "lit up like a lamp"); l.31 "the biggest idea he had ever had" (close in spirit to "biggest grin").
   - "long moment" ×3 (l.211, 280, 356), plus "in silence" (l.244) and "a long time" (l.248).
6. **l.96: "Still meant to do that"** is said by Beau. "Meant to do that" was Maverick's line (l.68), so "Still" makes no sense from Beau. Either give the line back to Maverick or rewrite Beau's.
7. **The arc echoes S01-003.** Maggie Mae's "Magnificent Autumn Party" collapses, a small thoughtful gesture wins, and the moral is "how you made them feel". The founder brief mandates this shape, so this is not a fault of the draft. But the author should lean on what is distinct here: *who a gift is for* (an audience of one versus "everybody"), and the snow science. Avoid any phrasing that recalls 003.
8. **Close echoes (house rule 5).**
   - l.416 "the first stars showed" sits very close to the banned "first star over Sunrise Ridge", and S01-006 already used "the first stars came out".
   - The final sweep "all the way across the sleeping valley" (l.460) repeats a device used in 001, 004 and 005 ("the whole sleeping valley", 004).
   - "Benny and Barney went last" echoes 002's "The brothers were the last to go".
9. **Minor craft points.**
   - l.407: "the best thing that had ever happened to her" overstates.
   - The little rabbits appear in Ch9 without having been present at the mill. Seat them on the step in l.381.
   - l.121: Benny "stacked" snowballs taller than Brody, which a young beaver cannot lift. Give Brody the lifting, or use a ramp of packed snow.

### Facts ("Sherlock")

**Snow physics.**
- *Dry, very cold snow will not pack* (Ch1–2). **Correct.** Powder that "ran through his fingers like sugar" after a very cold night; the snowball crumbles and shrinks as it rolls; the squeezed armful bursts. Benny's "Dry snow won't pack… It needs to be a bit warmer, so it's a bit wetter" (l.98) is right.
- *Near-freezing wet snow packs* (Ch3). **Plausible, under-explained.** A low December sun warming "the top of the snow just enough" can make a surface pack. But the balls are rolled "down to the grass", so they pick up the deeper, colder powder too. One phrase saying the air itself softened towards freezing would make it sound (recommended).
- *A warm thaw slumps snow* (Ch4). **Correct.** A warm, damp south wind; dripping; slumped walls; towers rounded to lumps; statues melting at different rates; a full, muddy creek.
- **F1 — "It's too wet now. It's the opposite of yesterday" (l.180) is muddled.** Near-freezing wet snow is the *best* packing snow; that is how Ch3 worked. What fails in a thaw is snow that is waterlogged, above freezing and still melting: slush. Yesterday morning's snow was too dry, and yesterday afternoon's was ideal. Reword, for example: "It's slush now. It's full of water and it's still melting. It slumps faster than you can pile it." (Required.)
- The creek "steaming a little in the cold" (l.21) is **correct**: steam fog over open water on a very cold morning.

**Snowflake facts.**
- Six-fold symmetry (l.207, 342, 358) is **correct.** The forms are correct and well chosen: "arms like feathers" and "little fir trees" (stellar and fernlike dendrites), "tiny plates on the ends, like spoons" (stellars with plate-tipped arms), "a plain, small, perfect hexagon, like a window" (a hexagonal plate). "A little star in the middle of a bigger star" and "like lace" (l.370) are also good.
- Chilled dark cloth (black velvet on a board, chilled an hour or more), not breathing on it, and working quickly are **all correct technique.** Melting at the edges into droplets from warmth and breath (l.364) is **correct.**
- **F2 — The "arms" contradiction.**
  - l.358: "four tiny perfect stars of ice. Each one had six arms… And one was just a plain, small, perfect hexagon". A hexagonal plate has six *sides*, not arms, and is not a star.
  - l.207: "Every one's a little star. Six arms, every one" overstates. Needles and columns also fall, and they are not stars.
  - Fix: "Four tiny crystals of ice, every one six-sided…", and let the hexagon be the one without arms. Keep "every one's a little star" as the miller's wife's affectionate memory if desired, but the narrator must not confirm it. "No two the same" is acceptable as her folk saying. (Required.)
- **F3 — "Big soft flakes… the very best kind of snow" (l.338).** Large, soft flakes in still, near-freezing air are usually *aggregates*: clumps of many crystals. These are poor for seeing single, perfect stars. The finest single crystals tend to come in colder, lighter snowfall, with small separate flakes drifting down. Either make it "small, separate flakes, drifting straight down in the cold, still air", or keep the big flakes and let the friends pick out single crystals among the clumps. Sadie Belle's still-day, open-meadow advice remains correct. (Required, as a one-line fix.)
- Seeing six arms "even without the glass" (l.342) is plausible for larger stellar dendrites (2–5 mm) on black velvet. Fine.
- **Ch10 (l.442):** examining a flake on his arm by holding it "up to the lantern-light ahead" while the lantern has gone on to the bridge. Seeing six arms in distant lantern light at night is implausible. Keep the lantern close; see S1, which fixes both.

**Magnifying glass.** A framed hand lens used for fine carving is **correct and well handled.** It is held over the specimen and looked through (l.354), it enlarges without touching, and the weather is overcast, so there is no question of focusing sunlight. Buck's "mind you don't drop it" and the return of the lens are good. Small point: Buck says "It goes back on its hook when you're done" (l.301) but then comes to collect it himself (l.414). Fine as kindness, but a half-line ("and to see the youngsters home") would tidy it.

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura" (safeguarding and bedtime suitability) and narration readiness

### Result: PASS WITH NOTES (conditional on S1–S4)

There is nothing frightening, cruel or harsh. The disappointment is gentle and resolved through action, and the bedtime descent works. The notes concern supervision near water and at dusk, one unaddressed night-time plan, and the handling of the miller's wife.

### Cold and wet children

Barney is soaked with "cold and red" paws (l.174). Benny brings him straight to the nearest warm place, where an adult provides a fire, a blanket and hot milk (l.184–191). This is good modelling. On Day 3 the miller's wife is wrapped in shawls, a blanket and a hat; everyone has a hot drink; and Buck brings the friends home with a lantern. Children falling into powder drifts (Charlie, Beau, Maverick) is brief and comic; Charlie is dug out at once.

### The creek in thaw

Silver Creek is "fast and full and muddy" (l.156) and the waterwheel turns in a "swollen creek" (l.189). No child goes near the water, which is good. But two points need care:
- Rusty's shortcut goes "without going round by the bridge" (l.305). A listener could read this as crossing the swollen creek another way. Make clear that it stays on the meadow side, for example "along the meadow edge, without needing to cross the creek at all".
- **S1 — The walk home.** Benny and Barney stop behind on "the path by the creek" in the dark while "the lantern went on ahead of them and waited at the bridge" (l.450). Two young children dropping out of the lantern's circle beside a creek in thaw, at night, falls short of house rule 7. Have Buck stop with them and hold the lantern close, which also fixes the flake-viewing implausibility (Lock Two). (Required.)

### Barney's night-time plan

**S2.** Barney announces he will build the palace "Tonight, while everybody's asleep" (l.37), alone in Fern Meadow. Benny agrees, and the plan is never revisited. Children going out alone at night should not be modelled unchallenged (house rule 7). Remove it, or let Benny steer it to daylight on the page (see R2). (Required.)

### Sledging at dusk

**S3.** "Before it got dark, while the grown-ups were still on the mill step," Barney, Charlie and the little rabbits go out to the meadow "beyond the willows" (l.397–407). That is possibly out of sight of the adults, at dusk. The slope itself is fine: "Not too steep. Not too high". Put a grown-up in view, for example the rabbits' father or mother going with them, or the heap visible from the step, and keep it short so it ends before dark. (Required.)

### Moving the miller's wife's armchair outside

The move is adult-led: the miller wraps her up and carries the chair with Brody (l.377), she stays under the eaves out of the snow, and she is helped back inside afterwards (l.434). The intention is good. However:

**S4.** Carrying an armchair *with her sitting in it* risks jolting a back the story says is bad. It also puts a load-bearing lift on a cub. Better modelling: she walks the few steps on the miller's arm (and her stick, from 007), while the miller and Brody carry the *empty* chair and the cushion behind her back. Or let a second grown-up help. (Required; a one-sentence change.)

Opening the window in winter for an elderly person is short and supervised by the miller, who holds it (l.348), with her wrapped in her shawl. That is fine.

### The magnifier

The lens is borrowed from an adult with clear care instructions, used under supervision, in overcast weather (no sun-focusing hazard), and returned. No concerns.

### Barney's disappointment and its resolution

The thaw is handled kindly: comic slumped statues, no blame, and Benny's gentle "Come and get warm". Barney's crossness at l.221 and l.236 is real and age-true, and his voice-raising is checked by the miller's glance, which is nicely done. The resolution comes through Barney's own choice ("Do you want to do it?" "Yes") and through action rather than consolation. The palace heap reborn as a sledging slope gives the failure a happy afterlife, so no child is left feeling the effort was wasted. The section is a little long (see R3) but never bleak.

### Moral shown, not preached

The story *shows* the moral superbly: the snowflake delivery, the miller's wife's pink cheeks, the heap reused for the smallest. It undercuts this by also *saying* it (l.238, 274, 289, 387–393; see R1). Buck's wisdom lands cleanly with no sermon.

### Wind-down and bedtime suitability

- **Energy curve:** high in Ch1–3, a dip in Ch4–6, rising to quiet wonder in Ch8. A brief squeal of sledging in Ch9, then a deliberate slowing in Ch10: snow stopped, still white valley, soft voices, flakes "one at a time", "Goodnight, Hickory Hollow."
