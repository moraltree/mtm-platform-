## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 7.9 / 10 — NOT YET PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This draft has the strongest emotional spine of the winter stories so far. A proud young eagle secretly cannot see the faint stars he is famous for showing. That is a real, child-sized secret, and his confession and his request for help are dramatised with genuine tenderness. Charlie's "At least you didn't say tomorrow" (l.58) is the best single line of the season. Rusty's "the Fidgets" (l.243) is delightful, and the two-voice star-guiding in Ch8–10 is a lovely image of shared competence.

Five problems hold the score down:
- **The central craft mechanic is recycled from S01-008, two stories ago.** In both stories the friends build a lantern from willow and paper in a kitchen, in secret from one character. Everybody contributes a job, there is a reveal, Benny bends the willow frame, and Maverick paints stars on deep blue in 008. The comedy of nearly giving away the secret also recurs (Charlie being sent on errands, l.313).
- **Maverick's crash-landing gag is lifted from S01-009.** There he sinks "in up to his white head" with "Meant to do that". Here he sinks "up to his neck" (l.129) with "That was on purpose" (l.107).
- **The moral is spelled out before Buck speaks** (house rule 1). Buck also delivers mid-story wisdom (l.78), and the narrator labels Maverick's growth twice.
- **Chapters 4–6 run long and sad, and the comedy in Ch2–4 is summarised rather than staged** (house rule 4).
- **The two biggest fact errors damage the climax** (see Lock Two). On the night the sky clears, a nearly full moon would be up beside the Pleiades. The Ladle (Big Dipper) would be lying on, or below, the northern horizon of a valley ringed by ridges.

### Strengths

- **Ch1 premise.** Star Night, the Star Guide and the settler names (Porch Star, Little Lanterns, Three Stepping Stones) build a believable, timeless folk custom in a few paragraphs. "So that they would have friends in the dark" (l.9) is lovely.
- **Ch2, Charlie at the green door (l.42–58).** Maverick chooses not to say "tomorrow" a fifth time. This is the brief's "accepting uncertainty" shown, not told, and it is the emotional turning point that makes everything after it possible.
- **The cloud flight (Ch3) is a mistake with a clear cause.** Benny's warning ("like being inside a bowl of porridge… how will you know which way is down?", l.101) and Sadie Belle's "Have you asked Grandpappy Buck?" (l.109) set it up fairly. Maverick's refusal ("I don't need to ask anybody", l.113) is exactly the flaw the saying answers. The literal flight makes Mountain Wisdom #77 land with real resonance.
- **Benny telling a grown-up (l.140)** is good safeguarding, modelled without fuss.
- **The confession (l.166–174).** The fact that eagles have poor night vision becomes a character secret. "I've always just remembered where they are, from my mother showing me" is touching and specific.
- **Rusty's reaction (l.235–247).** He does not mock, and he turns out to need Maverick's names. The help runs both ways, which keeps Maverick's dignity and fits Canon 4: difference is a distinction, not a deficit.
- **The children solve the problem** (house rule 2, largely). Sadie Belle asks the key question (l.189). Benny invents the projector by looking at the tin lantern (l.270–276). Maverick and Rusty split the sky between them. Buck only lights the lamp.
- **Ch8 reveal.** The stars appear "on the ceiling and the walls and the faces of everybody sitting there" (l.346), a beautiful image. Charlie counting six on her paws, and Maverick's "Then you can see better than me", are well judged.
- **Ch9 has a real payoff.** The real Little Lanterns follow the paper ones, and Maverick still sees only a smudge but does not mind. The flaw is accepted, not magically cured. That is honest and mature.
- **The closing exchange is warm and funny:** "Even if the old owl's a fox?" / "Especially then" (l.438–442).
- **The close is staged at night under real stars rather than at dusk**, with each friend's small discovery. The final Porch Star image ties back to l.9 ("find your way home"), so the ending feels earned rather than generic.

### Weaknesses

1. **Recycled mechanic from S01-008 (Ch6–8).** See above. Within one season, two group-built secret lanterns from paper and willow, revealed to an audience, will read as formula to a listener who hears the stories in order. The projector idea is fresh. What is recycled is the build-and-reveal staging.
2. **Recycled crash gag from S01-009 (Ch3, l.105–107, l.129).**
3. **The moral is stated before the saying (house rule 1).**
   - Buck, l.78: "There's a way to wait. That's about all there is, with clouds." This is mid-story wisdom, against Canon 2 ("his wisdom comes only as gentle Mountain Wisdom at a story's close").
   - Sadie Belle, l.185, and Maverick, l.264, both say "Nobody can" move the clouds. The theme is stated twice in near-identical words.
   - Maverick, l.430: "when I asked, it turned out somebody else knew the bit I didn't." This summarises the lesson immediately before the saying.
   - Buck's lead-in, l.434: "a young beaver who wanted to go everywhere and ask nobody." This paraphrases the moral in front of the saying.
   - The narrator labels the growth twice: "he said it without minding at all" (l.368) and "found that he did not mind one bit" (l.413).
4. **The comedy in Ch2–4 is summarised, not on stage (house rule 4).** The staring and the wing-flapping wind (l.66–68) are one-sentence summaries ("On the fifth day…"). The only staged comic beats are the recycled thistles and haystack. Reba's washing blowing away has comic potential and is thrown away in a clause.
5. **The sad stretch is long.** From l.138 (frightened youngsters, Charlie in tears) through the uneaten soup and the confession to "miserably" and "hung his head" (l.174), and on to Charlie's disappointment (l.258), there are roughly 1,300 words of low mood with little lift. The Fidgets moment (l.243–249) is the only relief.
6. **The resolution of the "waiting" half is luck.** The clouds simply clear in Ch9. That is acceptable, since waiting is the point, but the story never shows Maverick choosing to wait well. The lantern is a workaround, not patience. One beat where Maverick decides not to fly, or not to fret, on a later grey evening would make "when to wait" an act rather than an accident.
7. **The brief and house rule 2.** The brief says Maverick "learns from Buck". As written, the asking-for-help lesson comes from Sadie Belle (l.185–189) and the waiting lesson comes from Buck's mid-story line (l.78), which breaches Canon 2. The best reconciliation is to keep Buck's teaching to: (a) the firm, loving boundary in Ch4, which keeps things safe; (b) at most one question; and (c) the closing saying. Cut l.78's aphorism and replace it with one question. For example, "And if you knew, young Maverick, what would you do different tonight?" lets Maverick reach "nothing" himself. This keeps Buck principal without letting him supply the reasoning. Flag this reading to the founder.
8. **Buck is thin for a named principal.** He has about eight short appearances. The suggested question in Ch2 and a warmer beat with Maverick after the scolding in Ch4 (one or two lines of Buck sitting with him, not a lecture) would help.
9. **Some of the ensemble is decorative (house rule 3 is met, but only just).** Benny, Sadie Belle, Brody and Rusty do real work. Barney (a jibe and "What?"), Reba (washing and biscuits) and Beau (carrying on a pole) barely affect the plot. Maggie Mae fetches paper.
10. **Internal contradictions in the time markers.** Maverick laughs "for the first time in nearly a fortnight" on day 9 (l.249), then "smiled for the first time in ten days" on day 10 (l.284). Day 9 is not "nearly a fortnight". Ch6's "Every evening Maverick and Rusty met" (l.256) covers only one evening before "the tenth evening".
11. **Stock phrases and slips (house rule 6).**
    - "Charlie's face, lit up in the starlight" (l.413) is near-banned ("lit up like a lamp").
    - "now his eyes were bright" (l.276) is the "eyes shone" family.
    - "eyes as wide as saucers" (l.334) is a cliché.
    - "Nobody said a word" (l.387) is a variant of the banned "Nobody said anything".
    - "did not say anything" appears at l.162 and l.179.
    - "for a long time" appears five times (l.72, 131, 162, 211, plus "a long moment" and "a long while"), which leans on "long silence".
    - "He did not like it. He did not like it at all" is repeated at l.60 and l.201.
    - Only one true "the way X does" simile (l.383), which is within the limit.
12. **Brody "thinking, slowly, the way Brody thought about most things" (l.179)** is not v1.1 canon (warm-hearted, friendly, gentle) and could read as a gentle put-down. Make it "carefully".

### Facts ("Sherlock")

**F1 The moon. Must fix. This is the key error.** Star Night is defined as the darkest sky, just after new moon. The clear night arrives about 12 to 13 days later (see the timeline). By then the moon is waxing gibbous, about 90–95% lit. It rises in mid-afternoon and is high in the eastern and south-eastern sky all evening. In December it sits in or near Taurus, close to the Pleiades. In reality it would drown out the Little Lanterns' faint members, Orion's sword and most faint stars. The "so full of stars that hardly any dark was left" sky (l.391) could not happen.

Fixes, in order of preference:
- (a) Drop "after the new moon". Define Star Night as "the first clear, moonless evening of deep winter". Start the story with the moon waning, after last quarter, when it rises after midnight, so that two weeks of cloud end near new moon.
- (b) Keep the new-moon trigger but shorten the delay to four or five days. The plot cannot easily absorb this.
- (c) Mention the moon honestly. This undermines the climax.

**F2 The Ladle (Big Dipper). Must fix.** In mid-December at about 8 pm, at a Southern Appalachian latitude of about 35.6°N, the Dipper lies along the northern horizon. Calculated positions for that latitude: Dubhe about 12° altitude, Merak about 7°, Megrez about 4°, Phecda about 2°, Alkaid about 5° below the horizon. From a valley ringed by ridges it would be largely or wholly hidden. It cannot be pointed out from the porch at l.397 or found by Benny "twice" at l.446.

Fixes:
- Swap in **Cassiopeia**, which is high in the north in December evenings and circles the Porch Star. Its traditional figure is a chair, so an in-world name such as "the Rocking Chair" would be charming. Confirm with the founder because it is new canon.
- Or keep the Ladle on the projector only. Let the real-sky scene say it is "tucked down behind the ridge this time of year, and comes up later in the night". This is a nice teaching touch.

Also, "those seven, round it" (l.301, l.354) wrongly suggests the Dipper encircles Polaris. Make it "nearby, wheeling slowly round it all night".

**F3 Polaris.** It sits in the north and appears not to move ✓ (it moves less than a degree; fine for children). "That bright one in the north" (l.243): Polaris is only moderately bright (about magnitude 2), but it stands alone in a sparse patch, so "all on its own" (l.352) is apt. Change "bright" to "steady" (which also suits "Old Steady"). The idea of "find your way home by it" is folk usage. It gives the north, not home. That is acceptable as lore. See S6 for the safety framing.

**F4 Orion (the Three Stepping Stones).** It rises in the east in December evenings ✓. Mid-December at about 8 pm, the Belt is about 20° up in the east-south-east, so "rising over Sunrise Ridge" works if the ridge is not too high. It is not yet risen at dusk (it rises about 6:30 pm), so make sure the real-sky scene is mid-evening, not dusk. "Three bright stars in a perfect row" ✓ (nearly evenly spaced, nearly straight). Betelgeuse and Rigel "above and below" ✓.

**The sword:** three faint stars hanging from the Belt ✓. The middle "star" is actually the Orion Nebula, a soft fuzzy patch, which suits Rusty's "look a bit to the side". When Orion is rising, the sword lies to the right of, and slightly below, the Belt, not straight "under" it (l.446). Use "hanging from the Stepping Stones".

**F5 The Pleiades (Little Lanterns).** Well placed ✓. About 58° up in the east, almost directly above the Belt and slightly north (left as you face east), so "up and to the left of the Stepping Stones" (l.405) is correct ✓. Six visible to most eyes, seven on good nights ✓. "Tiny, misty cluster" ✓. This only holds once F1 is fixed.

**F6 Averted vision.** Correct ✓. The fovea has no rods, so faint objects show better slightly off-centre. Accurate for humans and other mammals. On a lamp-projected ceiling (l.360) it is harmless but less necessary. Keep it.

**F7 Fox night vision.** Foxes have a tapetum lucidum, a reflective layer behind the retina ✓. That causes eyeshine. Red fox eyeshine is usually reported as green-white to yellowish, so "little green moons" is acceptable ✓. Note that eyeshine is seen by an observer near the light source looking towards the eye. With the porch lantern turned low, "catching the faint glow" (l.403) is plausible. The tapetum improves sensitivity in dim light at some cost to sharpness. Fox acuity is lower than a human's, but resolving the brighter Pleiades members (separated by tens of arcminutes) is plausible, so the claim holds. "A moth on a tree trunk by starlight" is a boast; fine.

**F8 Bald eagle low-light vision.** Diurnal raptor, cone-rich retina, exceptional daylight acuity ✓. Sensitivity in deep darkness is relatively poor; owls are the opposite. "Made for the light" (l.174) is fair, and so is Buck's "Eagles are made for the daytime" (l.154). The claim that he cannot resolve the Pleiades in true dark is plausible fiction, not established fact. Phrase it as Maverick's experience, as it already is. Dusk and dim light are not darkness (see C1).

**F9 The pinhole star lantern. Must fix the design.**
- The principle is sound ✓. A light at the centre of a sphere projects holes onto the walls in the same arrangement as the sky, and bigger holes make bigger, brighter dots ✓. Buck's punched-tin lantern is the right real-world inspiration (traditional pierced-tin lanterns do throw speckle patterns).
- Optics: a flame 1–2 cm across inside a sphere about 25 cm across throws soft blobs a few centimetres wide on walls 1–2 m away, not sharp "points". Projections are also dim on a "dark wooden ceiling" (l.346). Suggest a small, low room or pale plaster, and soften "points" to "soft little stars".
- Material: "thick, dark-blue paper" must be fully opaque. Otherwise the whole ball glows blue and washes out the dots.
- **Safety and plausibility (see S1):** an oil lamp with a glass chimney cannot sit safely in a "cloth flap" inside a closed paper ball hung from a ceiling hook.
