## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.1 / 10. NOT PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This is a warm, clever, well-shaped story with the best natural-history spine of the spring block. The pie with no filling (l.158), the violets heap (Ch5) and the spicebush egg-laying (Ch8) are excellent. It meets both CANON flags: Maggie Mae is the gardener, and Reba is the curious watcher rather than the expert.

It falls short for six reasons:
- the calendar is broken: Ch8's "first day of June" cannot follow the days the text has already counted;
- Brody relearns the "don't dig it up to look" lesson that is the heart of his own story in 014, and an older friend has to stop him again;
- the close is a "wait till July" ending of the kind reviewers asked to retire, it reuses the season's "gold to rose to … blue" sequence for the third time, and it ends on small creatures quietly working on leaves at evening, as 018 did;
- the guilt beat is played three times;
- Maggie Mae gets a mini-moral speech;
- the ensemble outside the leads and Maggie Mae is mostly decoration.

### Strengths

- **The concept dramatises the moral, not just states it.** Brody's love is real but uninformed. Every one of his "beautiful" choices does harm: the doubles have no filling, he pulls the violets, and he trims the spicebush. Each repair comes from finding out. "Caring requires understanding" is shown and never stated.
- **Reba's method is the engine, exactly as the founder asked.** "Where do they go?" (l.90–94) is a perfect child-sized scientific question. Following the azure (l.101) and the paper-bag log (l.123–140) are canon-true: the cinnamon bag and the pie recipe she "knew … off by heart anyway" keep her food-first.
- **The Ch2 comedy escalates on stage.** The misspelt sign is followed by Benny's tactful silence (l.52), then the butterfly house with Charlie's cushion and a lone beetle (l.56–58), then Barney's scarf parade with the bee cloud and the pratfall into the doubles (l.60–68). "That's bees sorted, anyway" is a good laugh.
- **The pie analogy (l.158–168) is the story's best page.** It is accurate, food-flavoured, in Reba's voice, and it returns as a refrain ("with filling", l.176, l.394).
- **The turning point is genuine and earned by Brody.** The swallowtail tastes leaves with her feet and curls her abdomen under to lay (l.324). Brody finds the egg because he has learned to watch (l.328–334). The callback "That I nearly pulled out" lands, and "Only because it was too stuck" (l.266) is a lovely honest laugh.
- **Brody's own discovery of the moths (l.384–388)** shows he has learned the method rather than just being taught the answers. He writes it down and gives it to Reba, which is a good reversal.
- **The book gets the timescale right in spirit.** The rebuilt garden is "mostly green" (l.351), and the first real success comes through a host plant, not a nectar plant. That is the correct and honest answer to the founder's timescale flag.
- **Buck's visit (Ch9) is well judged.** He asks one question, the wisdom is bare, and the azure on his cap is a gentle grace note.

### Weaknesses

1. **The day count is impossible (major; house rule 9).**
   - Ch1 is "the last week of May" (l.3).
   - Ch2 counts five days (l.38–70). The scarf day ("The day after that", l.60) is itself day 5, and l.70 then says "On the fifth day" as though it were a new day.

   - l.197 says the weeds were thrown "five days before". It is at least eight to nine days by then.
2. **Brody's 014 lesson is recycled and undone (major; continuity and character).**
   - In 014 (l.249–253, l.368), Brody's signature story is the hickory nut he wanted to dig up "every day" and did not, because Buck said "give it time."

   - This replays the same beat with an older stopper, and it ignores the best available callback. Brody himself should remember the hickory and stop, with his claw in the air. That is also better for house rule 2.
3. **The close is a waiting ending with recycled pieces (major; house rule 5 and the reviewers' request).**
   - "Maggie Mae says July … I'll be here" (l.392–398) and "getting ready for summer" (l.400) make the final beat about a payoff that has not yet happened. This is close kin to the "waiting for their turn / next year" endings reviewers asked S01 to avoid.

   - "I made a garden full of pies with no filling … I didn't ask" (l.166–172).

5. **Maggie Mae delivers the moral early, and the drafting slips at l.289–291 (moderate; house rule 1).**
   - Two consecutive paragraphs are both attributed to "said Maggie Mae" with no beat between them, which is a drafting glitch.
   - The second, "A garden isn't a cake … Some things take the whole summer", is an aphorism from a non-Buck character that competes with the wisdom.

6. **The ensemble is thin beyond the leads and Maggie Mae (moderate; house rule 3).**
   - Maggie Mae does real, plot-moving work (the violets and the spicebush), and so does Charlie (the caterpillar sort and the watering, l.230–232).
   - Sadie Belle gets one clause (l.279).
   - Benny repaints the sign (l.301), which is a lovely gesture but does not move the plot, and lends a ruler.
   - Barney's two set pieces are comic failures. The scarf incident does reveal that bees follow scent, but Reba's smell reasoning (l.164) cites the Mill honeysuckle instead of it. That is a wasted setup and payoff: make Barney's scarves Reba's evidence.
7. **The comedy fades after Ch2 (minor; house rule 4).** Ch3–4 are charming but quiet. One staged comic beat in Ch3 or Ch4 would keep the escalation running, for example Brody's size against the creeping and following, or the recipe log.
8. **Stale locution (minor).** "Brody … did not get up for quite a while" (l.334) and "as still as a stone" (l.5, l.324) are fine once. The second "still as a stone" should be varied.

### "Sherlock", natural history and physics

**Verdict: NOT PASSED. Six required fixes.** The core science is good. Some timing and behaviour details are wrong in ways that a sharp parent or a nature-loving child would notice.

#### Correct and well handled

- **Double flowers (l.144–162).** In fully double cultivars, stamens and often nectaries are converted to petals, and the reproductive parts are buried. Pollinators largely ignore them, while single, open flowers (wild rose, blackberry, clover) are well used. Correct.
- **Pink five-petalled wild rose** (Rosa carolina type) flowering in late May to June: correct.
- **Blackberry blossom, clover, bumblebees** in late May: correct.
- **Tiger swallowtail in April** (l.5): the first brood flies from late March or April in the southern Appalachians. Yellow with black stripes and tails is correct. Landing on a nose is plausible, because they seek salts.
- **Spring or summer azure** (l.101–105): a small pale-blue butterfly nectaring on small white clustered flowers is correct. By late May this is strictly the summer azure, but "small pale-blue" stays generic, which is fine.
- **Spicebush swallowtail** (l.115, l.254, l.318–330):
  - black with blue dusting on the hindwings and pale marginal spots: correct;
  - tasting leaves with its feet (tarsal chemoreceptors): correct, and a lovely detail;
  - single eggs on the leaf undersides, pale greenish, spherical, about 1 mm: correct;
  - hatching about a week later: correct;
  - early instars that look like bird droppings: correct;
  - the crushed twig smelling spicy, lemony and peppery: correct.
- **Great spangled fritillary on violets** (l.203–207):
  - violet specialists: correct;
  - dark, spiny caterpillars with orange-based black spines: broadly correct;
  - the mother lays near violets: correct;
  - a brown, leaf-like chrysalis: correct.
- **Moths and pale, night-scented flowers** (l.384): correct in principle.
- **Bee balm, milkweed and coneflowers** as summer nectar plants flowering in July and August (l.281): correct for established plants.

#### Required fixes

- **S-1. Fritillary life-cycle timing (l.207, l.314, l.380, l.392–394).**
  - Great spangled fritillary caterpillars hatch in late summer and overwinter unfed. They feed on violets in spring and are near full size by late May. They pupate in late May to June, and the pupal stage lasts about two to three weeks.
  - Caterpillars that pupate in early to mid June should therefore **emerge by late June**, not "July". In the southern Appalachians, males are typically on the wing from early to mid June.
  - Fix l.392–394 so they emerge in the last days of June. That also gives the fresh close (L1-3).
  - Also, a caterpillar described as "about as long as Reba's claw" (l.203) in late May is too small. Say "as long as Reba's finger".
  - Optional: say that the babies hatch in late summer and wait out the winter before the violets come up. That is true and child-friendly.
- **S-2. Survival on the heap (l.197–236).** With the timeline fixed (M-1), the heap should be days, not more than a week, old. "dried-up" leaves (l.197) are also inconsistent with violets that "perked up" by morning (l.236). Make the heap "wilted" in a shaded spot, as l.203 and l.221 already say.
- **S-3. Spicebush swallowtail caterpillars at the end of June (l.400).**
  - Eggs laid in mid-June hatch about a week later. By 30 June the caterpillars are roughly in their second or third instar, still bird-dropping-like and still small. That works if the egg date moves to mid-June per M-1.
  - If the date stays early, by 30 June they would be in the fourth instar: green, with big false eyespots.
  - **"a row of" caterpillars is wrong** either way. They are solitary: one per leaf, each hiding by day in a leaf folded over with silk, and feeding mostly at night. Fix: "here and there, each in its own folded leaf".
  - Optional and very child-friendly: one caterpillar has already turned green "with two big pretend eyes". Do not mention why the eyes are there; the Absolute Prohibitions forbid predator references.
- **S-4. The swallowtail lays at dawn (l.312–324).** Butterflies need to warm up, and swallowtails lay eggs in warm sunshine from mid-morning to the afternoon, not "so early that the dew was still on the grass". Move the visit to mid-morning, after breakfast, with the dew gone.
- **S-5. The red trumpets and bees (l.21, l.164).**
  - Glossy, red, tubular, unscented flowers are typically **hummingbird flowers**, full of nectar. They are not "no filling". Bees see red poorly, which is a better reason than smell for bees to ignore them.
  - Reba's hedged "bees go by smell, I think. Partly." is honest, but the honeysuckle example is weak. Honeysuckle tubes are long, and bumblebees often rob them from the side.
  - Fix: Reba sees a hummingbird at the red trumpets, which is a lovely gentle surprise and stops them being lumped with the doubles, and Brody keeps them. Or use Barney's scarves as the scent evidence (L1-6).
  - The ruby-throated hummingbird is a new species detail, so list it for the founder if used.
- **S-6. Violets "closing up" in the evening at the end of June (l.384).** Common violets finish flowering by late spring. By late June there are no open violet flowers, and violets do not close nightly anyway. Cut this, or replace it with something true, such as evening primrose opening, which is pale yellow and opens at dusk.

#### Should fix

- **Seedling timescale (l.279–281).** Bee balm and coneflowers from small seedlings often do not flower in their first summer, and common milkweed raised from seed usually takes two or three years. The founder flagged this. Make Maggie Mae's plants divisions "from her own old clumps", which will flower in July and August. Let Maggie Mae say the milkweed may take longer. Put that inside the body, as part of the patience thread, not as a closing promise.
- **Chimney swifts (l.384).** "screaming" is the European swift's call and a harsh bedtime word. Chimney swifts twitter and chitter.
- **Fritillary pupation site (l.314, l.353, l.380).** Pupation usually happens low down among leaves and litter, often in a loose shelter of leaves, rather than hanging neatly from a violet leaf. "hanging … among the violet leaves, low down" is close enough.
- **Spicebush hosts (l.254).** "only lays its eggs on spicebush" is nearly true: sassafras is the other main host. "almost only" or "spicebush, or its cousin sassafras" is precise.
- **Spicebush habitat (l.9, l.15).** Spicebush is a shade-tolerant woodland-edge shrub. The garden is "sunny" but at the meadow's edge by the woods, which is just about acceptable. One phrase such as "in the shade of the woods' edge" would help.
- **Peonies (l.21).** The pom-poms read as double peonies, which are correct for late May. Peonies resent being moved in bloom, but this is a picture-book liberty and needs no change.
- **Bees following scarves (l.64).** Bees investigating a strong floral scent is plausible, but a bee "cloud" chasing a running child is comic licence. It is acceptable because the bees are framed as curious and not cross, and they leave quickly (l.66).

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding, bedtime suitability and narration readiness

**Verdict: PASSED, with three required fixes.**

#### Safeguarding

- **Blades (required; house rule 7).** Brody "trimmed it very hard, until it was a neat round shape" (l.15). This implies a child using shears or a blade. Make it his father who trims it at Brody's request, or have Brody snap the twigs off by paw. The paw option is less neat; the father option keeps the pudding shape.
- **Walking home in the dark (required; house rule 7).** Reba crosses the meadow at dusk (l.390), and the story ends as the light goes (l.400). Her den is "round the edge of the Hickory Woods" (l.336). Add one line saying Brody's father, or Reba's father, walks her home with a lantern, or that she is collected.
- **Caterpillar handling (required, small).** Some spiny caterpillars sting, though fritillaries do not, and children copy what they read. The text mostly models good practice ("lifted very gently on its leaf", l.230). Make l.236 match: set down "on their leaves", not by paw. Do not show bare-paw handling of spiny caterpillars anywhere.
- **Bees.** They are framed as gentle and curious, and nobody is stung. Barney running from them is played for comedy and resolves calmly. Acceptable.
- **Unsupervised work.** Brody's garden is behind his own den, the evening work includes Maggie Mae, and Brody's dawn run is in daylight. Acceptable.

#### Bedtime suitability

### Summary

"Brody and the Butterfly Garden" has an excellent premise, executed with real natural-history intelligence. The double-flower "pie with no filling", the violets heap and the spicebush egg-laying are strong, and it meets both CANON flags: Maggie Mae is the gardener, and Reba learns by watching. Mountain Wisdom #62 is word-perfect, unused elsewhere in S01 and delivered bare.

It does not pass round 1. Lock One scores 8.1 against a pass mark of 8.5. The main reasons:
- the calendar is impossible ("first day of June", l.312);
- it recycles Brody's 014 "don't dig it up" lesson;
- the close waits on July and repeats the gold/rose/blue sequence and 018's insects-at-evening image;
- the guilt is played three times;
- the ensemble is thin.

Sherlock found fixable timing and behaviour errors: fritillaries should emerge in late June, spicebush caterpillars are solitary and not in "a row", egg-laying does not happen at dawn, red trumpets are hummingbird flowers, and violets are not flowering in late June. Dr Noura passes the story, subject to three small fixes: a child trimming with blades, the walk home in the dark, and caterpillar handling. There are 22 required revisions plus founder advisories.
