## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.2 / 10. NOT PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

The core of this story is very good. A small number of fixable faults keep it under the mark: one structural, one in the close, and several in continuity.

### Strengths

- **The conceit fits the moral exactly.** Buck loses his voice and so has to listen (l.3–51). The storyteller of the Hollow becomes its audience, and "I AM LISTENING" (l.323) carries the moral "every voice deserves to be heard" without anybody saying it. The early slate business is funny and in character: "Where?" (l.15), and Buck underlining EVERY twice (l.45).
- **The payoff lands.** Chapter Ten, "The Croak", has Buck force out the saying in "a voice that was hardly more than a creak" (l.438). The Mountain Wisdom costs him something to say, which is the best delivery of a saying so far this season. The slate gag that follows ("NOW I'M GOING BACK TO BEING QUIET", l.442) undercuts the moment gently instead of moralising.
- **Three deep arcs, each true to character, as the CROWDING flag asks.**
  - Benny the measurer finds a story inside his numbers. Reba's "a number with a story inside it… like a cupboard" (l.204) ties the unlock to Reba's own curiosity about closed things (l.168).
  - Brody, the slow and gentle one, tries to bolt a dragon onto his story (l.137–147), then tells the true one about a tree he grew from a nut. "He told it as the tree had grown, a little at a time" (l.372) is the best sentence in the manuscript.
  - Sadie Belle's stage fright is solved by Charlie, using the stillness she learned with Sadie Belle in 013 (l.306, l.393–395). The mentoring is returned, it is earned continuity, and the child solves it.
- **The ensemble does real work (rule 3).**
  - Maggie Mae convenes the group, runs the practice, notices Sadie Belle and does not push her (l.80–90, l.282).
  - Reba unlocks Benny.
  - Barney unlocks Brody.
  - Charlie supplies the solution for Sadie Belle.
  - Rusty, Beau and Maverick stay light, as the brief intends. Maverick's two-hour saga ("which there were none of", l.68) is a good running gag.
- **VARIETY against 024 is handled.** This story is about true stories found in one's own life, performed indoors at a winter gathering. 024 is an imaginative game about cloud shapes. The two premises are distinct. (A note for 024: avoid restaging "telling a story in front of everybody".)
- **The Chapter Three practice is a real staged comic escalation.** Barney falls off the footstool, Benny's snow statistics send the row to sleep, and Brody's dragon and giant collapse (l.109–149). It then turns on Sadie Belle's silence (l.155–161).
- **Brody's closing exchange is tender and unforced.** He asks "Did you know?" and Buck pats his paw like a proud tree-trunk (l.448–452).

### Weaknesses

1. **The same unlock scene appears three times.**
   - Ch4, Ch5 and Ch6 run the same pattern: a friend alone and down on themselves says "it's only X / nothing happens", and a friend reframes it.
     - Benny: "It's a number" / "That's a story" (l.200–204).
     - Brody: "That's all… Nothing happens" / "That's not nothing happening" (l.251–255).
     - Sadie Belle: "It's not exciting. It's only a walk" / "It doesn't sound like only a walk" (l.286–288).
   - Sadie Belle's line is almost a copy of Brody's worry. Her real barrier is the faces, as l.86 and l.298 establish, not the story's content.
   - The repetition blunts the third unlock, and the third should be the strongest.
2. **Chapter Seven is a catalogue.** Seven stories are summarised in seven consecutive paragraphs (l.327–339). Each is charming, but the run becomes a list. It is followed by a second roll-call of all ten in l.436, and Chapter Two has a smaller catalogue (l.60–70). Barney's millpond story is also told in full twice (l.111 and l.327).
3. **The close recycles the season's own image.**
   - l.456 ends on footprints in snow, "straight ones and wobbly ones, going every which way, and all of them going home."
   - 009 ends on "three lines of pawprints in the new snow: one slow, one straight, and one wandering happily from side to side, all the way home".
   - 012 ends on marks in the snow at the bottom of the hill, "big ones and middle-sized ones… and one very small one".
   - That would make three winter stories in six closing on tracks in snow. The straight/wobbly contrast is lifted almost directly from 009, which breaks rule 5 in spirit.
   - Sadie Belle's grandmother's line (l.407) is lovely and should stay in Ch9. The final image needs to be something else.
4. **The "hush" beat is overused.**
   - "For a moment, nobody in the Mill made any sound at all" (l.360).
   - "The Mill was so quiet that you could hear the fire" (l.405).
   - "For a moment, nobody moved" (l.413).
   - Buck "wrote nothing on his slate at all" (l.378), which echoes 010's "did not say anything at all".
   - These are four variants of the banned "Nobody said anything" beat. Keep one.
5. **The saying has a lead-in (rule 1).** "Ten stories. Not one of them like any other." (l.436) comes immediately before #20 and paraphrases it.
6. **There are continuity slips** (details under Lock Two).
   - Benny and Barney's separate doors break the central device of Benny's arc (l.184, l.350).
   - "You came second in the leaping" (l.64) contradicts 012.
   - "The twins" (l.153) are not in the cast.
7. **Minor points.**
   - Maverick "tucked his beak under his wing" (l.123) reuses a banned close image, although mid-story.
   - "the most curious creature in the Hollow" (l.168) is verbatim from 007 l.23.
   - "you could have posted a letter in any of them" (l.446) introduces a postal system and is slightly off-world.
   - "BEST STORY NIGHT EVER" (l.442) reads a touch modern.
   - The Ch2 title "Ten Kinds of Worried" does not match the chapter: seven of the ten are confident in it.

**Required fixes**

- **M1. Benny and Barney share a lodge (blocking).**
  - Benny counts "steps. From my door to Barney's door. I count them every morning, when I go round" (l.184) and "from my door to my brother's door" (l.350).
  - Established canon: they live together. See 002 l.532 ("watched until their lodge door closed behind them"), 009 l.5 ("the brothers' lodge") and 009 l.432 ("their lodge was furthest"). Both also have one mother (013 l.397, l.438).
  - This is the central device of Benny's arc, so the fix needs a re-anchor, not a word swap.
  - The cleanest option: Benny counts his steps from the brothers' lodge door to a fixed morning destination, such as Buck's porch or the Old Mill steps.
  - In the fever week he went from the lodge by Fern Meadow for flowers and back again before setting out, which makes the count much larger.
  - Barney's hat and birthday entries then work the same way. On the birthday he "ran home" with a present, or ran to fetch Barney's surprise.
  - Adjust l.184, l.188, l.196, l.350, l.354 and l.358 together.
- **M2. Beau did not come second.**
  - "You came second in the leaping," said Rusty (l.64).
  - In 012, Rusty won the snow leap and Beau's nose-first leap "doesn't count" (012 l.196, l.202, l.532). No second place was ever recorded.
  - l.329 in this manuscript repeats the correct version ("how it had not counted").
  - Fix l.64, for example: "Your leap didn't count. You landed on your nose." Beau's reply in l.66 can stand.
- **M3. "The twins" (l.153).** There are no twins in the cast. Benny and Barney are elder and younger brothers (Bible §Benny), and Barney is already named in the same list. Delete "and the twins", or replace it with "and Benny" or "and Charlie".
- **M4. Benny's bramble entry contradicts the text and 012.**
  - Ch4 gives "four hundred and fifteen" as just chasing Barney's hat (l.206).
  - Ch8 expands it into "into the bramble patch, and Rusty showed me a shortcut, and we came out at the beaver ponds" (l.354). That is Rusty's famous story from 012 l.294, which was a group trip to the Old Mill, and Rusty tells it himself three paragraphs earlier in this story (l.331).
  - A detour through brambles to the beaver ponds would also add far more than three steps.
  - Restore the simple hat chase in l.354, and let Rusty keep his story.
- **M5. Maggie Mae's party details (l.337).** 003 has the risen cake, autumn leaves, roses, ladybirds and the teacup tower. There is no bunting. Replace "bunting" with "ladybirds" (the funniest true detail) or "leaves".
- **M6. Story Night and the Longest Night.**
  - The Bible's Longest Night (§ festivals) already ends with "Buck's old voice telling the oldest, warmest story of all", on his porch in December.
  - This manuscript invents a second annual Buck storytelling custom (the "Winter Tale" at the Old Mill) on "the last Saturday in January, when the nights are longest" (l.23).
  - It can coexist, and late January is a sensible place for a new custom. But "when the nights are longest" directly collides with the canon festival name and is also factually wrong (S1).
  - Reword to "when the nights are long and cold and the snow lies deepest". The founder must confirm Story Night as a new custom (N1 below).

  - Not an error, but a small character inconsistency.
  - His "nine inches, and a half" on 15 December should also not exceed 013's storm, which he called "the most I've ever measured". It does not, as long as the 013 storm came later, which it appears to have.
- **M8 (advisory). Buck "had never once in all those years asked what had become of it" (l.378).** In a small valley, with Brody visiting the tree constantly behind the Mill, Buck not knowing is a stretch. The l.452 ambiguity, that he knew without asking, is the better reading. Consider "had never once said a word about it" so l.378 and l.452 agree.

### "Sherlock", facts and plausibility: PASS WITH NOTES

- **S1. Late January does not have the longest nights (l.23).** The longest night is the December solstice. By the last Saturday in January, Appalachian nights are roughly 40–50 minutes shorter. "Coldest" and "snow deepest" are fair for late January. Fix as in M6.
- **S2. The hickory grows far too fast (l.227 against l.247 and l.251).**
  - Brody planted the nut "when I was very little. Littler than Charlie", which also reads oddly as a size comparison between a bear and a chipmunk. Make it "younger than Charlie is now".
  - So the tree is only a few years old. Hickories are notoriously slow: a seedling spends its early years building a taproot, and reaching about a metre in five years is typical.
  - "About as tall as Buck's lodge" (l.227) is implausible. It also conflicts with "taller than me now" (l.251, l.374), which implies a small tree.
  - Cut the lodge comparison. Keep the tree just taller than Brody, with a "straight young trunk no thicker than Charlie" or similar.
  - The rest of the hickory botany is accurate: an autumn-planted nut, nothing all spring because of slow emergence after the taproot, a shoot "at the very beginning of summer" (late but plausible), and bare winter branches. "Slow growing" even reinforces Brody's theme.
- **S3. The 906 steps arithmetic.**
  - Benny's premise is that the count varies daily with mud, snow and haste (l.188).
  - Reba sees a single "nine hundred and six" (l.178). Benny says it was "every morning for a week" (l.196, l.358).
  - Seven identical counts contradict his premise, and the notebook would show seven big entries, not one.
  - Fix: Reba sees "a whole row of big numbers… nine hundred and six, nine hundred and one, nine hundred and nine", and Benny names the biggest.
  - "Twice as many" for 906 against 412 is fine as a child's estimate.
  - "Ran, so fewer steps" (402 on the birthday) is correct, because running strides are longer.
- **S4. Deer and snow (l.403).** "Let the snow hold you up" is wrong for deer. They have high foot-loading and sink in deep snow unless it is crusted, and they cope by high-stepping and by using each other's trails. Keep "lift your hoof high, put it down slowly" and replace the third instruction, for example "and step where I step". If that changes the "side by side" image, the footprints can be side by side on the way back.
- **S5. Honey for a sore throat, and resting the voice (l.19, l.319).** Sound folk remedies. Fine.
- **S6. The mill wheel at night (l.456).** A wheel "turning steadily in the black creek" after the mill has shut, in deep January, is plausible if the race is unfrozen and the wheel is left to turn. Acceptable. Optionally make it "standing still under its icicles".
- **S7. Plausibility of "every youngster" (l.35, l.56).** The Hollow has other youngsters, such as the three littlest rabbits (010, 012), but only the ten friends tell stories. Either make Buck's slate say "EVERY ONE OF YOU" to the friends, or add one clause that the littlest ones did a song.

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding and bedtime suitability: PASS WITH NOTES (required changes below)

**Overall suitability.**
- The emotional temperature is excellent for bedtime. Worry, never fear. Every arc resolves warmly.
- Buck's illness is reassured immediately ("NOTHING WORSE", l.19) and resolves comically.
- Sadie Belle's freeze at the practice (l.155–161) is handled kindly, without anyone laughing or remarking on it. Maggie Mae models patient support that does not push: "did not tell her that she could do it. She did not tell her not to be silly" (l.88). That is strong safeguarding practice for anxious children.
- The considered fib is rejected (l.278).
- The ending is calm, slow and sleepy.

**Required changes**

- **N1. Fire safety (rule 7).** "Maverick stood up on the end of the hearth, spread his great brown wings" (l.333), beside a fire the miller "had built… up so high" (l.319). That is a child with spread feathers on a hearth beside a high fire. Move him to "stood up on a flour sack" or "on the miller's bench".
- **N2. Out alone at dusk (rule 7).**
  - Maggie Mae walks alone to Sadie Belle's house and home "through the dusk with her lantern" (l.272, l.294). Charlie has been waiting alone on Maggie Mae's doorstep and, as the youngest, presumably goes home in the dark (l.294–312).
  - Fix: Sadie Belle's mother walks Maggie Mae home with her lantern. At the end of Ch6, add one clause in which Charlie's mother arrives to collect her, or Maggie Mae walks her home to the beech.
- **N3. Icicles (rule 7: adults handle ice and roofs).** "Longest icicle: one foot, three inches, measured with permission from the miller" (l.119) implies a child measuring icicles hanging from the Mill. Make it "measured by the miller, with his long pole, while I wrote it down", or a fallen icicle.
- **N4. Laughing at Brody (Bible §15: no humour at a character's expense).**
  - The group laughs at Brody's collapsing dragon story, and he laughs "only on the outside" (l.149). Showing the hurt honestly is good, but it is never repaired.
  - Fix: Barney's scene in Ch5 (l.231) should open with a short, real sorry for the snort. This also helps L1-1 by varying the scene's engine.
- **N5 (advisory). Water.** Barney's millpond fall is a comic water accident retold three times (l.60, l.111, l.327). It is past-tense and he was clearly fine, but one clause would help. For example: "on a hot summer's day, at the shallow edge, and the miller fished him out with a rake handle before he'd even got his ears wet". This keeps it from modelling deep water as a joke.
- **N6 (advisory). Illness.** "Barney had the fever" (l.196, l.358) sounds more serious than it needs to. "had a bad cold" or "a chill" keeps the week in bed without alarming a listening child.
- **N7 (advisory). Reba's father named as the culprit in front of the whole Mill (l.335).** It is gentle, he takes it with good humour, and the Mill "roared" with him, so it is acceptable. If anything is changed, have him confess cheerfully before she points, so it stays laughter with him rather than at him.

### Narration readiness: READY WITH NOTES

- **Slate text convention.** Buck's slate lines are in capitals (l.13, l.19, l.35, l.45 "EVERY", l.323, l.333, l.442). So is Charlie's shouting (l.49). A narrator, or text-to-speech, will read both the same way, either flat or shouted.
  - Adopt one convention for written slate text, such as small caps or italic, and add a production note: "read in a slow, written-down voice".
  - Keep capitals only for Charlie's shouting, or render her shout in lower case with "shouted".
- **Buck's croaked line (l.438).** This needs a performance note (hoarse, very quiet) and must not be processed or volume-normalised up. The scene depends on the Mill straining to hear.
- **Benny's statistics (l.119).** The numbers are written out in words, which is good. "nine inches, and a half" is a pleasing rhythm. The list is long enough to land the joke. Do not cut it, but pace it with small pauses.
- **Proper nouns** are all easy to pronounce. Dialogue attribution is clear throughout. The three-simile opening (l.5: saw, bullfrog, gate) reads aloud very well.
- **"cattle grid"** (l.432) is fine in British English. US narration may prefer "cattle guard". No change required.
