## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.0 / 10. NOT PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This is some of the most assured prose of the season. The structure is clean: four map symbols, four true discoveries, an empty X and then the shoots. The title reversal ("led nowhere" → "HERE") is earned. The chestnut reveal is moving and true. It still falls short of the mark for five reasons:
- the close repeats, almost line for line, the "keep it for the next one" ending that 014, 015 and 016 have just used, and the brief warned against exactly this;
- the theme is signposted repeatedly before Buck speaks;
- Rusty relearns a lesson he already owns in 004, 007 and 012, through a stuck-in-a-thicket shortcut gag the season has used four times;
- Benny's compass, the story's one map-reading tool, does nothing;
- the ensemble is two real contributors plus decoration.

### Strengths

- **The structure is the moral.** Each symbol is decoded by a real, sensory discovery: the frog (l.127–139), the wavy lines and dot (l.211–213), the tree with a face (l.266–270), the three hills (l.279). The joke that "I drew the things I liked big" (l.66) pays off beautifully as "I drew it big because I loved it" (l.137). Map scale is taught as character, which is very good secondary-theme work.
- **The turning point is genuine and comes from a child.** Rusty's deflation at the stump is brief (l.342–344). Beau's slow looking, inherited from Sadie Belle in 012 (l.88, l.252, l.351), finds the shoots (l.359). The arc of Beau as the patient looker is consistent and satisfying.
- **The laurel set piece (Ch4) is well staged.** It escalates through hedge, sideways, uphill, under, over and a nose flick (l.178), then lands two reveals: Buck eating an oatcake at the bottom (l.182) and "came home without a hat. That's why the line goes round" (l.200). This is the funniest page in the story.
- **The view-as-map image (l.293–295) is lovely**, and the "HERE" map (l.404–412) is a beautiful, visual, narratable payoff.
- **The wisdom fits.** #39 suits the journey half of the moral precisely, and Buck delivers it bare (l.387).
- **Reba's note (l.236) is a perfect canon-true laugh:** "I WILL NOT EAT YOUR SHARE IF IT IS FOOD."
- **The day markers are clean:** end of April (l.3), "the rest of the week" (l.60), Saturday at the beginning of May (l.58), "The next morning" (l.398). The spring setting sits correctly after 016 (March).

### Weaknesses

1. **The close is recycled, and the brief warned against this specific beat (major).**
   - Buck files both maps in the drawer "So the next youngster who clears out this desk finds both" (l.416–418). This is the fourth "kept for whoever comes next" close in a row:
     - 014: the slate hung by the Mill door "until next winter";
     - 015: "So the next miller knows who to thank. And so does the one after that.", whose sentence shape is nearly identical;
     - 016: "Next year: bring Sadie Belle."
   - "Rusty went home the long way, by the creek, without hurrying" (l.422) is 004's penultimate beat: "Rusty went home the proper way, along the path, with no shortcuts at all."
   - The final image is a living thing unfolding "as if they had all the time in the world" (l.422). It mirrors 016's last line, where the seeds wait "for their turn".
   - The last paragraph is a sunny morning, which is a weak sleep cue (the same note 016 received).
   - "The first stars came out over Weather Ridge" (l.394) is a near-variant of the banned "first star over Sunrise Ridge" and of 006's "the first stars came out above the Hollow".
2. **The theme is told outside Buck's line (rule 1).**
   - The narrator signposts Rusty's change four times: "Rusty did not hurry them once" (l.264), "for the first time all day, he did not say it in a hurry" (l.281), "Rusty did not suggest a shortcut. Not once." (l.283) and "They could see the whole of the day … like a map" (l.295).
   - Rusty's recap montage (l.379) and "I don't think this led nowhere" (l.383) directly precede the wisdom. Together they form exactly the summarising lead-in the rule forbids.
   - Buck states the perseverance half himself: "And they're still trying. Still sending up new shoots, every spring. Still growing." (l.369).
3. **Rusty's arc is a regression, and the shortcut gag is a retread.**
   - Rusty already explains why shortcuts trap you in 004 ("the kind that looks quick, but then you're stuck in the briars"), muses on it in 007 (l.123) and tells "the famous bramble-patch shortcut" against himself in 012 and 014. Shortcut jokes also recur in 001, 002, 003, 009 and 016.
   - Rusty is canonically the shortcut fox, so the habit is fine. But "Straight up is quicker. Everybody knows that" (l.170), followed by getting stuck in a thicket, is the same joke, and the "slow down" lesson is one he has already learned on page.
   - l.98 ("He had found a great many shortcuts … Some of them had even worked") restates 007 l.123 almost exactly.
   - 004 is also a mistaken "treasure trail" story for Rusty ("I thought it was a treasure trail", 004).
4. **The compass is inert, and map reading stops at symbols.**
   - Benny's north insight and compass (l.62) are set up with a promise to return it, but nothing pays off:
     - Rusty "looked at the compass" (l.113, l.160), yet with no north on the map a compass reading cannot tell him that "the X is that way" (l.162);
     - the compass is never used to orient anything, never decides anything, and is never returned.
   - The secondary theme covers symbols and scale well. Orientation, the one thing set up explicitly, is dropped.
5. **The ensemble is thin (rule 3).**
   - Sadie Belle (advice that finds the blazes and the shoots) and Maverick (identifying the three hills, l.244, and fetching adults, l.392) do real work.
   - Benny's contribution is inert (W4).
   - Reba's is a funny note with no payoff: nobody ever tells her what the treasure was.
   - Maverick's canonical swagger is mostly absent. "None of them were looking at me" (l.244) is the one flash of it.
6. **Comedy is light in Ch2–3.** Ch2 has good small verbal gags ("Obviously", l.82; "very large tents", l.84), but there is no staged comic action until Ch4. Ch3 plays Rusty's dismissiveness ("That's nice", l.145) straight. Rule 4 asks for escalation across Ch2–4, and this escalation happens inside Ch4 only.
7. **Verbal tics:**
   - "slowly" appears 16 times;
   - "voice had gone small/rough" and "had gone very soft" appear three times (l.320, l.332, l.365);
   - "for the first time all morning/day" appears twice (l.227, l.281);
   - all-caps inscriptions appear four times (l.21, l.236, l.404, l.412). This joins the slate and card capitals of 014 and 015 as a season-wide tic.
8. **The relic overlap with 006 is real.** 006 is a line of forgotten old markers, rediscovered, that leads to a remembered secret ("like a path that somebody had remembered"). 017's forgotten blazed path (l.256–264) leading to a remembered tree is the same shape. The VARIETY flag stands. The map, peepers, spring and chestnut give 017 enough of its own identity to survive, but only if the blazes are not also the climax device. Here they are not, which is good.

### "Sherlock", natural history and physics

**Verdict: PASS WITH NOTES** (conditional on S-1 to S-4; S-5 to S-8 recommended).

**Verified correct:**
- **Spring peepers:** tan with a dark X-shaped cross on the back (l.139); about thumb-tip size, 2–3 cm (l.137); call from reed stems with a large balloon-like vocal sac (l.139); a single high whistled "peep" (l.127); evening chorus heard far off as "tiny bells" (l.394, the classic sleigh-bell description).
- **Laurel or rhododendron thickets ("laurel hells")** on steep Appalachian slopes are dense, tough and twisty, and are famously hard to push through (l.172–178).
- **White trillium (three petals) and violets** in late April and early May in a rich tulip-tree cove (l.205) are correctly timed. Tulip trees in fresh leaf are correct.
- **A sand-boil spring** (l.211): upwelling groundwater keeps a patch of sand churning. This is real and well described.
- **Trail blazes** (l.256–262): bark cut away to mark a route, leaving healed, lipped scars that stay legible for many decades. They stay at the same height because trees do not lift existing marks as they grow (l.260 "at the same height" is right).
- **Chestnut blight kills the stem, not the roots,** and the old root collars keep sending up sprouts for decades (l.369). This is correct and a lovely, true turning point.
- **American chestnut habit and lore:** a dominant ridge tree, very tall, flowering in late June and July with long creamy catkins that made the ridges look snow-covered (l.332), and sweet nuts falling in autumn (l.332). All correct.
- **Rot-resistant chestnut heartwood** makes a large old stump that survives for many decades plausible (l.312).
- **Map basics:** north, scale and symbols (l.62–66). A compass needle points north (magnetic north; fine for the age group).

**Required:**
- **S-1. The daytime full peeper chorus (l.127–131).** Peepers do call by day in spring, but sporadically. A deafening marsh-wide chorus that starts "as if somebody had given a signal" in mid-morning, an hour after setting off, is an evening and night behaviour. Make the morning a scattered few (still enough to find one), and keep the full chorus for dusk at l.394, which then also sets up the bedtime close.
- **S-2. Chestnut leaf teeth (l.359).** "Neat little teeth" is wrong in the identifying detail. American chestnut leaves are long and narrow, with **large, sharp, slightly hooked teeth, one at the end of every vein**. That is the field mark separating them from beech. Use "big, sharp, curved teeth, like the edge of a saw". l.406 and l.422 ("saw-toothed") are fine.
- **S-3. "Every one" (l.336).** The blight killed nearly all mature American chestnuts, but not literally all trees, and the root sprouts in this very story are living chestnuts. Use "nearly every one", which also resolves M-4.
- **S-4. Compass logic (l.113, l.160–162).** With no north on the map, a compass bearing means nothing until the map is oriented to a landmark. "The X is that way" cannot come from the compass as written. See P-3 for the fix, which is also a real map-reading lesson.

**Recommended:**
- **S-5. "A blackbird" (l.127).** British readers will picture the Eurasian blackbird, which is absent from Appalachia. "A red-winged blackbird", the classic marsh singer, is correct and vivid.
- **S-6. "So cold it made Beau's teeth ache" (l.219).** Appalachian spring water runs at about 12–14 °C. It is cold to drink but unlikely to ache the teeth. Soften to "so cold it made Beau gasp".
- **S-7. Blazes cut "with his knife" (l.262).** Traditional blazes were cut with a hatchet or axe. Either is plausible, and adult-handled either way. Optional.
- **S-8. "Biggest tree in the whole of the mountains" (l.332)** and the table-for-ten stump (l.312, about 2.5 m across) are at the extreme upper end for American chestnut (record trees reached about 3 m in diameter). This is acceptable as Buck's childhood memory. Leave it.

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding, bedtime suitability and narration readiness

**Verdict: PASS WITH NOTES** (conditional on N-1 to N-4; N-5 to N-7 recommended).

**The SAFEGUARDING flag is met.** Great-Grandpappy Buck is named, and he is present for every outdoor moment from the porch (l.94) to the summit and the wait at the oak (l.394):
- he waits at the foot of the laurel slope within earshot (l.182–184);
- adults (the miller and Beau's father) come with lanterns to walk everyone home in the dark (l.392);
- Rusty's mother collects him (l.396).

Nobody walks alone in the dark. Rusty's solo walks (l.398, l.422) are along the creek path in daylight. No flames, blades or water hazards are in children's hands. The blazing knife is a historical adult action.

**Required:**
- **N-1. Buck "puffing, with his paw on his chest" (l.285).** For a small listener, an elderly great-grandparent clutching his chest reads as a heart attack. This compounds with "for such a long time that Beau began to worry about him" (l.322) and "Buck's legs were tired" (l.392). Change the gesture to hands on knees or leaning on his stick, with a chuckle ("Sorry, young'uns. Old legs."), and keep l.322 as a moment of memory, not of worry.
- **N-2. The return should be planned, not a rescue (l.392).** As written, the lone supervising adult takes two youngsters to a ridge-top at sunset with no lantern and no plan, and they are saved by Maverick fetching help. Make the lantern party arranged in advance, for example "as Buck had arranged with the miller before they left" or Reba's note asking the miller to come up at sunset. This models a well-planned outing, and the warm waiting scene at the oak survives intact.
- **N-3. Set a boundary before the laurel (l.174–176).** Laurel hells are a classic place to get lost. Keep Buck's silent, teacherly wait, but add a single line before they go in, such as "Stay where you can hear my stick." The comedy is unharmed.
- **N-4. "He had been up half the night with it" (l.400).** This models staying up late in a bedtime story. Use "all morning, from breakfast on", or "until his mother said lights out".

**Recommended:**
- **N-5. Drinking from a wild spring (l.217–219).** This is adult-led and traditional, but many parents will balk at children being invited to drink untreated wild water. A founder call: either keep it as Buck's known lifelong spring, with Buck filling cups for them, or have them dip their paws and feel the cold instead.
- **N-6. Softening the sorrow.** The death of every chestnut (l.336) and Buck forgetting "the way … that I'd ever drawn a map at all" (l.336) are held in warmth and immediately redeemed by the shoots, so they are within "no unnecessary sadness". "Nearly every one" (S-3) softens the finality further. Keep the memory loss as charming forgetfulness. Do not add to it.
- **N-7. Bedtime landing.** The final paragraph is a bright morning (l.422). Moving the close to dusk or night (P-1) gives a much stronger sleep cue, and the far peeper chorus is a ready-made lullaby sound.

**Bedtime suitability:**
- The pacing slows correctly through Ch7–10.
- Stakes are gentle throughout: a thicket, a vanished path, an empty X.
- The stump disappointment lasts three lines before hope arrives.
