## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.1 / 10. NOT PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This is a lovely draft with a real natural-history engine. The hepatica closing in the basket is the turning point, and it is factually true, visual and sad without being bleak. Four things keep it under the mark:
- the premise retreads 009 closely;
- the moral is told several times before and after Buck gives it;
- the sadness runs for three chapters while the comedy is missing from Ch3–4;
- the outings near a river in spate are unsupervised.

### Strengths

- **The plot is driven by facts.** All three flowers act on the plot according to their real biology:
  - The hepatica shuts when the sun goes in and wilts once picked (l.119, l.129, l.144).
  - The bloodroot's stinging sap gives a "don't touch" beat (l.199–209).
  - The arbutus's soil partnerships rule out transplanting (l.260).

  Each failed idea (bring it; dig it up) is rejected for a true reason, which pushes the children to the real solution: take Maggie Mae to the flowers. This is the moral dramatised, not preached.
- **The turning point is strong and comes from a child.** Beau says "Then she has to come to them" (l.282). It answers Sadie Belle's despair directly and leads into the best set piece in the story.
- **The carrying-chair comedy (Ch7) is excellent.** It is staged and it escalates in three steps: Barney is tipped backwards ("Is this how kings go?", l.311), then forwards ("I can only see Brody's ears", l.319), then carried level. Sadie Belle's insight that "It's not about who's strongest... it's about who's the same height" (l.325) is a real child-found solution. The adult only ties knots and says "Down. Gently." This is the house model done right.
- **Ch2's escalating gag works.** Barney shouts "FOUND ONE!" three times and fewer friends run each time (l.72–80), ending in the mushroom argument and Benny losing count.
- **The Spring Book is a beautiful device.** The writing grows neater over the years (l.23), and the arbutus note is the key Sadie Belle remembers (l.256–262). The two-line coda, "Seen where it is, 5th March. Thank you." (l.378), and "Next year: bring Sadie Belle." (l.434) are economical and moving.
- **The Mountain Wisdom delivery is clean.** Buck reads the two lines twice, looks at Sadie Belle and gives #63 with no summarising lead-in (l.401–405).
- **The gardener suggestion is answered.** Maggie Mae has seed packets (l.5) and owns the knowledge: why hepatica closes (l.144), the arbutus note (l.260) and the seed set (l.422). The CANON flag is handled well. There is no skunk cabbage, and the one smell linked to Maggie Mae is lovely ("It smells like Maggie Mae's kitchen", l.240).
- **The hepaticas closing at sunset ("Goodnight... See you in the morning", l.385–389)** is the best bedtime image in the story.

### Weaknesses

1. **The premise recycles 009 (major).** In 009 an injured grown-up (the miller's wife, bad back) misses the seasonal "first" she has never missed "since I was a girl". The children try to bring it to her, it would not survive being brought indoors ("They'd melt"), so they bring her to it, and Brody carries her chair out. In 016:
   - Maggie Mae is injured (sprained ankle).
   - She is missing the first flower, and "I've never missed a year" (l.25).
   - The flower dies when brought indoors.
   - She is carried out, and Brody carries again.

   The beats map almost one to one. 014 also has Benny picking "the first flowers I could find" for a bedridden Barney (014 l.196). That makes this the third "first of the season for an invalid" story in seven.
2. **The moral is told outside Buck's line (rule 1).**
   - The narrator foreshadows it: "how much trouble that word 'bring' was going to cause" (l.39).
   - Maggie Mae generalises: "Wildflowers mostly don't like being picked..." (l.144).
   - The grandmother's maxim "Not where you take it" (l.156) is the moral in miniature, given by an adult figure.
   - After Buck, Maggie Mae restates #63 almost exactly: "somebody who isn't even born yet... they'll be here" (l.428).
3. **A beat recycled from 014.** "And my grandmother said..." / She stopped. / "What did she say?" (l.148–156) is 014's "she said..." / She stopped (014 l.288, l.405), with the same grandmother-saying device. In 016 the hesitation has no motive, because she tells it at once.
4. **Too much sadness and no comedy in Ch3–4 (rule 4).** The guilt and ache run from l.129 to l.278:
   - ears flat and eyes filling (l.138);
   - "now I've spoiled it" (l.160);
   - "could not quite smile" (l.170–172);
   - a sleepless night (l.177–179);
   - "a strange ache" twice (l.185, l.223);
   - "It's no good" (l.278).

   That is about a third of the book. Ch3 and Ch4 have no comic beat at all, so the escalation the rule asks for in Ch2–4 stops after Ch2.
5. **The close is a pattern, and it is daytime.** The final image is next year's seeds "waiting for their turn" (l.438). It repeats the season's habit of closing on something waiting for the future:
   - 005: the latch-string "ready for whoever might come next";
   - 006: "waiting for next year";
   - 014: the slate "until next winter";
   - 015: the wheel "only resting".

   "Walked home together... side by side" echoes 009 and 004. None of this is on the banned list, but it is not fresh. The last paragraph is a bright mid-morning with bees "busy and buzzing", which is a weaker sleep cue than Ch9's dusk.
6. **Rusty is thin.** He fetches Maverick (l.60) and knows the shortest path (l.115), and nothing he does is character-true or decisive. Brody is mostly a pair of shoulders. Both are acceptable, but they are the weak links in an otherwise good ensemble.
7. **Small craft slips:**
   - "rest their arms" for a deer (l.391);
   - a bobcat "thumped his tail" (l.242);
   - "a bottle cap" (l.74) is litter in a Hollow whose "creek stays clean" (Bible), and it is a faintly manufactured, dated object;
   - Sadie Belle sits "a little apart from the others" (l.403) with no reason given, which leaves an unresolved sadness just before the wisdom;
   - "like a queen" appears twice (l.344, l.393), as does "fist/fists" (l.129, l.342);
   - the full "Found by..." list is read twice (l.166, l.374).

### "Sherlock", natural history and physics

**Confirmed correct:**
- **Hepatica (*Hepatica americana / acutiloba*)** is among the very first native wildflowers in the southern Appalachians. It blooms from late February to April, often on south-facing, leaf-littered slopes, so early March on a sunny bank is right.
  - Hairy stems (l.99), lavender, pink and white colour forms (l.101) and a creamy stamen cluster (l.99) are all correct.
  - The flowers close at night and in cloudy, cool weather (l.119, l.129, l.144, l.385). This is accurate and the story's best fact.
  - A picked flower wilting and closing within an hour of cloud and wind (l.117–129) is plausible.
  - "Six petals": they are technically petal-like sepals and the number varies from about 5 to 12. "Six" is acceptable for a child audience.
- **The snow-melt logic** is correct: south-facing banks lose snow first and are warmest (l.58, l.64). Benny's "Flowers can't come up through snow" is acceptable as a child's reasoning.
- **Bloodroot (*Sanguinaria canadensis*)** blooms March–April, early on warm slopes, so 4th March on a sunny bank is plausible.
  - The single grey-green leaf wrapped round the stalk is correct (l.189).
  - 8–10 (up to about 16) white petals around golden stamens is correct.
  - The sap is irritant and toxic: "stings... not for mouths" (l.209) is correct, and good safety modelling.
  - A small fly visiting (l.217) is correct, because early flies and bees pollinate it.
  - Bloodroots "everywhere" by mid-March (l.420) is plausible.
- **Trailing arbutus (*Epigaea repens*):**
  - Leathery evergreen leaves, battered over winter, are correct (l.236).
  - Clusters of waxy, tubular pink-and-white flowers with a sweet, spicy scent are correct (l.232, l.236).
  - It is notoriously hard to transplant because it depends on soil fungal partners (ericoid mycorrhizae). The note "friends in the soil that we cannot see and cannot carry" (l.260) is an excellent child-level rendering.
  - It blooms from March to May, so early March is at the early edge but credible on a warm, sheltered bank, and "still in bloom" in mid-March is fine.
- **Early bees in March** are correct: queen bumblebees and small mining and sweat bees visit hepatica (l.370, l.420). A "sleepy" first bee is apt.
- **Bluets ("tiny pale-blue flowers in the grass")** bloom in March and April (l.420). A singing chickadee in March (l.420) is correct.
- **Hepatica setting a nodding cluster of green seeds** (l.416, l.422) is correct in kind, and they are ant-dispersed.
- **The sprain care** is sound: rest, elevation and a compression bandage, with no weight-bearing for a week (l.7).

**Required fixes:**
- **S-1 (l.420).** "New green leaves unfurling on the beeches" in mid-March is wrong. American beech leafs out from mid to late April in the southern Appalachians. Replace it with "the buds on the beeches growing long and pointed" (beech buds are distinctively long and cigar-shaped), or with red maple flowers, which really do bloom in March.
- **S-2 (l.84).** The bank faces south, and the friends stand at its top looking down it towards the creek. They are therefore facing the sun, so it cannot be "warm on their backs". Use "warm on their fur" or "warm on their faces". Do not have anyone look up at it (rule 7).
- **S-3 (l.197–199, also l.397).** Bloodroot sap is **orange-red**, not "bright red... like the reddest berry". The name comes chiefly from the **root**, which holds the most sap. Suggested answer to Beau: "Because its root's full of orange-red juice, and so's its stem, if you break it."
- **S-4 (l.260, echoed l.268 and l.397).** "Moved, it always dies" overstates it. Use "Moved, it almost never lives". That keeps the force of the point and stays accurate.

**Recommended:**
- **S-5 (l.420).** "Two early butterflies, small and yellow" in mid-March is possible (clouded sulphurs can fly on warm March days), but the classic first butterflies here are the **spring azure** (small and pale blue) and the **mourning cloak** (dark, with cream edges). A spring azure would also echo the pale-blue bluets.
- **S-6 (l.416).** "The hepaticas were nearly over... petals had dropped" by mid-March is tight if the first flowers opened on about 3rd March. Use "many of the hepaticas were going over" and "the earliest ones had dropped their petals".
- **S-7 (l.236).** Eastern hemlock usually favours cool, moist, often north-facing ravines, while arbutus likes drier, acidic, well-drained banks, often under pine or oak. A hemlock at the creek edge of the bank is possible, but "under a big old pine" or "under an oak" is the safer habitat match.
- **S-8 (l.80).** For the mushroom gag, a **scarlet elf cup** is a red, cup-shaped fungus that genuinely appears in late winter and early spring and really does look like a flower. It would make Barney's "it was the right shape" both funny and true. No touching or tasting (N-5).

**Sherlock result: PASS WITH NOTES** (conditional on S-1 to S-4).

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding, bedtime suitability and narration readiness

**Positives:**
- The bloodroot warning is explicit and correct ("not for paws... not for mouths", l.209), and Reba chooses restraint (l.211–213).
- Digging up a wild plant is considered and rejected (l.256–264).
- In the chair test, an adult ties and checks every knot (l.297) and supervises every lift ("Down. Gently. Both ends at once.", l.313).
- The injury is gentle and quickly reassured ("not a break, thank goodness", l.7), and there is no dwelling on pain.
- There is no menace, predation or sarcasm, and the humour is never at anyone's expense. Barney enjoys his tumble (l.315).
- The overall message (look, don't take) is a healthy one for children.

**Required:**
- **N-1 (l.46–92). The first search is unsupervised beside high water.** Seven youngsters, including the smallest (Charlie), go to the top of a "steep bank" above a creek "loud and brown with meltwater", and Charlie lies on the slope (l.90). Add an accompanying grown-up. Sadie Belle's mother is the natural choice, since she reappears in Ch7–9. Keep everyone at the top of the bank, with the water "well below".
- **N-2 (l.181–183). The second outing is also unsupervised, and the text names the danger.** "She had promised her mother she would not go anywhere on her own while the creek was running so high" suggests that three youngsters together are enough. Have her mother (or Reba's) come with them.
- **N-3 (l.346, l.368). The injured passenger is placed near a drop.** The chair is set down "where the bank fell away below" and then carried "along the bank" on a steep slope. Specify a flat place well back from the edge, and a level path along the top to the hemlock (Rusty's route; see L1-6).
- **N-4 (l.412–414). Ch10 sends two youngsters alone to the bank above Silver Creek.** Maggie Mae is canon *young*, and the 015 review treated her as a youngster. Add a grown-up, such as the miller's wife checking the ankle on the way, or state that the creek is back down and they keep to the top path in company.

**Recommended:**
- **N-5 (l.109, l.80). Mouths and wild plants.** Sadie Belle picks the hepatica "with her soft mouth". It is natural for a deer, but hepatica is in the buttercup family and its fresh sap is irritant. Since the story later teaches "not for mouths" (l.209), consider "nipped through the fuzzy stem" without the mouth emphasis, or have Reba's paw do the picking at Sadie Belle's request. ### Summary

"Sadie Belle and the First Spring Flower" has a strong, fact-driven heart. The botany is used to drive the story, not just as decoration:
- the hepatica closing when picked is the turning point;
- the bloodroot sap gives the "don't touch" beat;
- the arbutus's invisible soil partners rule out digging it up.

Beau's "Then she has to come to them" and the carrying-chair comedy (Ch7) are among the season's best child-solved set pieces. Buck's #63 is word for word, with no lead-in.

It scores 8.1, not 8.5, for four reasons:
- the premise closely retreads 009, and touches 014's flowers for Barney;
- the moral is pre-stated at l.39, l.144 and l.156 and restated at l.428;
- about a third of the book is guilt and ache, with no comedy in Ch3–4, and the "she said..." / She stopped beat is lifted from 014;
- the close follows the season's "waiting for next year" pattern on a bright daytime note.

Safety must be fixed: three outings beside a meltwater-swollen creek have no grown-up, and the chair carrying an injured passenger is set by a drop. Sherlock's four factual fixes are small:
- beech leaf-out;
- sun direction;
- bloodroot sap colour;
- "always dies".

Lock Two and Lock Three pass with notes. Revisions 1–16 are required for round 2.
