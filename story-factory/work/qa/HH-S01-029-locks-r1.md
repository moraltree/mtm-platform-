## LOCK ONE: "Piers Morgan" (story quality)

**Score: 8.2 / 10. FAIL (pass mark 8.5)**

This is a warm, well-built ensemble story. The structure works: an inciting absence, a list, four strands, a gathering. The comedy is good, and the strongest line in the story is Sadie Belle's quiet confession. It fails Lock One on fixable problems:
- **Rule 1.** Maggie Mae speaks the Wisdom's logic in Ch4, and the story then restates it three or four more times.
- **Rule 5.** The close over-signals 030, and its last image repeats a recent closing frame.
- Two beats recycle 026 and 009.

### Strengths
- **The turn is earned, and it is fair play.** Sadie Belle is quietly missing from the Ch1 roll-call of who is lying where (l.23–33) and from the spring ("Nobody had noticed at first", l.83). Her Ch3 confession (l.91) is unforced and in character for v1.1's gentle, caring Sadie Belle. The noticing then spreads naturally: "everybody started noticing" (l.97). This is the best inciting turn in the summer block.
- **Every one of the ten friends does real, in-character work (Rule 3).**
  - Sadie Belle notices first.
  - Reba gives up the cool cellar to run the jug (l.228–236). It is a lovely food-first-to-kindness move that respects v1.1.
  - Barney, "Chief Splasher", gets evaporation right before Benny does (l.189–195). It is a delightful inversion that does not belittle Benny.
  - Benny keeps the list and directs the build.
  - Brody carries the poles, ties the high corners and is the one who noticed the rabbits.
  - Maggie Mae provides the through-draught and the hosting.
  - Charlie has the "emptier, not bigger" idea for the burrow (l.257).
  - Beau is silly on purpose. His fence-post walk is planted in Ch1 (l.29–31) and paid off in Ch7 (l.277).
  - Rusty finds the spring and persuades Buck.
  - Maverick's survey spots the haymakers (l.333).
  - Charlie's count is right: ten friends against four "bits" (l.134).
- **The children solve everything (Rule 2).** Buck asks no questions at all, and no adult supplies a method. Brody's father digs the holes (l.187), Rusty's father escorts (l.64, l.120, l.308, l.374), the miller lends sacking and lifts his wife down. These are safe adult tasks only.
- **The comedy escalates on stage (Rule 4).**
  - The fanning feedback loop, from the fans to Brody's sycamore leaf to "everybody was fanning somebody, and nobody was any cooler at all" (l.42–50).
  - The heat-test on Barney, "not scientific and also not polite" (l.58).
  - Maverick's "It is not … a very magnificent look. But it works." (l.56) is swagger with self-mockery, exactly v1.1.
  - "Hottest everything" (l.21).
  - Guilt is short (l.105–115) and turns on "Well. We're thinking now." (l.115).
- **The Rusty–Buck persuasion (l.290–306) is the emotional set-piece, and it works.** Rusty forgets his clever lines and simply asks. His real appeal is to share a discovery, and Buck's "cleverest shortcut I've ever been taken on" (l.306) turns Rusty's canon habit into a gift.
- **The safeguarding brief is honoured.** Nobody is ill. The elders are "wilted", "hot" or "tired", never unwell. There is no fire and no drought peril. Water play is ankle-deep with adults watching (l.120, l.337). Small buckets and jugs are used, and the "big bucket … too heavy for anybody" is refused (l.228).
- **The Mountain Wisdom is verbatim and unused (l.347).** It is staged at the creek rather than the porch, which is permitted by founder default #6 and is fresh.

### Weaknesses
1. **The moral is pre-stated (Rule 1).**
   - **Maggie Mae, l.126:** "We can't fix all of it. But there must be bits we can fix." This is #57 in paraphrase, two words from the original, in Ch4. Benny's l.122 ("Nobody can fix weather") sets it up. "list of the bits" (l.128) and "Four bits" (l.132) then make the whole plan run on the saying's vocabulary. When Buck speaks at l.347, the child has heard the lesson already.
   - **Slogans:** Benny's "Everybody takes somebody." (l.172) and Maverick's "everybody who needed somebody had somebody" (l.333) are a tagline used twice.
   - **Narrator:** "By Friday evening, the heat had not broken. But everybody was managing." (l.329) states the saying's shape: the whole trouble remained, the corners were fixed.
   - **Rusty, l.109:** "We were busy keeping ourselves cool. And we didn't even think." The first sentence is concrete and fine. The second names the moral failing outright. It is borderline, and a lighter touch is better.
   - **Charlie, l.251/257:** "We can't make the burrow bigger … But we could make it emptier" is concrete problem-solving and acceptable. But it is a third "can't X, but can Y" beat on the Wisdom's pattern. Vary its shape if you touch it.
2. **Buck gives a second maxim (Rule 1, Canon 2).** "It's been here a good while. Folk just go past it too fast to see." (l.314) is a Mountain-Wisdom-style saying on a different theme: slow down and notice. It is exactly the type of line cut from 026 in round 1 (026 L1-1).
3. **Rusty's slow walk re-stages 026 and states a second theme.** The l.310–316 expansion (only in the scratchpad copy) has an energetic youngster slowed to Buck's pace who notices small things on the way. That is 026's Ch6 walk to Grandpappy's Pool ("At my speed", beetles on the way), from three stories ago, and it leans on 025's slow-down theme too. Three consecutive "slow down" beats make a season motif. "Never knew this was here" and Buck's reply (l.312–314) turn it into a lesson. One concrete image is enough: the green tunnel where Buck stands with his eyes closed is the best of them.
4. **The close over-signals 030 and recycles a recent frame (Rule 5).** See the 030 assessment below.
   - "something in Benny's face had changed … as if a door somewhere had opened a crack and let a thin line of light in" (l.376) is the narrator explaining the plant. It also tips the brothers' inner turn into 029, which belongs to 030.
   - The last line, "Behind them, Silver Creek went on running … down the valley, and out of the Hollow, and away" (l.378), repeats a recent closing frame:
     - 016's and 027's "Behind them, …" opener;
     - 026's "went on singing it, steady and slow, all down the dark valley, without him" (the story before last);
     - 025's "went on skimming … until the light was gone";
     - 001's "The creek went on talking to its stones".
   - The last word, "away", ends a story about a community holding together on an image of leaving.
   - "Benny and Barney were the last to take their paws out" (l.356) echoes 002's "The brothers were the last to go."
5. **The Ch9 lead-in to the Wisdom summarises (Rule 1 / Rule 5).**
   - l.331 is a 120-word recap of every strand.
   - l.345 has Buck look "along the row. At Granny Opal … At the miller's wife … At the little rabbits … At all of them". This catalogue re-uses 009's pre-Wisdom "He looked at … He looked at … He looked at".
   - Together with l.329 and l.333, the reader gets four restatements in one chapter before Buck speaks.
   - "It was too lovely for talking, so hardly anybody did." (l.343) brushes the banned "Nobody said anything" (Rule 5).
6. **The engine is familiar.** A quiet carer is discovered (018: Maggie Mae doing the miller's wife's washing "never said a word"), then the friends split into teams for vulnerable neighbours (011, 018, 022). This is not a fail, because the heat setting and four-strand structure make it its own story. But it is one more reason to keep the close and the lead-in fresh.
7. **Repetition for read-aloud.** "cool" appears 30 times, "hot" 32, "very" 34 and "somebody" 11. The first two are thematic, but 30 uses of "cool" will be audible. "Oh, my dears" appears twice for Granny Opal (l.183, l.211), plus "Oh, my" twice (l.201). Trim "cool" by about a third.

### Sherlock: verdict REVISE (minor)

**Required fixes**
- **S-1. An earth-and-root hollow does not "hold the heat" (l.91).** Soil buffers temperature, so burrows and earth hollows stay *cooler* than the air in a heatwave. That is why animals burrow. Make the problem air, not heat: "it's close and airless in there, and dark, and she can't sit in the dark all day". The same applies to the rabbit burrow. Its problem as written is crowding (nine in a small burrow, l.101, l.249), which is fine. Do not add "hot burrow" language.
- **S-2. Watering in the heat "only scorches the leaves" (l.25) is a garden myth.** Water droplets do not meaningfully burn leaves. The real reasons for watering at dawn and dusk are that less water evaporates and more reaches the roots, and that at dawn the leaves dry by day, which helps prevent mildew on roses. Fix: "because in the heat of the day the sun drinks the water up before it ever reaches the roots". Optionally add "at the roots", consistent with 018.
- **S-3. A through-draught with "no wind. Not a breath." (l.9 vs l.222–224).** Cross-ventilation needs some air movement. A temperature difference between a shady north side over moving water and a sun-baked south side does drive a gentle exchange, and the mill-race itself drags a little cool air along with it. Add one clause, e.g. "and the race, running past, pulled a thread of cool air along with it". Keep Maggie Mae's explanation, but "the cool air pushes the hot air out" is acceptable folk physics.
- **S-4. "Thick old walls" for a timber barn (l.267).** See M-2. The rest of the paragraph is sound: high roof, heat collecting in the rafters, the floor cooler, doors at both ends.
- **S-5. "Icy" spring water (l.68, l.318).** Spring water emerges at about the local mean annual temperature, roughly 10–13 °C in the southern Appalachians. That is cold enough to make you gasp on a hot day, but not icy. Use "cold" or "cold as a cellar". Rusty's "Freezing cold" (l.62) is hyperbole in character, so keep it.
- **S-6. "The first frogs began to croak" in early September (l.354).** Most Appalachian frog choruses (green frogs, bullfrogs) have tailed off by late August. Bats over water at dusk are correct. Crickets are the strongest early-September dusk sound. Katydids are also in season, but they were 026's closing chorus, so avoid them. Suggested: "The crickets started up in the grass, and somewhere down among the reeds a late frog croaked, once."
- **S-7. Barney sprinkles the top of the sacking "with his paws" (l.195)** after Brody, the tallest, had to tie the corners at the tops of the poles (l.187). Barney cannot reach over it.
