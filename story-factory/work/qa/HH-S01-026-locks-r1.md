## LOCK ONE: "Piers Morgan" (story quality)

**Score: 8.1 / 10. FAIL (pass mark 8.5)**

This is one of the stronger S01 round-1 drafts. The premise is warm and original, and the musical texture is genuinely lovely. It fails on a cluster of fixable problems, not on structure:
- Buck delivers an off-canon maxim before the Wisdom.
- The narrator restates Brody's growth several times.
- The close re-stages 019 and 008.
- The reveal beat recycles 024's quick wipe of the eyes.
- Maverick's "shy" contribution is never used.
- The lent banjo contradicts 001.

### Strengths
- **The premise is fresh and well matched to its lead.** A tune with no name "is a hard thing to call back" (l.25). This is a beautiful, child-sized problem with no villain and no peril, and it pays off logically when the tune is named at the end. It is clearly different from the archived "Brody's First Banjo Song": the story is about recovering a tune, not learning a first one.
- **The method grows out of character, as the coordinator asked:**
  - Benny counts the threes (l.111–113) and then spots Buck rocking in threes (l.270–284). This is exactly his v1.0 musical trait: "Keeps time — taps out rhythm on wood". It is the best use of Benny's canon in the season, and it pays off again at l.356 when he taps the rail quietly whenever Brody hurries.
  - Rusty whistles birdsong in canon, and his "twiddly bit" that "might have been a wren" (l.105–109) turns into a gentle run "like water round a stone" once it is slowed down (l.311–313). This is a clean, satisfying reveal.
  - Reba finds the middle in the miller's wife's pie-humming (l.133, paid off at l.360–362). It is food-first canon Reba, planted as a joke in Ch3 and paid off as a discovery in Ch8. Excellent.
  - Sadie Belle's "Play it as if it's going to stay" (l.350) is the best line in the story. It is in character, unforced, and it becomes a motif (l.352, l.389).
- **The comedy is warm and specific:**
  - The kingfisher's "great disapproval" (l.56).
  - "The banjo did not argue." (l.62), which is echoed and well rhymed at l.448.
  - Beau's "It should have had a big finish," said "from the ground" (l.131).
  - "Three-and-a-halves" (l.162).
  - "A bee falling down a drainpipe" (l.164).
  - The slow-walk gag in which Brody ends up carrying everything while Buck carries nothing (l.248) is a classic comic escalation that also carries the theme without stating it.
- **Brody's arc is shown well.** He goes from frantic speed (l.54, l.58, l.178) through frustration ("I can't do it", l.182) to finding the tune one note at a time (l.315). The joy of learning is dramatised: his scales are "getting better", his paws "surer" (l.344). The broken string is a well-judged, low-stakes low point.
- **Buck mostly stays the soul and not the hero.** He asks one question (l.19). He remembers only after Brody has found the tune (l.393–403). He restrings and tunes the banjo, a safe adult task.
- **The Mountain Wisdom is word for word and unused.** #40 at l.421 matches v1.0 §8 exactly, and it comes with no summarising lead-in.
- **Ch10's distant bear-sized hum "in threes" going round the bend (l.436) is the freshest closing image in some time.** It is the one element of the close that should certainly be kept.

### Weaknesses
1. **Buck states the moral before the Wisdom, which breaks Rule 1 and Canon 2 ("He never lectures").**
   - "You can tune a banjo quick. Or you can tune it right. Took me about sixty years to find out you can't do both." (l.223) is a second Mountain-Wisdom-style maxim on the same theme. It is essentially #27 or #36 in Buck's mouth.
   - Buck is aphoristic elsewhere too:
     - "It'll come back when it's ready." (l.25) previews the Wisdom.
     - "Strings break. That's what they're for. Breaking, so the banjo doesn't." (l.186) is a maxim on a different theme, and he repeats it at l.217.
     - "At my speed." (l.237) and "Then we'll get there" (l.252) also lean that way.
   - The slow tuning scene is stronger without any words. Let the long "Plink. A little turn of the peg." do the work.
2. **The narrator restates Brody's growth (Rule 1, softer instances).**
   - "He had learned that much." (l.307)
   - "And for the first time, he did not sound as if tomorrow were too long to wait." (l.335)
   - "he found that he did not mind as much as he would have done a week ago" (l.344)
   - Each is small, but together they spell out the lesson three times before Buck does. Keep the concrete evidence ("His scales were getting better. His paws were getting surer.") and drop the commentary.
3. **The close re-stages earlier closes (Rule 5).** None of the banned list appears verbatim: no banjo from its corner, no "porch went quiet", no first star. But:
   - **Buck alone on the porch after the youngsters leave at blue dusk is 019's close**, also a Brody-and-Buck story. 019 has "the porch emptied… until only Buck was left… in the blue dusk". 026 has "The children went home in the long blue dusk… Buck stayed out on the porch." (l.426–428).
   - 008 already ended on the empty porch with "the slow creak of the rocking chair, and the soft notes of the banjo". 021 ended on Buck rocking as he watched them go.
   - **"as they had every late summer night for longer than even Buck could remember" (l.444)** repeats 016's "for longer than anybody in Hickory Hollow could remember" (bloodroots) and 020's "since long before Buck was a young beaver".
   - The natural chorus carrying on into the night is the 016, 017 (peepers) and 022 (bees humming all night) close family.
   - **"until his eyes grew heavy… who sang the whole valley to sleep" (l.450)** drifts into the falling-asleep close the coordinator asked to avoid (006, 012, 013, 025). 016's "the whole bank was asleep" is a further echo.
   - **On naming:** "Brody's Tune" (l.446) is a naming peak. Unlike the inscriptions of 014 (slate), 015 (board), 016 (painted name) and 017 ("HERE"), it is spoken, private and motivated by l.25. I judge it acceptable *if* the staging around it is new. An alternative that pays off Maverick (see Weakness 5): Buck nearly names it and then doesn't, because a shy tune does not need calling now that it has somewhere to live.
4. **The reveal is moving but thinly set up, and it recycles a beat.**
   - The fair-play clues are good: the fifth note "simply there, waiting, like a step… in the dark" (l.299), and the paw that goes on "all by itself" (l.372). But both come after Ch6. Nothing in Ch1–3 hints that Brody has any history with the tune.
   - One early line would make the reveal feel earned rather than sprung, for example at l.7–9: the tune feels like one whose shape he already knew, though he could not say why. 010 already established Brody humming along with Buck's banjo "without knowing he was doing it", which is a ready precedent.
   - **"Sadie Belle… wiped her eyes very quickly, and pretended she had something in them" (l.411) recycles 024's "Brody wiped his nose on the back of his paw, very quickly, so that nobody would see"**, from the story before last.
   - "He did not need to." (l.411) and "All along." (l.407) nudge the moment towards sentimentality. It is warm enough without them.
5. **The ensemble passes Rule 3, but three friends are light.**
   - **Maverick:** his "shy tune" observation (l.207) is the most interesting idea any friend has, and nothing uses it. Brody "thought about that all the way up the path" (l.209), and then it disappears. It also contradicts Rusty hearing the tune from the porch steps (l.105) and Charlie hearing it at the Pool (l.76) (see M-3). The natural payoff is at l.415–417: Brody, about to rush in front of the crowd, remembers that the tune doesn't come when it's called, and plays as if the porch were empty.
   - **Charlie:** her contribution ("it goes up at the end") is something Brody already knew (l.78). In Ch8 she only waits (l.366–368). Her one real clue, that Buck plays it when fishing at Grandpappy's Pool (l.76), could send the children to the Pool (see Weakness 6).
   - **Beau:** comic relief, with a nice recantation (l.327). That is fine as one of nine.
   - Ch3 (l.103–145) is a parade: each friend gets one beat, in turn. It is charming but static. Rule 4 escalation really happens only in Ch4.
6. **Buck's invitation to the Pool sets up the method (Rule 2, marginal).** The children find the actual method: Benny spots the rocking. But Buck chooses the Pool, the walk and the slowness ("At my speed", l.237), and the l.223 maxim gives the key reasoning in advance. If Brody asks to go to the Pool on Charlie's clue (l.76), and Buck simply agrees "At my speed", then the children own the route as well as the method.
7. **Continuity: the banjo was "lent him at the start of the summer" (l.35).** In 001 (autumn), Brody "took his own little banjo out of his rucksack. Buck had been teaching him, string by string, all through the summer". He plays his little banjo again in 013. See M-1.
8. **There is too much repetition for a read-aloud text.**
   - "slowly" appears 23 times, "like a question" 7 times and "very" about 60 times.
   - "as he did everything" appears twice (l.184, l.244), and "the loveliest" twice in l.5.
   - "Like a question" is a deliberate refrain, but at seven uses it stops landing. Five, including l.7, l.129, l.374 and l.419, would be stronger.
9. **It sits close to 025.** Two consecutive stories end on slowing down and counting slowly: 025's "Count slower" swallows and 026's "Forwards, two, three". The themes are different (simplicity versus patience), so this is not a fail, but it is a reason to keep 026's close away from counting-as-the-last-beat. Buck's "I forgot whose it was" (l.403) is also the third "Buck had forgotten" beat this season (013's crackle, 017's peepers). It is gentle, but it is becoming a motif.

### Sherlock: verdict REVISE (minor)

**Required fixes**
- **S-1. Katydid syllables (l.432–434).** The text gives "Katy-did. Katy-didn't. Katy-did." and then "Three notes. Over and over". "Katy-didn't" is four pulses. The common true katydid (*Pterophylla camellifolia*) really does vary between two-, three- and four-pulse phrases, so the sounds are accurate but "Three notes" is not. Fix by using "Katy-did. Katy-did." for the three-note line, or by saying "Mostly three notes."
- **S-2. A broken string does not silence a banjo (l.178).** "and the banjo went silent". The other strings still sound. Use "and the tune stopped".
- **S-3. Minnows "Not swimming forwards… Just keeping still" (l.252).** Minnows hold station facing into the current (positive rheotaxis) by swimming just hard enough to stay in place. "Every one of them pointing the same way" (l.254) is correct. Change Buck's line to something like "swimming just hard enough to stay where they are". This is truer and is itself a lovely patience image. See Dr Noura on "breakfast".

**Verified as sound**
- **Katydids:** true katydids sing from the deciduous canopy, males at night, starting at dusk, in late July to October in the southern Appalachians. "as they do on late summer nights" (l.430) and "down in the trees by the creek" are correct. The rhythmic, repetitive phrasing is correct. Optional and true: the song slows as the night cools.
- **No fireflies in a mid-August close** is consistent with most Appalachian firefly species finishing by late July. The katydids are a better choice for the season.
- **Kingfisher (belted)** on a branch over a creek, flushing and flying off along the stream (l.56): correct. Optional: it usually goes with a loud rattle.
- **New strings stretch and keep going flat**, so a newly fitted string needs repeated retuning (l.211–223). This is a true and nice detail.
- **Old-time and country tunes are mostly in duple or quadruple time; waltzes are in three** (l.113). This is accurate, and Benny's "very unusual" is fair.
- **Mid-August:** second-cut hay and early apples (l.3, l.64) are plausible. Spider webs, dragonflies and beetles are fine.
- **Memory logic:** Buck can play or hum a tune automatically but cannot retrieve it on demand without a name or cue (l.23–25, l.360). This is a plausible and familiar experience. The miller's wife absorbing his tune into her own humming (l.360) is plausible.
- **Bear cub scale:** a few-month-old black bear cub in its first summer is roughly pumpkin-sized (l.403). Cubs are born in winter dens, so a "summer evening" is consistent.
- **Timeline and counts:** day 1 (lesson, rock, survey, porch), day 2 (the Pool), then "six more days", with Reba on the fourth and the end on the sixth. These are internally consistent.

## LOCK THREE: "Dr Noura" (child safety, development and bedtime tone) and narration readiness

### Dr Noura: verdict PASS WITH CONDITIONS

**Conditions (required)**
- **N-1. The walk home at dusk (l.426, l.436) needs grown-ups shown.** Rule 7 says nobody walks alone in the dark, and S01 has always shown lanterns and families on the way home.
  - "The children went home in the long blue dusk" shows no adults. The lone "bear-sized hum… gone round the bend" (l.436) reads as Brody walking alone.
  - Brody's folks are present (l.413). Have them walk him home, and let the grown-ups bring lanterns for the others.
  - Granny Opal, an elder going home along the creek path, should go with somebody, as in 021's "Sadie Belle's mother said she would walk Granny Opal home".
  - Maverick goes home before dark (M-2).
- **N-2. Bait tin (l.248).** Fishing at the Pool is canon (v1.0: "less about the fish than about the sitting"). But a "bait tin" implies worms on hooks, which edges towards the predator-and-prey prohibition (§15), as does "see if the fish are in a talking mood" in a world where creatures talk.
  - Replace the bait tin with Buck's stool, a flask or a lunch tin. The carrying gag still works.
  - Keep the line uncaught, in v1.0 style. Nothing is caught on the page now: good.
- **N-3. Minnows' "breakfast" (l.252).** "letting the creek bring their breakfast to them" implies minnows eating drifting insects. It is a mild predator/prey reference that the S-3 rewording removes.

**Advisory**
- Brody sits alone on the big flat rock by Grandpappy's Bridge in the afternoon (l.48). It is daytime and on the bank, so the risk is low. Following the 001 precedent, add "in sight of Buck's porch".
- Make the restringing explicit in one clause at l.211, for example "with a bright new string that Buck had put on himself". It is clearly implied now, and Rule 7 is already met.
- Maverick's "a tune falling down the stairs" (l.168) lands while Brody's "ears had gone hot" (l.174). Beau and Benny's loyal lines soften it. To keep humour clear of being at a character's expense (§15), let Brody laugh at the image later, for example when the tune finally comes right.
- Buck's "I forgot whose it was" (l.403) is a gentle portrait of an old person's memory, warmly held. For children with elderly grandparents it is comforting rather than worrying, because the memory comes back. Keep it light, as written.

**Wellbeing and tone**
- Frustration is modelled well. Brody's "I can't do it… And now I've broken a string" (l.182) is met by an adult's calm reassurance ("Strings break") and practical repair, not by shame. Persistence is shown as small daily steps.
- The friends are kind throughout. Barney's marching song is used to make Brody laugh and relax (l.354), which is a good model of play helping learning.
- The reveal is emotional but not sad. Brody's babyhood memory comes with his parents present. It meets "safe, loved" directly.
