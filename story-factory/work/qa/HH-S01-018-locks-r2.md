**New in round 2 (required):**

- **S-1 (R2). Chains and spring (l.93, l.103).**
  - A key-wound eight-day longcase clock is *weight*-driven. Its weights hang on gut or cable lines wound onto barrels, and it has no mainspring.
  - Chains belong to 30-hour clocks, which are wound by pulling the chain, not with a key.
  - So "you'll break the spring" (l.93) and "counted the links of the chains" (l.103) are both wrong for this clock.
  - Fix with no extra words:
    - l.93: "you'll wind the weight right up into the works";
    - l.103: "looked inside the little door at the bottom of the clock, and measured how far the weights had dropped, and worked it out on the back of his hand with his pencil".
  - The real technique is to wind until the weight sits just below the top. Benny's "exactly the right number of turns" is then a correct and characterful way to say it.
- **S-2 (R3). The prop (l.160–162).**
  - A clothes prop raises the *middle* of a line strung between two posts. Lowering it brings the middle down in a dip, while the ends stay high at the posts.
  - So "until the whole line was hanging at just about the height of a chipmunk" (l.162) is not possible.
  - Long sheets at chipmunk height would also lie on the ground.
  - Fix: "until the middle of the line dipped down low enough for Charlie to reach the pegs on tiptoe. She did the middle first, and then walked the prop a little way along and did the next bit." That is about +15 words, and walking a prop along a line is real practice.

**Advisory (unchanged from r1, optional):**
- "the buds wither, and they never open" (l.378) slightly overstates it.
- "tidied away even more greenfly than the grown-up ladybirds" (l.411) is contestable.
- "or spray them" (l.382) is a small timelessness point.
