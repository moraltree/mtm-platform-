## Lock One: "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.1 / 10 (pass mark 8.5): NOT PASSED

This is a well-made, warm, often funny story. It has a clear physical problem, a sensible three-step escalation (bounce, tilt, rocking cap) and a lovely payoff with the bell. It is close to publishable but not there yet. It has three main problems. The brotherly rivalry the brief asks for is thin and one-sided. The moral is spelled out three times at the end. And a run of small logic and continuity slips will catch out a careful adult reader. All of this can be fixed without restructuring.

### Strengths

- **Concrete, child-legible problem.** Each failure is something a 5-year-old can picture: the plank bends like a branch with a bird on it, it sits crooked like a picture on a wall, the block rocks. The bounce in Ch3 ("boing, boing, boing ... like a very surprised ball") is the comic high point and will read aloud brilliantly.
- **The bell is a model set-up and payoff.** It is planted in Ch1 ("I'll think about the bell"), picked up in Ch2 ("The bell's not bad") and paid off in Ch9 ("I said I'd think about the bell. I thought about it."). The final image of the bell rung by the wind (Ch10) is the best thing in the manuscript: earned, quiet and sleepy.
- **Ch7, "Two Ways of Knowing", is the heart of the story and works.** "You measured it, and I felt it. Between us, we know the whole shape." This is the moral shown through action. Benny, Barney and Buck repeat the cycle (feel, measure, shave a whisker) six times, which shows real collaboration rather than just claiming it.
- **Reba is genuinely funny and useful.** "It's the only responsible thing to do", the "mysteriously not quite full" basket, and "like a plate between a pie and a table" (her line prompts the cap idea). Her clay-packing gives her a real practical contribution.
- **Benny's deadpan is on-canon.** "It's the only bell on the only bridge over Pebble Run." / "That's what I said." Barney's "It's very dry. I'm not sure I like it" followed by jumping in is a strong button for Ch8.
- **Buck is handled correctly.** He keeps the saw and the chisel, refuses to tell them how ("I'm not going to tell you how to do it"), and supervises without solving. The Mountain Wisdom is canonical (#68).
- **Community widening in Ch8** brings in Maggie Mae, Brody, Charlie and the rabbit family, each with a distinct one-line beat.

### Weaknesses (with chapter references)

1. **The brotherly rivalry is undercooked and one-sided (brief fidelity).** The brief says Barney's unconventional suggestions *frustrate* Benny until they discover combining approaches. In practice Benny is irritated for one paragraph (Ch4, l.194–196) and fully converted by the end of the same chapter ("Barney had a good idea", l.220). After that, Ch5–8 contain no interpersonal friction at all. Barney is never frustrated by Benny's method, yet Buck's summary in Ch10 claims "they each thought the other one was getting in the way". The story has not earned that line. The emotional arc peaks in Ch4 and the second half is pure problem-solving.
2. **Benny's flaw reads as incompetence rather than rigidity.** Benny is canonically "the engineer", Buck's apprentice, with "knowledge of the old craft". Having him fail to imagine a mid-span support at all ("It had never once occurred to him", Ch3 l.165; "He had simply never thought of it", Ch4 l.206) makes him look like he does not know his trade. The brief asks for a *clever* footbridge with *unexpected* problems. A single unsupported plank is neither clever, nor are its problems unexpected. His flaw should be over-attachment to his plan, not ignorance.
3. **The moral is stated, then restated, then restated again.** In Ch8 the narrator says the bridge was "something in between, and something better than both" (l.402). In Ch9 Benny thinks "the parts he had not been right about, Barney had" (l.445), and then the brothers say it aloud ("I wasn't very good at listening at first", l.481). In Ch10 Buck gives a three-paragraph summary of the lesson (l.510–514) *before* the Mountain Wisdom. Bible §10 says "If a moral ever feels like a moral, it has been done wrong" and that Buck's wisdom should be the only telling. As written, the wisdom lands as the fourth telling, not as a goodnight kiss.
4. **The test-and-fail loop is repetitive.** Reba's test runs in Ch3, Ch5, Ch6 and Ch8 with near-identical staging ("She stepped onto the plank ... walked to the middle ..."). "Not one nut!" is used in Ch5 (l.269) and again as the triumphant line in Ch8 (l.422), so the Ch5 use spends the payoff early. Reba's nut-eating gag appears about seven times (l.49, 116, 155, 259, 314, 424, 500). Each is fine on its own, but together they make her one-note, and her canonical *curiosity* barely appears.
5. **Barney becomes the wise voice and slips out of his speech style.** Barney's canon speech style is "fast, bubbly ... half-finished sentences". In Ch6 and Ch7 he delivers the story's most polished aphorisms ("That's not the bridge being naughty. That's the bridge being honest."; "Between us, we know the whole shape."). The narrator even has to excuse it ("like a squirrel suddenly reciting poetry"). This is charming once, but twice makes Barney a second Buck.
6. **A dropped idea.** Barney's curved "like a rainbow" plank (Ch4, l.210) is actually a sound engineering idea (a cambered or arched span). It is raised and never mentioned again. The rope idea is paid off, so the curve should be either paid off or explicitly and kindly set aside.
7. **Stock and generic phrasing** that sounds templated:
   - "let out a long breath that he felt he had been holding since the day before" (Ch5, l.271). This is a widely recognised cliché and an AI tell.
   - "something prickly and uncomfortable rise up in his chest" (Ch4, l.194).
   - "drained out of him like water out of a cracked jug" (Ch6, l.286).
   - "the grin on his face was the biggest of the whole autumn" (Ch4, l.222).
   - "a slow, thoughtful smile, the kind he only smiled when something had gone in deep" (Ch10, l.520).
   - "There was a long, quiet moment" (l.202), followed by "There was a quiet moment" (l.294).
   - "laughed at the same time, which they very rarely did on purpose" (Ch7, l.381) is unclear in meaning and trips when read aloud.
8. **The ending staging is recycled from earlier Season One stories.** Brody's "little banjo" joining in "carefully, one at a time" behind Buck appears near-identically in 001, 003, 004 and 005. "Reba ... a honey cake crumb on her whiskers" echoes 004's "a honey cake crumb on her nose". The manuscript rules forbid recycled endings, and the founder asked for varied staging. The bell-in-the-wind image is original. Lean on it and change the surrounding furniture.
9. **The opening under-uses the house structure.** Bible §10 asks for an unhurried seasonal and time-of-day opening. Ch1 opens on a nicely voiced description of Pebble Run, but autumn is shown only through nuts. One or two sentences of autumn light, colour or smell before "Every year, somebody falls in" would bring the opening in line with house style at very little cost.
10. **Internal logic slips** (detailed under Lock Two) will be noticed by an adult reader: the stream's width, the "flat" stones that turn out to be round, sliding a stone pier under a plank, and the "corners" of a round stone.

### Required revisions (Lock One)

1. **Make the rivalry real and two-way.**
   - Ch4: Benny resists the pier idea more firmly. For example, he agrees only to "try it to show it won't work", or he insists the pier be built strictly to his numbers.
   - Ch5 or Ch7: add one beat where Barney grows impatient with the measuring ("Can't we just *put* it on?"). Ideally his impatient try fails too, so that each brother's method fails alone before they combine.
   - Move Benny's full, spoken acknowledgement ("Barney had a good idea") from Ch4 to Ch7, after the cap is solved by feel and measure together.
   - This makes Buck's Ch10 line "they each thought the other one was getting in the way" true.
2. **Give Benny a clever plan whose failure is a surprise, not an oversight.**
   - Make Benny's Ch2 design genuinely clever: for example, notched plank ends that lock onto the bank stones, a rail that lifts off for winter, or a deliberately chosen single span "so nothing stands in the water to wash away".
   - The bounce then becomes a consequence of a reasoned choice (avoiding a pier), not of never having heard of one.
   - Cut "It had never once occurred to him" and "He had simply never thought of it". Replace them with Benny's *reason* for rejecting a pier, which Barney's "We're beavers" then answers.
3. **Say the moral once, through Buck.**
   - Cut or compress the narrator's "something better than both" sentence in Ch8.
   - Keep Benny's quiet Ch9 reflection as images only (the bounce, the tilt, the paw on the block). Remove "And the parts he had not been right about, Barney had."
   - Keep at most one short exchange between the brothers on the bridge, and make it playful rather than confessional.
   - Cut Buck's Ch10 summary to one or two sentences of *observation* (what he saw them do, not what it means), then let #68 do the work.
4. **Vary the test beats and save "Not one nut!" for Ch8.**
   - Give the Ch5 success a different line from Reba.
   - Compress either the Ch5 or the Ch6 walk-through.
   - Cut two or three of the nut-eating jokes and replace one with a curiosity beat in character for Reba (peering under the plank, poking the cap's underside, asking "why").
5. **Restore Barney's voice.** Roughen his Ch6 and Ch7 insights into half-finished, bubbly speech (for example, "It's like ... it's like the bridge keeps *telling* us stuff. First it went 'I need a middle!' and now it's going 'I need a flat bit!'"). Let Benny or Reba put the idea into a neater phrase if one is needed. Cut the squirrel-poetry simile.
6. **Resolve the rainbow-curve idea.** Either use it (Buck later pencils a slight upward camber into the plank) or have Benny kindly explain why not this time. Do not leave it hanging.
7. **Replace the stock phrasings** listed in weakness 7. Remove the second "quiet moment". Rewrite l.381 to a clear image.
8. **Re-stage the porch close.** Find a fresh way for Brody to join the music, or give him a different porch beat this time. Give Reba a different sleepy image from 004's crumb. Keep the bell-in-the-wind close.
9. **Add one or two sentences of autumn and time-of-day grounding** at the top of Ch1.

**Internal continuity errors (fix):**

- **C1. Stream width (Ch1, l.5 and l.7; Ch2, l.106; Ch3).** Pebble Run is described as narrow enough that "a grown beaver could almost step across it". Yet it needs four stepping stones, and the plank is so long that two beavers can barely carry it and bouncy enough to throw a raccoon airborne. Pick one scale. Recommended: cut "almost step across" and make it "wider than it looks: four long hops on stepping stones".
- **C2. Flat stones versus the round top stone (Ch5, l.233 versus Ch6, l.280–292).** Barney's established gift is finding stones "flat on the bottom, flat on the top". In Ch6 the top stone turns out to be rounded and "There isn't a flatter one. You've brought up every stone in Pebble Run." Either make the Ch5 description admit that the top stone was the best they could find ("all but the very top one, which was the only stone tall enough"), or change the cause of the tilt.
- **C3. "Slid the pier ... underneath" (Ch5, l.255).** The pier is a stacked stone pillar built in the stream. It cannot be slid. It also implies the plank stayed in place over the build site. Rewrite as "the brothers lifted the plank, swung it over the new pier and lowered it down".
- **C4. Buck's summary is inaccurate (Ch10, l.510).** "They each thought the other one was getting in the way" is not true of Barney as written. Fix through Lock One revision 1 or reword.
- C5 (minor). "Measured it across, and front to back, and from corner to corner" (Ch7, l.341): a round stone has no corners. Use "and all the way round".
- C6 (minor). "Benny measured it. 'Level'" (Ch7, l.375): levelness is checked, not measured. Use "checked it with his little level" or "with a cup of water on top".
- C7 (minor). Reba "arrived at the bottom" (Ch1, l.21): the bottom of what? Use "at the bottom of the bank where Pebble Run meets Silver Creek".

### New details needing founder confirmation

1. **Pebble Run**: a new named stream, a tributary of Silver Creek joining "just below the beaver ponds", crossing the path between the beaver lodges and "the great nut groves".
2. **The Pebble Run footbridge**: a new permanent structure. It does not conflict with the canonical seven old wooden bridges, which cross Silver Creek, but it adds to the map, and later stories may reference it.
3. **"The great nut groves"**: a new named-ish location, possibly just the Hickory Woods. Confirm or rename to the Hickory Woods.
4. **Benny and Barney's father** (Ch4, l.204: "Benny had watched his own father pack stones..."). This is the first reference to the brothers' living parent. No parents are established in v1.0 or v1.1, and "the brothers' lodge" (Ch2, also in 003) implies the brothers live on their own. It needs a founder decision, or cut it and use "watched Great-Grandpappy Buck pack stones...".
5. **The family as "builders and menders since nearly the very beginning"** and **Buck "helped to build half the bridges in the valley"**: consistent with v1.0 §Buck's biography. Listed for completeness only.
6. **Mountain Wisdom #68 attributed to Buck's own great-grandpappy** (Ch10, l.518). This adds a lineage detail: the saying is handed down rather than Buck's own. It is compatible with v1.0, which says the origin tale came from "his grandpappy ... and so on back", but confirm that it is acceptable to attribute numbered wisdom to ancestors.
7. **The brass bell "off an old harness in the Great Barn", given by the miller** (Ch9, l.449). The miller has been used before. However, a *harness* implies some creature in the Hollow pulls loads in harness, which sits awkwardly in a world where all animals are equal neighbours. Recommend changing it to "off the old Great Barn door" or "from the miller's spare mill-bell".
8. **Barney's tactile "gift" for stones**: a new character trait that may recur. Confirm it as canon-compatible.
9. **Seed packet** (Ch2, l.74): a printed commercial seed packet is a faintly modern object. Consider "a curl of birch bark" for timelessness (Bible §15).

### Fact checks (Sherlock)

- **Plank deflection (Ch3).** Buck's statement is correct in direction: a simply supported beam deflects more as it gets longer ("The longer it is, the more it gives"). It actually understates the effect, because mid-span deflection grows with the cube of the span. "Strong and still aren't quite the same thing" correctly distinguishes strength from stiffness. "Twice as thick is twice as heavy" is correct. Strictly, doubling thickness would cut deflection roughly eightfold, so Benny's idea would work. Buck rejects it on weight grounds, which is valid, and the text never claims it would fail. **Accurate.**
- **Bounce launching a walker airborne (Ch3).** This is exaggerated for comedy. A seasoned hickory plank thick enough to carry beavers, over a stream a beaver can "almost step across", would barely flex. It is acceptable as cartoon physics only if the span is lengthened per C1.
- **"Seasoned hickory ... the strongest wood there is" (Ch3, l.161).** This is Benny's overstatement. Hickory is among the strongest and toughest North American hardwoods, notable for shock resistance, but not the strongest wood. It is fine as a character's claim. Optionally let Buck smile at it.
- **Pier (Ch5).** "A pier, which is the proper name for a pillar in the middle of a bridge" is correct. Intermediate supports are piers; end supports are abutments. **Accurate.**
- **Dry-stacked stone with clay packing in a current (Ch5).** Real beavers do move stones and pack mud into their structures, so this is plausible in-world. Bare clay in gaps would erode in running water over time. Optional: "clay and mud and little sticks, the way beavers always pack a dam".
- **Plank rocking on a domed stone (Ch6).** Plausible: a plank on a convex top can roll about its long axis. It is slightly contrived that it held through Reba's walk, return and skip and only tilted afterwards. Acceptable.
- **Cap (Ch6–7).** "Cap" (pier cap, bearing block or bolster) is a real term. Shaping a timber to sit on an irregular stone by trial, feel and paring is essentially *scribing*, which is a genuine craft technique. **Accurate and nicely observed.** Using seasoned oak for a wet location is reasonable (white oak is rot-resistant).
- **Rope ties to the trees (Ch8).** Tethering a plank footbridge's ends is a real practice, mainly to stop the plank being carried away in high water and to stop it shifting. It would not affect bounce, and the text claims only "so it can't wriggle". **Accurate.**
- **Hickory nuts "bobbing off downstream" (Ch1, Ch3).** This is questionable. Sound, shelled hickory nuts generally *sink*: floating is the standard sign of a bad, empty nut. Nuts still in their husks may float. Fix: "still in their green-brown husks, bobbing" or "rolling away along the stony bottom". This is low priority, but it is the kind of thing a sharp parent knows.
- **Raccoons' "cleverest paws" (Ch5).** Raccoon forepaws are highly sensitive and dexterous. **Accurate.**
- **Beavers building in moving water (Ch4).** Accurate natural history.
- **Fireflies omitted in autumn.** Correct. Fireflies are a summer phenomenon in the Appalachians, and omitting them is the right call for an autumn story.
- **Wisdom fit.** #68 (two ears, one mouth) is about listening before speaking. The story's moral is listening to *different ideas*. The fit is adequate, helped by Barney's "I wasn't very good at stopping talking", but not exact: Benny's fault was dismissing ideas, not talking too much. #76 ("There's more to be learned in listening than in ten years of talking") fits similarly. Neither fits better. #68 is acceptable. The founder may consider whether a saying about different ways of knowing exists in the 109 and would fit better.

## Lock Three: "Dr Noura" (safeguarding, emotional safety, bedtime suitability) and narration readiness

*This is an AI role-based assessment, not clinical or professional safeguarding advice.*

### Result: PASS WITH NOTES

There is nothing frightening, cruel or menacing. The stakes are lost nuts and pride. Adults handle the blades. Unkindness is minor and resolved. The ending is calm and reassuring. The notes concern water-play imitation and a little unsupervised dusk activity near water. Each can be fixed with a line or two.

### Safeguarding findings

- **Frightening content: none.** The bounce is comic and explicitly "Not dangerously". Barney and Reba stand "in the shadows" at dusk in Ch9 and speak suddenly from the bank, but "I knew it!" is cheerful and it is a gentle surprise, not a scare. OK.
- **Tools: well handled.** "The saw stays in my paws" (Ch2). Buck cuts and chisels; the children watch from a distance ("stood back"). Buck checks the knots. This matches the founder default (adults handle blades). One small point: Benny uses "a twist of wire" alone in Ch9. That is harmless, but "a twist of twine" would remove even that.
- **Water: needs two small fixes.**
  - N1. Ch5 sets a good rule: work only in the shallows, never past tummies, and only while Buck watches. But in the same chapter Barney is described as "dive down and come up with exactly the right stone" (l.233). That breaks the rule the story has just set and models diving in a stream. Change to "reach down with his paws and come up with...".
  - N2. Barney's in-stream play in Ch1 and his jump into Pebble Run in Ch8 ("Oh, good," said Barney, and jumped in) model jumping into cold, moving autumn water. Beavers are aquatic, which mitigates this in-world, and it is funny. Still, given the age range, soften Ch8 to "waded happily into the shallows" or similar, and keep the joke. Ch1 is acceptable as characterisation.
  - Ch9: Benny goes down to Pebble Run alone at dusk and kneels on the bridge, and Barney and Reba come looking for him. Nobody is in the water, the bridge is proven safe, and it is near home with the lodge lantern visible. Low risk. An optional line such as "with Buck's lantern in sight up the path" (already partly present) is enough.
- **Imitation: children building in water.** The story clearly frames building in water as supervised and limited to daylight ("nobody built in water after sundown"). That is good modelling and offsets the points above.
- **Exclusion and unkindness.** Benny's sharp tone in Ch4 ("a little more sharply than he meant to") is acknowledged by the narrative and resolved. Benny's flat "No. No." to Barney's ideas in Ch1 is affectionate and later softened by the bell. Charlie laughing at Barney's impression of Reba's bounce (Ch10) could read as laughter at Reba's expense. Add a half-line showing Reba laughing loudest, or joining in with her own "Oooh! Oooh!", to keep it with her, not at her (Bible §15). Calling Barney's wise remark like "a squirrel suddenly reciting poetry" (Ch6) is mild but faintly belittling, and Lock One already asks for it to be cut.
- **Trust in responsible adults: strong.** Buck supervises, sets clear and reasonable limits, praises effort, and lets the children solve the problem.
- **Moral shown versus preached.** It is shown well in Ch7, then over-told in Ch8–10 (see Lock One revision 3). From a child-development view, the repeated explicit statements reduce the child's own discovery of the lesson. Fixing Lock One revision 3 resolves this.
- **Bedtime wind-down.** Ch9–10 slow appropriately: honey-coloured light, a lantern, the creek, a slow tune, a faint bell, drowsiness. The final paragraph is calm and reassuring. However, Ch10 l.500 is a busy, energetic retelling (impressions, hiccups) placed in the wind-down chapter. Shorten that recap so the porch section slows sooner. Overall bedtime suitability is good.
- **Reassuring close:** yes. "The little bridge stood steady in the dark" is an excellent safety image to end on.

### Narration findings

- **Sentence clarity:** generally very good. The repetition with small changes ("It did not bounce. / She walked to the middle. / It did not sag.") is excellent for reading aloud.
- **Unclear when read aloud:**
  - "laughed at the same time, which they very rarely did on purpose" (Ch7, l.381).
  - "eating one nut for every two she put back in" (Ch1, l.49) is momentarily confusing aloud (put back where?). Use "eating one nut for every two she put in her basket".
  - "Reba had come back across Pebble Run, the long way, by the stepping stones, and only fallen in once" (Ch4, l.216) is grammatically fine, but the reader may stumble on it. Use "and had only fallen in once".
- **Dialogue attribution:**
  - Ch6–7 brothers' exchanges sometimes run three or four lines without tags (for example l.326–330, "And who's good at measuring?" / "And who's good at knowing how a stone feels?"). It is clear on the page, but a narrator voicing two similar young beavers would benefit from a tag on the second line ("said Benny, grinning").
  - Ch9 l.459, "said a voice from the bank": the voice is not identified until two sentences later. That is fine as a deliberate reveal, but make sure the narrator can voice it as Barney.
  - Ch1 l.35–47 (slide, swing, bell): the untagged "No." lines are clear and work well.
- **Visual-only jokes:**
  - Barney's trapdoor-with-password drawing is described in words and works aloud.
  - "With her pink bow over one eye" works.
  - "Did a small dance" in the centre of the bridge works.
  - Barney's impression in Ch10 is reported, not performed, so it works.
  - No issue found.
- **Rhythm:**
  - Strong onomatopoeia: "boing, boing, boing", "plip, plop, plip", "creeeeeak", "Ting", "Ting-ting". For "creeeeeak", give the narrator a performance note on length; the elongated spelling is fine.
  - The Ch8 community paragraph (l.426) is one long sentence chain. Consider breaking it into three or four sentences for breath.
- **Pronunciation:** nothing difficult.
