## Lock One

### "Piers Morgan", senior editor and literary critic

### Score: 8.0 / 10 — NOT YET PASSED (pass mark 8.5)

This is a well-built, warm, intelligent draft, and the best "how things work" story of the winter run. The opening idea is excellent: Benny wakes and notices the *absence* of a sound (l.7–19). "Exactly," said Benny (l.27) is a perfect first comic button. The investigation has a real scientific shape: diagnosis from the miller, a hypothesis, a control and a variable, a field observation from Maverick, an inference, and a historical precedent that confirms it. The founder's moral is enacted rather than preached. Barney's blanket, given out of pure kindness to a cotton reel, turns out to be the key, and Benny is the one who can read it. The opening simile ("like a lid on a pot", l.3) quietly plants the solution. The numbered board with "BENNY. BARNEY." (l.444–448) is a lovely, earned keepsake.

Five things hold the score below the pass mark:
- **The close is not fresh** (house rule 5). The last image is an object in the moonlight. The great wheel turning in the dark repeats the last image of 014, the story immediately before this one. The final "Next time…" / "No. But you can help" exchange is 009's close in a new coat.
- **The comedy does not escalate on stage** (house rule 4). Barney's ideas (hot water, drums) are only talked about at the kitchen table. Brody's "try them small" (l.149) promises a sequence of small comic tests, and it never comes. The tin-race model works once indoors (l.174–178) and then plays no further part.
- **The theme is spelled out before Buck speaks** (house rule 1): Brody at l.306–310 and the miller at l.335.
- **Most of the ensemble are a one-line montage** (house rule 3). Maverick's contribution is real and decisive. Reba, Rusty, Sadie Belle, Beau, Maggie Mae and Charlie each get a single sentence in Ch8. Rusty's beat is recycled almost word for word from 009.
- **Several safety slips** (house rule 7, detailed under Lock Three): Maggie Mae at the Mill stove, Brody bending sheet tin, Maverick perched on a smoking chimney, and unaccompanied walks along a frozen creek, one of them before it is light.

### Strengths

- **The opening chapter (l.3–39).** The absent sound, the brothers' scarves, the "frozen flower" wheel (l.31) and the miller saying "I stopped it" (l.39) give a crisp hook with no menace. The callback to Benny's ruler (l.5; 013) and his counting habit (l.17; 014) is good continuity.
- **The rope line (l.44–50, l.84).** "Find out all you like… From this side of the rope" is the brief's safeguarding flag turned into a line of character. It frames every later scene.
- **Character engines.** "Benny thought like a measuring stick… Barney thought like a firework" (l.91–93) is vivid and fair to both brothers. Barney looking at the little wheel "very small and very cold and very alone" (l.196) and then lending it his baby blanket is the emotional heart of the story, and it is completely in character.
- **Brody's two interventions** (l.149 "try them small"; l.306 "you both found it") use his steady, gentle canon. "I've come to sit on whoever's winning" (l.143) is a good laugh.
- **The reveal (Ch5–6).** Maverick's "Under the ice, no mush. Out in the open, mush." (l.229) followed by the two buckets is a satisfying, child-sized discovery. Benny's "his voice was getting faster, which it hardly ever did" (l.278) shows character through behaviour.
- **The miller remembering the race-boards only *after* the children reach the idea (l.327–335)** is exactly what house rule 2 allows, and it gives the story a nice sense of lost craft rediscovered.
- **The blanket-sharing scene (l.401–405)**: "Both of them had cold feet. Neither of them minded." This is the best two lines in the draft.
- **Bookended rhythm.** "Clack. Splash." and "eleven turns a minute" read well aloud and pay off at l.419–427.

### Weaknesses

1. **The close is recycled (house rule 5).**
   - l.470: "in the last thin moonlight, the little red wheel stood quite still…" The final image is an object in the moonlight. It does not literally "glow", but it is the banned beat in all but one word.
   - l.456: "the great wheel at the Old Mill, turning in the dark". 014 ended on "the great wooden wheel turning steadily in the black creek". Two consecutive stories now close on the same image.
   - l.460–468: Barney proposes something for "next time", Benny gently caps it, and Barney's idea is shrunk. This is 009's close ("Benny," said Barney. "Next winter…" / "No palaces." / "A very small heap is fine"). The same whispered "Benny?" / "What?" frame has already been used once in this story (l.397–399).
   - Benny's "No. But you can help." also undercuts the moral the story has just earned. It casts Barney's kind of thinking as the junior one, which pulls against Canon 4 ("difference is a distinction, never a deficit").
2. **The comedy is described, not staged, and does not escalate (house rule 4).** Chapters 3–4 have warm moments (the cocoa glare, the mother retreating, "the cat", the washing dropped), but the set-pieces are conversations. Brody's "try Barney's ideas small" sets up a sequence that never happens: hot water on a model paddle that refreezes, drums or a saucepan banged beside a frosted paddle with no effect, then the blanket. Staging two of those quickly, with each failure bigger and sillier than the last, gives Ch4 its missing escalation. It also gives the model mill a purpose and makes the blanket the third and winning try instead of a lucky aside.
3. **The moral is pre-stated (house rule 1).**
   - Brody, l.310: "Barney made the blanket… Benny worked out what it meant." This is the founder's moral in Brody's mouth.
   - The miller, l.335: "You worked that out between you."
   - Brody's short "Seems to me, you both found it" (l.306) can stay because it is in character. The explanation that follows it and the miller's summary should go, so that #30 arrives fresh.
4. **The ensemble is thin outside the leads (house rule 3).** Maverick passes: his scouting is the plot's hinge (l.225–229). Every other friend gets one montage sentence in Ch8 (l.366–370), mostly generic: "stamped the snow flat", "ran messages".
   - Rusty's "shortest way… without going near the creek" (l.368) is 009 l.309 ("It doesn't go anywhere near the creek") again.
   - Reba has nothing of her v1.1 food curiosity.
   - Sadie Belle and Beau are interchangeable.
   - At least three friends need a beat that changes the plot. Natural fits: Reba's nose for the pantry finds the store of old sacks or straw; Rusty scouts and finds the overgrown weir path, or where the barn ladder is; Charlie's careful stuffing becomes what makes the straw layer even; Maggie Mae organises the line. Any three, given one exchange of dialogue each, would satisfy the rule.
5. **Buck is a cameo who arrives only to speak (l.429–431).** House rule 2 is respected, but he appears from nowhere at the exact moment he is needed. A single earlier glimpse would make his arrival earned, for example Buck on the creek path in Ch8 with a mug, or the brothers passing his porch in Ch1.
6. **Logic seam: the model and the experiment are two different things.** The tin race (l.172–178) is never frozen. The test that matters is two static buckets (l.184–211), and the "control" is Benny's whole test while Barney's "test" is not a test at all. The weakness is not fatal, because it is clearly meant to be a happy accident. But the moral is "careful problem-solving", so the careful thinking should show (see weakness 2).
7. **Line-level points.**
   - l.72 "if this cold lasts a week, which the old ones say it might" against l.436 "The cold lasted nine more days, exactly as the old ones had said". These do not agree.
   - "as if it had been listening, it began to snow" (l.374) is a convenient coincidence, and it is also a physics problem (see Sherlock S-1).
   - "Morning, lads" (l.35) reads Yorkshire, not Appalachian. "Morning, boys" would suit the setting better.
   - "like two small steam engines" (l.29) brushes the §15 rule against technological references. "Like two small kettles" keeps the image.
   - "Barney's eyes went wide" (l.157) and "Barney's mouth fell open" (l.290) are stock phrases.

### "Sherlock", natural history and physics

The core mechanics are **accurate**:
- **Frazil ("ice mush").** Clear, still, bitterly cold nights cause rapid heat loss from open, turbulent water. The water supercools by a fraction of a degree and forms countless small ice needles and discs (l.68). Frazil sticks readily to supercooled trash racks and to itself, and blocks intakes within hours. All of this is correct, including "steaming" open water (sea smoke, l.66 and 229).
- **Ice cover suppresses frazil.** An ice sheet insulates the water beneath and stops the open-surface heat loss that drives supercooling, so frazil forms where water runs open below a weir or out from under an ice edge (l.225–229, 278). This is correct, and Maverick's observation is exactly what is seen on real rivers.
- **Covering races and flumes.** Covering races and intakes with boards, straw or brush, and letting snow insulate the top, is a historical anti-frazil practice. The race-boards (l.331) are plausible.
- **Wheel icing and imbalance.** Spray freezes unevenly on the paddles, which unbalances the wheel and strains the axle and gearing, so millers did stop wheels in hard frost (l.60). This is correct, and it answers the slate's "why the miller stops the wheel".
- **Bald eagle at open water in winter** is correct. So is a chickadee in February.

**Required fixes**
- **S-1. Snow "out of a clear sky" forming "a thick white blanket" (l.374–376) is not physical.** Clear-sky "diamond dust" is real but far too slight to bury a race in an evening. It also creates a scientific confound: if cloud had come over, the night would have been milder and less frazil could form, so the next morning's result (l.409) would prove nothing about the boards. l.387 says the stars were "sharp as needles", so the night was clear again. Fix: keep the sky clear and have the grown-ups shovel snow from the yard over the straw (this was also the historical practice), or say only that a light dusting fell. Do not have heavy snow fall.
- **S-2. Maverick sits "on top of the Mill chimney"** (l.368, echoed at l.429), but the chimney is "smoking" (l.31) and the Mill stove is in use (l.370). Move him to the roof ridge or the gable end. (This is also a safety point.)
- **S-3. The water flow at the grate is muddled.** At l.409 the water is already "running clear through the grate". At l.415 the miller pulls a lever and the water is "released, pouring through the grate". Make the lever open the sluice or penstock gate onto the wheel, with water going through the grate and down the bypass overnight. Similarly, l.66–68 has the race "still moving" while the grate is blocked; one clause saying the water spills over the side or down the bypass would fix it.
- **S-4. Paddle icing during the nine days is left unresolved.** The boards cure the grate problem, but in a "bitter" cold the running wheel's spray will still ice the paddles (the miller's first problem, l.60). Add one clause at l.438: the miller runs her only in the warmest hours and stops her at dusk, knocking the paddles clear each morning.
- **S-5. "Burrows under the snow stay warm" (l.288)** overstates the effect. Snow keeps the ground underneath near freezing, not warm. "Stay warmer than the air" is accurate.
- **S-6. The bucket experiment is plausible but generous.** An open metal bucket "frozen almost solid" overnight in a severe frost is credible for a small bucket. A covered bucket with only a fingernail-thin rim of ice is credible if it is well insulated and snow-covered on a single night, though the base still sits on frozen ground. Two small changes make it secure: say the buckets were filled from the pump (well water starts a little warmer), and give the covered bucket "a thin rim of ice round the edge" rather than almost none. Benny's own reading should also allow for the control growing its own ice "lid". A small bucket keeps freezing below a thin lid, which a pond, with its depth and its snow on top, does not. One line from Benny ("A thin lid isn't enough. It has to be a thick one, like the pond's, with snow on top") turns a possible objection into more careful thinking.
- **S-7. Wheel speed.** The Bible's wheel is "great" and turns "slow and patient". Eleven turns a minute is brisk for a large wheel (large breastshot and overshot wheels typically turn about 3–8). I recommend "six turns a minute" throughout (l.17, 56, 178, 427, 438). The motif is unchanged.
- **S-8 (minor).** Benny says it is "a very long race" (l.288), but thirty boards laid across the race would cover only about thirty board-widths. Either say the boards are wide ones laid lengthwise, or drop "very long".
- **S-9 (minor).** "As soon as it tumbles over the weir… it starts steaming", followed by mush "starting" just below (l.229), is acceptable as written. Real frazil generally appears a short way downstream once the water has supercooled, so "a little way below the weir" would be more exact.

## Lock Three

### "Dr Noura", safeguarding, bedtime suitability and narration readiness

**What works.**
- The slate's safeguarding flag is visibly honoured at the core. The rope line is established with a clear, non-frightening reason (l.48). No child crosses it at any point (l.364 says so explicitly).
- Adults do all the work near the water and on the wheel. The miller works on the wheel tethered (l.358), which models safe adult practice.
- Barney's hot-water idea is rejected by Barney himself on safety grounds (l.109).
- The father keeps the sharp tools (l.166) and the mother pours the water (l.174).
- There is no villain, no menace and no predation, and no humour at anyone's expense beyond ordinary, quickly resolved sibling squabbling.
- The ending is calm: meltwater, a steady sound, and bed.

**Required changes**
- **N-1. Unsupervised and pre-dawn outings.** The brothers walk the frozen creek path to the Mill alone (l.29), go there alone again (l.221, l.241), and are at the rope line "before it was even fully light" (l.407). House rule 7 requires supervised outings and no walking in the dark. Fix: their father walks them down at l.29 and l.407 (with a lantern at l.407), or they go with Brody and Brody's father. A single clause each time is enough. A path set back from the creek would also help.
- **N-2. A child at the stove.** "Maggie Mae and the miller's wife kept a big pot of hot cordial going on the Mill stove" (l.370). Adults handle flames. The miller's wife tends the pot (seated, see M-5), and Maggie Mae, as hostess, ladles from it once it is set on the table and carries the mugs round.
- **N-3. Sheet tin and cutting.** Brody "bent a long strip of tin" (l.172) before its sharp edges were filed. Benny's paddles were "cut" and slotted into a cotton reel (l.170) without saying who did the cutting. Fix: the father cuts and files the tin first and Brody bends the smooth strip into a channel; the father cuts the slots and Benny measures and glues.
- **N-4. Maverick on a smoking chimney** (l.368, 429). Move him to the roof ridge (see Sherlock S-2).
- **N-5. Sleeplessness is shown twice** (l.212 "lay awake"; l.385 "did not sleep much"), plus the anxious "What if it doesn't work?" (l.391). For a bedtime listener, keep one. l.385–405 is the better scene. Change l.212 to the brothers falling asleep while still arguing in whispers, or similar.
- **N-6 (recommended).** Barney is cold in bed because his blanket is outside (l.401). The sibling sharing is lovely and should stay. One later clause in which their mother finds them in the morning and fetches the spare quilt reassures young listeners that grown-ups notice.

**Bedtime suitability.** Good overall. The tensest moment (the miller on the wheel platform, "everybody… watched without making a sound", l.358) is brief and resolved by the tether. "If you're not careful you crack the axle" (l.60) is not frightening.

**Narration readiness.**
- The sound effects ("Clack. Splash.") read aloud beautifully.
- The capitals (l.159, 292, 296, 446) are clear cues for the reader.
- The miller's two explanations (l.60, l.68) are long single speeches. Split each with a beat (for example Benny writing, or the miller pointing) so a narrator can breathe and a child can take it in.
- The Ch8 lists (l.358, l.368) are dense. Break l.368 into one short paragraph per friend, which also helps L1-4.
- "Race" is explained on first use (l.54), which is good. Repeat "the millrace" once in Ch8 so that listeners who remember 012's sledge races are not confused.
- "lads" (l.35) → "boys".
- No pronunciation traps; "frazil" is wisely not used.
