## Lock One — "Piers Morgan", senior editor

### Score: 8.1 / 10 (pass mark 8.5): NOT PASSED

This is a clean, warm, often genuinely funny manuscript with three or four set-pieces a child will ask for again. It is let down by structural shortcuts at exactly the points where the brief's moral is meant to be earned. The story's best scene, Maggie Mae choosing to let go of the timing, happens off-stage. The heroine's defining quality in the brief, her Southern hospitality, is barely seen at the party itself. And the moral is spelled out three times over.

### Strengths

- **Set-pieces with real comic invention.** The cake pushing the oven door open "like a large golden sponge trying to see what was going on outside", with the apple rose "like a hat, very high up, looking surprised" (Ch3, l.133), is the best image in the book. "Free o'clock" (Ch2, l.86) is a perfect child-level joke that also works in audio. The ladybird crossing out MAGNIFICENT (l.194) is a lovely touch.
- **The staggered-arrival party** (Ch8, l.348–366): "the party never really started and never really stopped". This is an original structural idea that turns the plot's mishap into the shape of the celebration.
- **Charlie's voice** is distinct and lovable: "I can smell a list!", "I can do exactly", "More is more". Her mishaps come from child logic (the flour "looked lonely"), not stupidity. Using cheek-pouches for the invitations is a smart inheritance of Pip's canonical pouch trouble (Bible v1.0 Pip profile).
- **Ensemble cameos are in character.** Benny's measuring stick and his verdict "It means you can choose the biggest" (l.376) is the best ensemble beat. Maverick's line "exactly on time for a fashionable eagle" is brash and comical, but kind. Rusty takes a shortcut and apologises to the roses. Reba reads "cake" and stops there. Beau turns his card upside down. Sadie Belle's quiet crush stays unnoticed.
- **The emotional low point is honest.** Charlie folds the apron and puts it on the chair (l.237), and the "beautiful, perfect, empty cottage" (Ch6) is quietly affecting for adults.
- **The mended teacup with its visible line** (Ch9) is a strong emblem: it puts the moral into an object without anyone needing to say it.
- **The closing image** pays off Chapter One's list and is a satisfying circular ending: "HAVE FRENDS", with a neat tick beside it.

### Weaknesses (with chapter references)

1. **The cliffhanger is never paid off (Ch7→Ch8).** Ch7 ends: "I think I might need to tell you something about the invitations." Ch8 then jumps straight to "the strangest party… and the best." We never see the confession or Maggie Mae's reaction. That reaction is the real test of the moral: a perfectionist learns her guests may arrive at any time between three and sunset, and chooses to laugh. It is the climax of her arc, and it is skipped. It is also never explained how Charlie now knows about the smudges, because Ch2 says she "did not notice at the time".
2. **The brief's central quality is under-dramatised.** The brief asks the story to "celebrate Maggie Mae's sophisticated Southern hospitality". We see her planning, but at the party (Ch8) she does no hosting at all. Brody carries the chairs, guests arrive in a list, and Maggie Mae dances. The stagger device begs for her to greet each late guest as though they were exactly on time. That would show her hospitality and her changed heart in a single action.
3. **The moral is preached, not shown.** It is stated in four places:
   - by the narrator in Ch6, l.278–280: "Not one of them said anything about Charlie… She had forgotten to plan who it was for";
   - by Maggie Mae in Ch7, l.315: "a party isn't cups and cakes and garlands. It's friends";
   - in Buck's four-sentence preamble in Ch10, l.448–452;
   - on the list itself: "HAVE FRENDS".

   The Bible says plainly that "if a moral ever feels like a moral, it has been done wrong" and that Buck's wisdom is the only telling allowed. As written, Buck is repeating what two characters have already said.
4. **The Mountain Wisdom is a slight mismatch.** #85 ("Nobody remembers what you owned…") is canon and quoted correctly. But this story is not about possessions, it is about perfection. Its emblem is also a treasured *owned* object, the grandmother's cup, so the saying half-contradicts the image the story has just built. Buck's lead-in also talks about tables, not ownership. Better fits are #34 ("The best-built bridge is the one that's been mended a hundred times with love"), which chimes with the cup, or #84 ("A shared trouble is half a trouble. A shared joy is two joys."). If #85 stays, its lead-in should be cut back so the saying lands alone.
5. **POV and continuity slips in Ch5.**
   - l.229 gives Maggie Mae's thoughts as "the party was at three, or possibly eight, or free o'clock". She cannot know this: she learns about the smudges only at the end of Ch7.
   - l.213 says "nobody had told her yet what time they were coming". She set the time herself and is unaware of the smudges, so this line is confused.
6. **The Ch5 mishaps are rushed and mechanical.** Three "She helped by…" disasters arrive in quick succession: open windows blow the name-cards out, the watering can rolls into the roses, the teacup tower falls. The first two are physically contrived and have no motive. Chapters 2–4 each gave Charlie a reason rooted in child logic, and these do not. The emotional turn of the book rests on this chapter, so it needs at least one fully staged, motivated mishap rather than a list.
7. **Recycled series beats.**
   - Charlie runs off hurt and sits alone until a friend quietly sits beside her: this repeats S01-001 Ch6 (Charlie under the elderberry bush, Rusty sits down beside her). Two Charlie-runs-off-hurt arcs in the first three stories is a pattern listeners will notice.
   - The closing sleep catalogue repeats S01-002's ending almost image for image: "Reba was asleep with a crumb on her whiskers" (002: "a honey cake crumb on her whiskers") and Benny and Barney asleep against each other.
   - Maggie Mae's introductory description (l.13) is nearly word for word the S01-001 one ("roses and warm honey and a little bit of lavender").
   - The canon pack forbids recycled endings.
8. **The opening skips the house structure's "Sunrise on the Hollow".** "Maggie Mae had a list." is a strong first line, but the listener never arrives in the Hollow: there is no creek, woodsmoke or autumn morning before the plot starts (Bible §10, step 1).
9. **The cast list makes a canon claim the story contradicts.** l.15 says "She had never given a party before. Not a real one." Canon (v1.1) calls Maggie Mae a "generous hostess". Making this her first party ever contradicts that. "She had never given a party for the whole Hollow before" keeps the stakes and keeps canon.
10. **Absolution is over-corrected (Ch7, l.311).** "You didn't do it wrong. I did." Charlie *did* secretly triple the rising powder against instructions. Maggie Mae's apology is excellent, but the blanket absolution flattens the lesson and is undercut two lines later ("I put too much rising powder in." / "You did."). Both should own their part.
11. **Minor phrasing that reads as generic.**
    - "The words were out before she could catch them. They hung in the air…" (l.233)
    - "felt hollow, like a teacup with nothing in it" (l.260)
    - "Charlie's whole face lit up" (l.407)
    - "Her blue eyes were shining" (l.333)
    - "sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a friend is to be there and not say anything at all" (l.295: narrator telling, and a second moral)
    - "It turned out to be the strangest party that Hickory Hollow had ever seen, and the best" (l.348)

    "Sugar" appears 8 times, and Maggie Mae's Southern voice rests almost entirely on it and on "Oh, my stars". "Magnificent" appears 12 times, which is deliberate as a motif but at the edge of tiring for the adult reader.
12. **The ensemble does not contribute to the resolution.** This is acceptable for a two-hander brief, but the Bible's "everyone has a place" beat is thin. Apart from Brody carrying chairs, the friends are a comic parade rather than people whose gifts matter. One small contribution would help, for example Benny solving the bench or Rusty fetching the stray name-cards.

### Required revisions

1. **Ch7 end / Ch8 opening: stage the confession and the choice.** Add a short beat of about 120–200 words:
   - Charlie explains the cheek-pouch smudges, and how she realised (for example, she remembers the cards came out damp and soft).
   - Maggie Mae has a visible first impulse to panic, then chooses to laugh.
   - She decides that whoever comes, whenever they come, will be welcome.

   This is the climax of her arc and must be on the page.
2. **Ch8: show the hospitality.** Give Maggie Mae a gracious, distinctly Southern welcome for the staggered arrivals, for example keeping the kettle on all afternoon and greeting every guest as "right on time", with a fresh cup and a seat for each. Use 2–3 short welcomes, not one per guest. Replace at least half of the "sugar"s with other warm Southern-belle turns ("Well, I declare", "bless your heart" used kindly, "come on in and sit a spell").
3. **Cut the moral back to one telling, Buck's.**
   - Ch6: delete or soften l.280 ("She had planned a perfect party. She had forgotten to plan who it was for.") and the second half of l.278.
   - Ch7: trim Maggie Mae's speech at l.315 to the apology itself, without the "a party isn't cups… It's friends" thesis.
   - Ch7: cut the narrator's maxim at l.295.
   - Ch10: reduce Buck's preamble (l.448–452) to one or two lines.
   - Choose the wisdom with the founder: either #34 (ties to the mended cup) or #85 with no table preamble.
4. **Ch5: fix the POV slips.**
   - l.229: remove "or possibly eight, or free o'clock" from Maggie Mae's thoughts, or move it into an explicit narrator aside to the listener.
   - l.213: rewrite or delete "nobody had told her yet what time they were coming".
5. **Ch5: rebuild the mishap sequence.** Fully stage one mishap with a child-logic motive. The teacup tower "to save time" is the right candidate, because it carries the emotional turn. Compress or cut the window and watering-can mishaps, and give whatever remains a reason.
6. **Remove recycled series beats.**
   - Ch10 sleep catalogue: drop Reba's crumb on her whiskers, and vary the Benny/Barney image.
   - Ch1, l.13: rephrase Maggie Mae's introduction so it does not copy S01-001.
   - Ch7: stage Charlie's refuge differently from S01-001, for example finding her actively *doing* something (trying to make Maggie Mae a replacement rose, or a second card). This also deepens Charlie.
7. **Ch1: add a short Hollow-grounding passage** of about 80–150 words (autumn morning over Fern Meadow, woodsmoke, birdsong) around the first line, per Bible §10 step 1.
8. **Ch1, l.15: align with canon.** Change "never given a party before" to "never given a party for the whole Hollow before" (or similar).
9. **Ch7, l.311: rebalance the apology.** Use something like "You made a few muddles, sugar. I made the biggest one." Keep "I spoke to you sharply, and you didn't deserve it".
10. **Polish the generic phrasing listed under weakness 11** (at least the l.233, l.295 and l.348 lines).

### Sherlock — fact checks

- **Ladybirds overwintering in leaf litter: broadly accurate, with two corrections.**
  - Many native ladybirds (Coccinellidae) overwinter in aggregations in leaf litter, grass tussocks, under bark and in crevices. Aggregations of hundreds are realistic.
  - Warmth does rouse them: overwintering ladybirds become active when brought into a heated room, and autumn ladybirds coming indoors is a well-known Appalachian phenomenon.
  - Moving them to a dry, sheltered spot outdoors (l.217) is exactly right, and "some of them came back" (they seek warmth) is realistic.
  - Correction 1: "deep, *damp* leaf piles" (l.190). Overwintering ladybirds favour sheltered, *dry-ish* spots, because damp encourages fatal fungal infection. Suggest "deep, snug leaf piles".
  - Correction 2: "opened its red wings" (l.180). The red parts are the hard wing-cases (elytra), and the flying wings unfold from beneath. Suggest "lifted its red wing-cases and buzzed".
  - Ladybirds stirring again on a warm, golden autumn afternoon (Ch8) is accurate.
- **Baking powder overuse: partly accurate, with one correction.**
  - Four times the leavening does make a cake rise over-fast, overflow its tin, then collapse and sink: correct in kind. Pushing the oven door open and "going on rising for a little while on the table" are comic exaggerations, which are acceptable as hyperbole.
  - Correction: too much baking powder usually gives a coarse, crumbly texture and a noticeably bitter, soapy or metallic taste, not "very nice, if rather chewy" (l.151). Suggest "a little odd-tasting and very bouncy". Barney can still love them, and the joke and Benny's later verdict survive.
- **Chipmunk cheek-pouches:** these are internal, skin-lined pouches that chipmunks keep fairly dry (so seeds store well). Cards coming out "a little damp" with running ink is a mild stretch, but it is acceptable comic licence. No action required. Optionally make the main cause creasing and squashing rather than damp.
- **Sassafras leaves "shaped like little mittens"** (l.170): accurate, and native to the region. Red maple, yellow hickory and brown oak autumn colours are accurate.
- **Robin on the window box, a late moth at the lantern on a mild autumn evening:** both plausible.
- **Eagle "tucked his head under his wing"** (l.456): folk phrasing. Birds actually turn the head back and tuck the bill into the back feathers. This is acceptable in a picture-book register.
- **Bobcat fluffing fur to keep warm:** accurate (piloerection).

## Lock Three — "Dr Noura" (safeguarding, emotional safety, bedtime) and narration readiness

*This is an AI role-based assessment, not clinical advice.*

### Result: PASS WITH NOTES

Nothing in the story is frightening, and there is no menace, violence or predation. The hurt is handled with care and fully repaired well before the close. The wind-down is good. The notes below are improvements, not blockers.

### Safeguarding findings

1. **The sharp words and sending Charlie home (Ch5, l.231): appropriate and well repaired.**
   - Maggie Mae's outburst is stressed, not cruel ("Please. Just stop… Please just go home"). It is clearly framed as out of character ("higher and sharper than she had ever heard it"), and the narrator shows immediate regret ("The words were out before she could catch them").
   - Charlie's response is quiet and dignified (she folds the apron). That is poignant for 4–8s, but not distressing.
   - The repair in Ch7 is a model one: Maggie Mae seeks Charlie out herself, names the behaviour ("I spoke to you sharply"), says "you didn't deserve it", offers physical comfort, re-invites Charlie and expresses need ("I need you").
   - The distance between hurt and repair is about one and a half chapters. That is acceptable, because Ch6 starts the turn towards repair.
   - Note: Charlie only blames herself ("I kept doing it wrong") and never says how she *felt*. One line letting Charlie name the feeling ("It made my tummy go small and sad") would model emotional literacy for young listeners.
   - Note: "You didn't do it wrong. I did." should be rebalanced (L1 revision 9). Children benefit from seeing both parties own their part, rather than learning that secretly ignoring an instruction carried no fault.
2. **Imitation risks.**
   - *Oven:* Maggie Mae (the older figure) puts the tin in and takes it out with a cloth. Charlie only pours ingredients. Low risk. Optional: "an oven cloth" rather than "a cloth".
   - *Secretly adding extra ingredients:* a consequence is shown and owned. Low risk.
   - *Breaking crockery:* there are no shards, only a detached handle, and no child picks up broken pieces. Carrying a tower of cups is clearly shown as a mistake. Low risk.
   - *Glue:* Maggie Mae does the mending. Low risk.
   - *Lanterns and flames:* Buck (the adult) lights them with a taper, which complies with the founder safeguarding default. However, the lanterns hang "in the rose arch" above a garden with leaf garlands and leaf piles. Adding "glass lanterns" makes it clear the flames are enclosed. Low risk.
3. **A child alone by water (Ch7).**
   - A distressed small child goes off alone for several hours, sits on a willow root "where the water ran slow and green", and nobody knows where she is.
   - Mitigations: Sadie Belle has been with her for an hour, the water is slow, and there is no peril.
   - Founder default: "children not left in danger". Recommend a single phrase placing her "on the dry bank, well back from the water", or relocating the refuge (see L1 revision 6).
4. **Trust in responsible adults.** Buck appears only at the close, appropriately: he must not solve the children's problem. No adult knows Charlie is missing, but the absence is short and resolved by Maggie Mae, the big-sister figure. Acceptable.
5. **A reassuring close.** The young ones fall asleep outdoors in a garden on an autumn night (Ch10), with no indication of warmth or of getting home. Recommend one line, for example Maggie Mae fetching quilts, or Buck saying he will see everyone safe home when the tune is done. This gives the "safe" note the Bible's final test requires.
6. **The moral is shown, not preached.** It is shown well through the teacup and the stagger party, but over-told in words (see L1 weakness 3). From a child's perspective repetition does no harm. From the franchise's perspective it fails "never state it as instruction". This is handled by L1 revision 3.
7. **Bedtime pacing.** Ch8 is the high-energy peak (dancing, a bench falls over), which is right for chapter 8 of 10. Ch9 and Ch10 slow convincingly: shorter sentences, a slowing tune, stars, a moth, sleepers.
   - The final reveal ("HAVE FRENDS", the tick) is a gentle smile, not a jolt.
   - "Goodnight, Hickory Hollow." is a good closing cadence.
   - Optional: end on one more slow sensory line after the tick, before the goodnight.

### Narration findings

1. **The misspellings are visual-only jokes.** "HOLE WIDE WORLD" (l.268) sounds identical to "whole" when read aloud. "ASISTANT" (l.268) and "HAVE FRENDS" (l.464) are also inaudible. The *sentiment* survives in audio but the *humour* is lost. Recommendations:
   - Add one narrator cue at the first card, for example: "spelled the Charlie way, which mostly meant the way it sounded".
   - The final card can then stand. Its emotional payoff (the tick) works without the spelling.
2. **Read-aloud clarity is generally excellent.**
   - Short declaratives, clear paragraphing, and onomatopoeia that is fun to perform ("Mmf mmf mmf!").
   - Capitals for emphasis ("A PARTY?", "SO carefully") guide the reader well.
   - The long breathless sentence at l.229 is effective read aloud, once its POV error is fixed.
   - l.366 and l.391 are long but parse naturally.
3. **Dialogue attribution is clear throughout.** The unattributed lines (l.155 "I can help!", l.325 "And the ladybirds?", l.442/444) alternate between only two speakers, so they are unambiguous. Maverick's embedded dialogue (l.354) is clear.
4. **"Free o'clock" works better in audio than in print.** Keep it.
5. **Chapter titles.** "Everybody Comes at Once, and Not at All" reads well aloud. "Under the Willow" may be heard as the retired character's name (see Lock Two).
6. **Rhythm.** The "She helped by…" anaphora in Ch5 reads aloud well rhythmically, even though it is structurally rushed (see L1). The Ch10 tune description ("It went up a little, and came down a little, and went round again") is ideal lullaby prose.
7. **Repetition fatigue for the adult reader.** There are 8 "sugar"s and 12 "magnificent"s. Trimming "sugar" (L1 revision 2) also helps narration.
